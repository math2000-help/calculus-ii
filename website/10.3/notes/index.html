10.3 Infinite Series¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

A ball is dropped from two metres and bounces again and again, each rebound reaching \(\frac35\) of the previous height. It bounces infinitely often, yet it comes to rest, so the total distance it travels ought to be finite. How far does it go? In Section 10.1 an infinite sum was defined as the limit of its partial sums. That tells us what a series means, but not how to evaluate one: for most series nobody can find a formula for \(S_n\). This section treats the two families where it can be done, geometric series and telescoping series, and then proves the rules that let us add, scale and shift convergent series. It ends with the fact on which the next sections rest: for a series of nonnegative terms, convergence is the same as boundedness of the partial sums.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • write the \(n\)th partial sum of a series with any starting index and say what convergence means;

  • recognise a geometric series, find its first term and ratio, decide whether it converges, find its value and estimate the error of a partial sum;

  • write a repeating decimal as a geometric series and as a fraction;

  • evaluate a telescoping series (using partial fractions or logarithm rules) and recognise when it diverges;

  • use the properties of convergent series, including changing the starting index, and say when they do not apply;

  • show that a series of nonnegative terms converges by bounding its partial sums;

  • prove the main theorems of this section.

Series and convergence¶

Definition 10.3.1: Convergence of a series

Let \(\{a_k\}_{k=M}^\infty\) be a sequence, where \(M\) is an integer. The \(n\)th partial sum of the series \(\sum_{k=M}^\infty a_k\) is the sum of its first \(n\) terms,

\[S_n=a_M+a_{M+1}+\cdots+a_{M+n-1}=\sum_{k=M}^{M+n-1}a_k .\]

If \(S_n\to S\) the series converges, and \(S\) is its sum or value: \(\sum_{k=M}^\infty a_k=S\). If \(\{S_n\}\) diverges (including the cases \(S_n\to\infty\) and \(S_n\to-\infty\)), the series diverges and has no value.

For \(M=1\) this is Definition 10.1.13. The only new point is the starting index. For \(\sum_{k=3}^\infty a_k\) the \(n\)th partial sum is \(a_3+a_4+\cdots+a_{n+2}\), the sum of the first \(n\) terms, which is not the same as the sum up to \(a_n\). When the starting index does not matter we write simply \(\sum a_k\).

To evaluate a series we find a formula for \(S_n\) and take its limit. Only a few families allow this; we meet the two most important ones now.

Geometric series¶

Definition 10.3.2: Geometric series

A geometric series is a series in which every term is the same constant multiple of the term before it. If the first term is \(t\ne0\) and the ratio is \(r\), the series is \(t+tr+tr^2+tr^3+\cdots\).

The series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty ar^k\) has first term \(a\) and ratio \(r\). The series \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty3\left(-\frac12\right)^k\) has ratio \(-\frac12\) but first term \(3\left(-\frac12\right)^2=\frac34\), not \(3\). Always compute the first term from the actual starting index. The \(n\)th partial sum of a geometric series is a finite geometric sum (Theorem 10.1.15), and that gives a complete answer.

Theorem 10.3.3: Geometric series

Let \(t\ne0\) and let \(\sum\) be the geometric series with first term \(t\) and ratio \(r\). Its partial sums are

\[S_n=t\,\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\quad(r\ne1),\qquad S_n=nt\quad(r=1).\]

(a) If \(|r|<1\), the series converges and its value is \(\dfrac{t}{1-r}\) (first term over one minus the ratio).

(b) If \(|r|\ge1\), the series diverges.

(c) If \(|r|<1\) and \(S\) is the value, the error in using \(S_n\) is \(S-S_n=\dfrac{t\,r^n}{1-r}\).

Proof. The first \(n\) terms are \(t,tr,\dots,tr^{n-1}\), so \(S_n=t(1+r+\cdots+r^{n-1})\). If \(r=1\) this is \(nt\); if \(r\ne1\) it equals \(t\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\) by the finite geometric sum.

(a) If \(|r|<1\) then \(r^n\to0\) (Theorem 10.2.11), so by the limit laws \(S_n=\frac{t}{1-r}\,(1-r^n)\to\frac t{1-r}\).

(b) Let \(r=1\). Then \(|S_n|=n|t|\) is unbounded, but a convergent sequence is bounded (Theorem B.2), so the series diverges. Now let \(|r|\ge1\) and \(r\ne1\), and suppose \(S_n\to S\). Solving \(S_n=t\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\) for \(r^n\) gives \(r^n=1-\frac{1-r}tS_n\), and by the limit laws the right side converges (to \(1-\frac{1-r}tS\)). So \(\{r^n\}\) would converge. But for \(r>1\) we have \(r^n\to\infty\), and for \(r\le-1\) the sequence \(\{r^n\}\) diverges (Theorem 10.2.11). This contradiction shows that \(\{S_n\}\) diverges.

(c) \(S-S_n=\dfrac t{1-r}-\dfrac{t(1-r^n)}{1-r}=\dfrac{t\,r^n}{1-r}\). ◻ ∎

Example 10.3.4: Geometric series: value or divergence

Evaluate each series or say that it diverges. (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty6\left(\frac23\right)^k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty5\left(-\frac34\right)^k\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3^{k+1}}{5^k}\); (d) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty1.07^k\).

Solution. In each case find the first term \(t\) and the ratio \(r\).

(a) The first term (at \(k=0\)) is \(t=6\) and \(r=\frac23\), with \(|r|<1\). The value is \(\dfrac{6}{1-2/3}=18\).

(b) The series starts at \(k=2\), so \(t=5\left(-\frac34\right)^2=\frac{45}{16}\) (not \(5\)), and \(r=-\frac34\). The value is \(\dfrac{45/16}{1+3/4}=\dfrac{45}{28}\).

(c) Rewrite \(\dfrac{3^{k+1}}{5^k}=3\left(\dfrac35\right)^k\). At \(k=1\) the first term is \(t=\frac95\), and \(r=\frac35\). The value is \(\dfrac{9/5}{1-3/5}=\dfrac92\).

(d) Here \(r=1.07>1\), so the series diverges. Its partial sums \(S_n=\dfrac{1.07^n-1}{0.07}\) grow without bound, like a bank balance with \(7\%\) compound interest.

A repeating decimal is a geometric series in disguise.

Example 10.3.5: A repeating decimal as a fraction

Write \(0.1\overline{27}=0.1272727\ldots\) as a fraction.

Solution. Split off the part that does not repeat: \(0.1272727\ldots=0.1+0.0272727\ldots\) The repeating part is the series \(0.027+0.00027+0.0000027+\cdots\). Each term is \(\frac1{100}\) of the one before, so it is geometric with \(t=0.027\) and \(r=0.01\):

\[0.027+0.00027+\cdots=\frac{0.027}{1-0.01}=\frac{27}{990}=\frac3{110}.\]

Hence \(0.1\overline{27}=\dfrac1{10}+\dfrac3{110}=\dfrac{11+3}{110}=\dfrac7{55}\). Check by long division: \(\frac7{55}=0.127272\ldots\)

Example 10.3.6: A bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from a height of \(2\) m. Each time it hits the floor it rebounds to \(\frac35\) of the height from which it fell. What total distance does it travel?

Solution. The ball first falls \(2\) m. After that, each bounce consists of a rise and an equal fall. The first rebound height is \(2\cdot\frac35=\frac65\) m, so bounce \(1\) adds \(\frac{12}5\) m; the second rebound height is \(2\left(\frac35\right)^2\), so bounce \(2\) adds \(4\left(\frac35\right)^2\) m; in general bounce \(j\) adds \(4\left(\frac35\right)^j\) m. The total distance is

\[2+\sum_{j=1}^\infty4\left(\frac35\right)^j .\]

The series is geometric with first term \(\frac{12}5\) and ratio \(\frac35\), so its value is

\[\sum_{j=1}^\infty4\left(\frac35\right)^j=\frac{12/5}{1-3/5}=6 ,\]

and the ball travels \(2+6=8\) m in all. As a check, after three bounces the distance is \(2+2.4+1.44+0.864=6.704\) m, and the partial-sum formula \(2+6\left(1-0.6^3\right)\) gives the same number.

Example 10.3.7: How many terms are enough?

How many terms of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty3(0.7)^k\) must be added so that the partial sum is within \(10^{-4}\) of the value?

Solution. The value is \(S=\dfrac{3}{1-0.7}=10\). By Theorem 10.3.3(c) the error after \(n\) terms is \(S-S_n=\dfrac{3(0.7)^n}{0.3}=10(0.7)^n\). We need \(10(0.7)^n<10^{-4}\), that is, \(0.7^n<10^{-5}\), so \(n>\dfrac{5\ln10}{\ln(1/0.7)}\approx32.3\). The smallest such integer is \(n=33\). Check: the error is \(10(0.7)^{32}\approx1.1\times10^{-4}\) (too big) and \(10(0.7)^{33}\approx7.7\times10^{-5}\) (small enough).

Knowledge Check 10.3.1: Geometric series

Find the first term and the ratio, decide whether the series converges, and find its value if it does: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty2\left(\frac15\right)^k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^\infty\left(-\frac12\right)^k\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{4^k}{3^{k+1}}\).

Pause and think 10.3.1: Why the condition \(|r|<1\)?

A student writes: “Let \(S=1+2+4+8+\cdots\). Then \(S=1+2(1+2+4+\cdots)=1+2S\), so \(S=-1\).” Find the mistake by looking at the partial sums. Then explain why the same manipulation \(S=1+rS\), which gives \(S=\frac1{1-r}\), is legitimate when \(|r|<1\).

Answer and discussion

The partial sums are \(S_n=1+2+\cdots+2^{n-1}=2^n-1\), which tend to \(\infty\): the series diverges and there is no number \(S\). The manipulation assumed that \(S\) is a number and then solved for it; the result \(-1\) is nonsense (a sum of positive terms cannot be negative). For \(|r|<1\) the series does converge by Theorem 10.3.3, so \(S\) is a number, the rearrangement \(S=1+rS\) is a valid use of properties (1) and (5), and solving gives \(S=\frac1{1-r}\), in agreement with the theorem.

Telescoping series¶

In a telescoping series each term is a difference, and when the partial sum is written out almost everything cancels, like the sections of a collapsing telescope.

Theorem 10.3.8: Telescoping series

Let \(\{b_k\}_{k=M}^\infty\) be a sequence. For every \(j\ge1\),

\[\sum_{k=M}^{M+j-1}\left(b_k-b_{k+1}\right)=b_M-b_{M+j}.\]

Consequently \(\sum_{k=M}^\infty(b_k-b_{k+1})\) converges if and only if \(\{b_k\}\) converges, and then its value is \(b_M-\lim_{k\to\infty}b_k\). More generally, if \(m\ge1\) is an integer and \(b_k\to L\), then

\[\sum_{k=M}^\infty\left(b_k-b_{k+m}\right)=\left(b_M+b_{M+1}+\cdots+b_{M+m-1}\right)-mL .\]

Proof. The formula for the finite sum is proved by induction on \(j\). For \(j=1\) it says \(b_M-b_{M+1}=b_M-b_{M+1}\). If it holds for \(j\), add the next term \(b_{M+j}-b_{M+j+1}\): the sum of \(j+1\) terms is \(b_M-b_{M+j}+b_{M+j}-b_{M+j+1}=b_M-b_{M+j+1}\).

Hence the partial sum is \(S_j=b_M-b_{M+j}\). If \(b_k\to L\), then \(b_{M+j}\to L\) (Theorem B.3) and \(S_j\to b_M-L\). Conversely, if \(S_j\to S\) then \(b_{M+j}=b_M-S_j\to b_M-S\), and so \(b_k\) converges, again by Theorem B.3. This proves the first two statements.

For the last statement, each difference splits into \(m\) one-step differences,

\[b_k-b_{k+m}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(b_{k+i}-b_{k+i+1}\right).\]

Add this over \(k=M,\dots,M+j-1\) and exchange the two finite sums. The inner sum over \(k\) telescopes by the finite formula (for the sequence starting at \(M+i\)) to \(b_{M+i}-b_{M+i+j}\), so

\[S_j=\left(b_M+\cdots+b_{M+m-1}\right)-\left(b_{M+j}+b_{M+j+1}+\cdots+b_{M+j+m-1}\right).\]

Each of the \(m\) sequences \(b_{M+j+i}\) tends to \(L\) as \(j\to\infty\), so \(S_j\to(b_M+\cdots+b_{M+m-1})-mL\) by the limit laws. ◻ ∎

To use the theorem we must write a term as a difference; partial fractions (from the chapter on integration techniques) and logarithm rules are the usual tools. The theorem also says what goes wrong when it fails: the series converges only if \(\{b_k\}\) converges.

Example 10.3.9: A telescoping series with partial fractions

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac6{(k+1)(k+3)}\).

Solution. Partial fractions give \(\dfrac6{(k+1)(k+3)}=3\left(\dfrac1{k+1}-\dfrac1{k+3}\right)\), because \(\dfrac1{k+1}-\dfrac1{k+3}=\dfrac2{(k+1)(k+3)}\). With \(b_k=\frac1{k+1}\) this is \(3(b_k-b_{k+2})\), a step of \(m=2\), and \(b_k\to0\). Writing out the partial sum, the terms \(\frac14,\frac15,\dots\) cancel and only the first two positive and last two negative terms survive:

\[S_n=3\left(\frac12+\frac13-\frac1{n+2}-\frac1{n+3}\right).\]

Since \(\frac1{n+2}\to0\) and \(\frac1{n+3}\to0\), the value is \(3\left(\frac12+\frac13\right)=\frac52\).

Example 10.3.10: Telescoping with logarithms: one series diverges, one converges

Decide whether each series converges, and find its value if it does: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\ln\left(1+\frac1k\right)\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\ln\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)\).

Solution. (a) Since \(\ln\left(1+\frac1k\right)=\ln\frac{k+1}k=\ln(k+1)-\ln k\), the term is \(b_k-b_{k+1}\) with \(b_k=-\ln k\). Then \(S_n=\ln(n+1)-\ln1=\ln(n+1)\to\infty\). The series diverges, which also follows from the theorem because \(b_k=-\ln k\) does not converge. Notice that the terms \(\ln(1+\frac1k)\) tend to \(0\), and still the series diverges.

(b) Factor \(1-\frac1{k^2}=\frac{(k-1)(k+1)}{k^2}=\frac{k-1}k\cdot\frac{k+1}k\), so

\[\ln\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)=\ln\frac{k+1}k-\ln\frac k{k-1}=u_{k+1}-u_k\qquad\text{with }u_k=\ln\frac k{k-1}.\]

Therefore the partial sum from \(k=2\) to \(k=n\) is \(u_{n+1}-u_2=\ln\dfrac{n+1}n-\ln2=\ln\dfrac{n+1}{2n}\) (a finite telescoping sum). Because \(\frac{n+1}{2n}\to\frac12\) and \(\ln\) is continuous, \(S_n\to\ln\frac12\), so

\[\sum_{k=2}^\infty\ln\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)=-\ln2\approx-0.693 .\]

The product form explains the answer: \(\prod_{k=2}^n\left(1-\frac1{k^2}\right)=\frac{n+1}{2n}\), and the logarithm turns the product into a sum.

Knowledge Check 10.3.2: Telescoping series

Evaluate each series or say that it diverges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac1{2k}-\frac1{2k+2}\right)\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac2{k^2-1}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\sqrt k-\sqrt{k+1}\right)\).

Pause and think 10.3.2: Parentheses in an infinite sum

Group \(1-1+1-1+1-1+\cdots\) in two ways: \((1-1)+(1-1)+(1-1)+\cdots\) and \(1+(-1+1)+(-1+1)+\cdots\). What does each grouping suggest the sum is? What do the partial sums of the original series do, and what does the definition of convergence say about it? How are the partial sums of the grouped series related to those of the original?

Answer and discussion

The first grouping suggests \(0+0+\cdots=0\), the second \(1+0+0+\cdots=1\): two different answers for the same series, so grouping cannot always be allowed. The partial sums of \(1-1+1-1+\cdots\) are \(1,0,1,0,\dots\); the sequence has two subsequences with different limits (\(1\) and \(0\)), so by Theorem C.2 (see Appendix Example C.6) it diverges, and the original series has no sum. The grouped series have as partial sums a subsequence of the original ones: \(S_2,S_4,S_6,\dots\) (all \(0\)) for the first grouping and \(S_1,S_3,S_5,\dots\) (all \(1\)) for the second. For a convergent series, any subsequence of the partial sums has the same limit (Theorem C.2), so inserting parentheses never changes the value; for a divergent series it can create the appearance of a value.

Properties of convergent series¶

Theorem 10.3.11: Properties of convergent series

Let \(c\) be a real number.

(1) If \(\sum a_k\) converges to \(A\), then \(\sum ca_k\) converges to \(cA\).

(2) If \(\sum a_k\) diverges and \(c\ne0\), then \(\sum ca_k\) diverges.

(3) If \(\sum a_k\) converges to \(A\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges to \(B\), then \(\sum(a_k\pm b_k)\) converges to \(A\pm B\).

(4) If \(\sum a_k\) diverges and \(\sum b_k\) converges, then \(\sum(a_k\pm b_k)\) diverges.

(5) Let \(M\) be a positive integer. The series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k\) and \(\sum_{k=M}^\infty a_k\) either both converge or both diverge, and if they converge, \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=(a_1+\cdots+a_{M-1})+\sum_{k=M}^\infty a_k\).

Proof. Throughout, \(S_n\) and \(T_n\) are the partial sums of \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\), taken from the same starting index.

(1) The \(n\)th partial sum of \(\sum ca_k\) is \(\sum_{k}ca_k=c\sum_ka_k=cS_n\) (a property of finite sums). Since \(S_n\to A\), we get \(cS_n\to cA\) by the limit laws (Theorem B.4).

(2) Suppose \(\sum ca_k\) converged. Then by part (1), with \(\frac1c\) in place of \(c\), the series \(\sum\frac1c(ca_k)=\sum a_k\) would converge, contrary to hypothesis.

(3) The \(n\)th partial sum of \(\sum(a_k\pm b_k)\) is \(S_n\pm T_n\), which tends to \(A\pm B\) by the limit laws.

(4) Suppose \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converged. Since \(a_k=(a_k+b_k)-b_k\), part (3) would make \(\sum a_k\) converge, a contradiction. The case \(a_k-b_k\) is the same with \(a_k=(a_k-b_k)+b_k\).

(5) Let \(S_n\) be the partial sums of \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k\) and \(T_n\) those of \(\sum_{k=M}^\infty a_k\). The first \(M-1+n\) terms of the first series are the \(M-1\) leading terms followed by the first \(n\) terms of the second, so \(S_{n+M-1}=(a_1+\cdots+a_{M-1})+T_n\), that is, \(T_n=S_{n+M-1}-S_{M-1}\). If \(S_n\) converges, so does \(S_{n+M-1}\) (Theorem B.3), and \(T_n\) converges, to \(S-S_{M-1}\). Conversely, if \(T_n\) converges, then \(S_{n+M-1}=T_n+S_{M-1}\) converges, and so does \(S_n\), by Theorem B.3 again. In the convergent case \(S=S_{M-1}+T\), which is the stated formula. ◻ ∎

Split a sum only when both pieces converge

Properties (3) and (4) do not cover two divergent series: \(\sum1\) and \(\sum(-1)\) both diverge, yet \(\sum(1-1)=0+0+\cdots=0\) converges (Pause and think 10.3.3). So never split a convergent series into two series unless you know that both pieces converge. There is also no rule for products: \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac12\right)^k\left(\frac13\right)^k=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac16\right)^k=\frac65\), while \(\left(\sum\left(\frac12\right)^k\right)\left(\sum\left(\frac13\right)^k\right)=2\cdot\frac32=3\).

Example 10.3.12: Using the properties

(a) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left[\frac3{4^k}-2\left(-\frac13\right)^k\right]\). (b) Decide whether \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{4^k+7^k}{6^k}\) converges. (c) Find \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=4}^\infty\frac5{3^k}\) in two ways.

Solution. (a) Both \(\sum3\left(\frac14\right)^k\) and \(\sum2\left(-\frac13\right)^k\) are geometric with \(|r|<1\), so they converge: their values are \(\dfrac3{1-1/4}=4\) and \(\dfrac2{1+1/3}=\dfrac32\). By property (3) the combined series converges and its value is \(4-\dfrac32=\dfrac52\).

(b) Divide each term: \(\dfrac{4^k+7^k}{6^k}=\left(\dfrac23\right)^k+\left(\dfrac76\right)^k\). The first series is geometric with \(r=\frac23\) and converges (to \(\frac{2/3}{1/3}=2\)). The second is geometric with \(r=\frac76>1\) and diverges. By property (4) the series diverges. (It would be a mistake to say “the first part converges, so the series converges”.)

(c) Way 1 (the formula). The first term is \(t=\frac5{3^4}=\frac5{81}\) and \(r=\frac13\), so the value is \(\dfrac{5/81}{2/3}=\dfrac5{54}\). Way 2 (property 5). The whole series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac5{3^k}\) has value \(\frac5{2/3}=\frac{15}2\); remove the first four terms \(5+\frac53+\frac59+\frac5{27}=\frac{200}{27}\) to get \(\frac{15}2-\frac{200}{27}=\frac5{54}\). The two answers agree.

Knowledge Check 10.3.3: Properties of series

(a) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left[2\left(\frac13\right)^k+5\left(\frac1{10}\right)^k\right]\). (b) Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left[\left(\frac9{10}\right)^k-2\left(\frac{11}{10}\right)^k\right]\) converge? Name the property you use. (c) Find \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=5}^\infty\left(\frac12\right)^k\).

Pause and think 10.3.3: Two divergent series, one convergent sum

(a) Give two divergent series \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) for which \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converges. (b) Give two divergent series for which \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) diverges. (c) Which of properties (3) and (4) of Theorem 10.3.11 cannot be strengthened to cover “both series diverge”, and why?

Answer and discussion

(a) \(a_k=1\) and \(b_k=-1\): both diverge (\(r=1\) geometric, and property (2) with \(c=-1\)), while \(a_k+b_k=0\) gives a series with every partial sum \(0\). (b) \(a_k=b_k=1\): the sum \(\sum2\) diverges. (c) Neither property can be extended: property (3) needs both series to converge, since (a) shows that two divergent series can give a convergent sum, and property (4) is about exactly one divergent series, since (b) and (a) show that with two divergent series the sum can either converge or diverge, so no conclusion is possible.

Series with nonnegative terms¶

Most series have no formula for \(S_n\). For a series of nonnegative terms we can still decide convergence, because the partial sums can only move in one direction.

Theorem 10.3.13: Series with nonnegative terms

Let \(a_k\ge0\) for all \(k\), with partial sums \(S_n\). Then \(\{S_n\}\) is nondecreasing, and

(a) the series converges if and only if \(\{S_n\}\) is bounded above; in that case its value is \(\sup\{S_n\}\), so \(S_n\le\sum a_k\) for every \(n\);

(b) if \(\{S_n\}\) is not bounded above, then \(S_n\to\infty\).

Proof. \(S_{n+1}-S_n\) is the next term, which is \(\ge0\), so \(\{S_n\}\) is nondecreasing. (a) If \(\{S_n\}\) is bounded above, it converges to \(\sup\{S_n\}\) by the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Theorem A.5). If the series converges, then \(\{S_n\}\) converges and is therefore bounded (Theorem B.2). (b) Let \(K\) be any number. Since \(K\) is not an upper bound, some \(S_N>K\), and then \(S_n\ge S_N>K\) for all \(n\ge N\) because the partial sums are nondecreasing. ◻ ∎

The theorem changes the question from “what is the limit?” to “do the partial sums stay bounded?”, and bounding is often easy. For example, Example 10.2.31 showed that the partial sums of \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) stay below \(2\), so that series converges (we cannot yet say to what). The value, which is \(\frac{\pi^2}6\), is much harder.

Example 10.3.14: Convergence by bounding the partial sums

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k\,2^k}\) converges, and locate its value.

Solution. All terms are positive. For \(k\ge1\) we have \(k\,2^k\ge2^k\), so \(\dfrac1{k\,2^k}\le\dfrac1{2^k}\), and

\[S_n\le\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{2^k}=1-\frac1{2^n}<1\]

for every \(n\) (a finite geometric sum). So the partial sums are bounded above by \(1\), and the series converges by Theorem 10.3.13. The first three partial sums are \(\frac12,\ \frac58,\ \frac23\), so the value is between \(\frac23\) and \(1\). (It is in fact \(\ln2\approx0.6931\), a fact that comes from the power series of the natural logarithm, a topic of the next chapter.)

Looking ahead: series with a variable¶

If the ratio of a geometric series involves a variable \(x\), the value of the series is a function of \(x\), defined for those \(x\) where the series converges. This is the first step on the road to power series, the subject of a later chapter, where series of this kind are used to write functions such as \(\ln x\), \(e^x\) and \(\sin x\) as “infinite polynomials”.

Example 10.3.15: A geometric series with a variable

For which real numbers \(x\) does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac{x+1}4\right)^k\) converge, and what is its value?

Solution. For each fixed \(x\) this is a geometric series with \(t=1\) and \(r=\frac{x+1}4\). By Theorem 10.3.3 it converges exactly when \(\left|\frac{x+1}4\right|<1\), that is, \(|x+1|<4\), or \(-5<x<3\), and then

\[\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac{x+1}4\right)^k=\frac1{1-\frac{x+1}4}=\frac4{3-x}.\]

For example, \(x=1\) gives \(r=\frac12\) and the value \(\frac42=2\), the sum of \(1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots\). At \(x=3\) the ratio is \(1\), and at \(x=-5\) it is \(-1\); the series diverges there, and for every \(x\ge3\) and every \(x\le-5\).

Group 1: Geometric series¶

Example 10.3.16: A first term that is not the coefficient

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac5{4^k}\).

Solution. Write \(\dfrac5{4^k}=5\left(\dfrac14\right)^k\). The ratio is \(\frac14\), and since \(|r|<1\) the series converges. The series starts at \(k=1\), so the first term is \(t=\frac54\), not \(5\). The value is \(\dfrac{5/4}{1-1/4}=\dfrac53\). Check: \(S_3=\frac54+\frac5{16}+\frac5{64}=\frac{105}{64}\approx1.64\), which is below \(\frac53\approx1.67\), as it must be for positive terms.

Example 10.3.17: Finding the ratio from the terms

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{3^{k+1}}{(-5)^{k-1}}\).

Solution. The ratio of consecutive terms is constant: \(\dfrac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\dfrac{3^{k+2}/(-5)^k}{3^{k+1}/(-5)^{k-1}}=\dfrac3{-5}=-\dfrac35\), so the series is geometric with \(|r|<1\). The first term is the one at \(k=2\): \(t=\dfrac{3^3}{(-5)^1}=-\dfrac{27}5\). The value is \(\dfrac{-27/5}{1+3/5}=-\dfrac{27}8=-3.375\). Check: the partial sums \(-5.4,\ -2.16,\ -4.104,\ \dots\) alternate around the value, as they should when \(r<0\).

Group 2: Decimals and applications of geometric series¶

Example 10.3.18: A repeating block of three digits

Write \(4.\overline{036}=4.036036036\ldots\) as a fraction.

Solution. The whole-number part is \(4\). The rest, \(0.036036\ldots=0.036+0.000036+\cdots\), is geometric: each term is \(\frac1{1000}\) of the one before (the block has three digits), so \(t=0.036\) and \(r=0.001\). Its value is \(\dfrac{0.036}{1-0.001}=\dfrac{36}{999}=\dfrac4{111}\). So \(4.\overline{036}=4+\dfrac4{111}=\dfrac{448}{111}\). Check: \(448\div111=4.036036\ldots\)

Example 10.3.19: A perpetuity

A fund pays \(\$1000\) at the end of every year forever, and money earns \(4\%\) interest per year. What lump sum today has the same value as all the payments? How much of it is needed to cover the first \(30\) payments?

Solution. A payment made \(k\) years from now is worth \(\dfrac{1000}{1.04^k}\) today. The lump sum is the series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\dfrac{1000}{1.04^k}\), geometric with \(t=\dfrac{1000}{1.04}=\dfrac{25000}{26}\) and \(r=\dfrac1{1.04}=\dfrac{25}{26}<1\). Its value is \(\dfrac{25000/26}{1-25/26}=25000\) dollars. The first \(30\) payments are worth the partial sum \(S_{30}=25000\left(1-\left(\frac{25}{26}\right)^{30}\right)\approx17292.03\) (by Theorem 10.3.3, \(S_n=t\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}\) with \(\frac t{1-r}=25000\)). The rest, about \(7708\) dollars, pays for payments \(31, 32,\dots\) and is the remainder \(\frac{tr^{30}}{1-r}=25000\,r^{30}\) of part (c) of the theorem.

Group 3: Telescoping series¶

Example 10.3.20: A step of three

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac2{k(k+3)}\).

Solution. Since \(\dfrac1k-\dfrac1{k+3}=\dfrac3{k(k+3)}\), we have \(\dfrac2{k(k+3)}=\dfrac23\left(\dfrac1k-\dfrac1{k+3}\right)\). With \(b_k=\frac1k\to0\) this is a telescoping series with step \(m=3\), so the value is \(\dfrac23\left(b_1+b_2+b_3-3\cdot0\right)=\dfrac23\left(1+\dfrac12+\dfrac13\right)=\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac{11}6=\dfrac{11}9\). Check with the partial sum: \(S_n=\dfrac23\left(\dfrac{11}6-\dfrac1{n+1}-\dfrac1{n+2}-\dfrac1{n+3}\right)\), and at \(n=1\) this gives \(\frac23\left(\frac{11}6-\frac12-\frac13-\frac14\right)=\frac12\), the first term.

Example 10.3.21: A telescoping series with square roots

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{\sqrt k+\sqrt{k+1}}\) converge?

Solution. Rationalise the denominator by multiplying by \(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k\): \(\dfrac1{\sqrt k+\sqrt{k+1}}=\dfrac{\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k}{(k+1)-k}=\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k\). This is \(b_k-b_{k+1}\) with \(b_k=-\sqrt k\), and the partial sum is \(S_n=\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt1=\sqrt{n+1}-1\), which tends to \(\infty\). So the series diverges, although its terms tend to \(0\) (they behave like \(\frac1{2\sqrt k}\)). The theorem agrees: \(b_k=-\sqrt k\) does not converge.

Group 4: Combining and decomposing series¶

Example 10.3.22: Properties with given sums

Suppose \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=5\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty b_k=-2\), and \(a_1=1\), \(a_2=\frac12\). Find (a) \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty(3a_k-4b_k)\); (b) \(\sum_{k=3}^\infty a_k\); (c) decide whether \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty(a_k+2)\) converges.

Solution. (a) Both series converge, so properties (1) and (3) apply: \(\sum(3a_k-4b_k)=3\cdot5-4\cdot(-2)=23\). (b) By property (5), \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=a_1+a_2+\sum_{k=3}^\infty a_k\), so \(\sum_{k=3}^\infty a_k=5-1-\frac12=\frac72\). (c) The series \(\sum2=2+2+2+\cdots\) is geometric with \(r=1\) and diverges, while \(\sum a_k\) converges, so by property (4) the series \(\sum(a_k+2)\) diverges.

Example 10.3.23: Working backwards from the value

The geometric series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty3r^k\) has value \(12\). Find \(r\), and then find \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty3r^{2k}\).

Solution. A value is only possible if \(|r|<1\), and then \(\dfrac3{1-r}=12\). So \(1-r=\frac14\) and \(r=\frac34\), which does satisfy \(|r|<1\). The second series is \(\sum3\left(r^2\right)^k\), geometric with ratio \(r^2=\frac9{16}\) and first term \(3\), so its value is \(\dfrac3{1-9/16}=\dfrac{48}7\). Check: \(\frac{48}7\approx6.86\) is smaller than \(12\), which makes sense because \(r^2<r\) makes the terms smaller.

Group 5: Partial sums and remainders¶

Example 10.3.24: Recovering the series from its partial sums

A series has partial sums \(S_n=\dfrac{3n}{n+2}\). Find its terms and its value.

Solution. By Theorem 10.1.14, \(a_1=S_1=1\) and for \(n\ge2\)

\[a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}=\frac{3n}{n+2}-\frac{3(n-1)}{n+1}=\frac{3\left[n(n+1)-(n-1)(n+2)\right]}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac6{(n+1)(n+2)}.\]

The formula also gives \(a_1=\frac6{2\cdot3}=1\), so it holds for every \(n\ge1\). The value is \(\lim S_n=\lim\dfrac{3}{1+2/n}=3\). Check: \(a_2=S_2-S_1=\frac32-1=\frac12=\frac6{3\cdot4}\).

Example 10.3.25: A remainder with alternating signs

How many terms of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty5\left(-\frac45\right)^k\) are needed so that the partial sum is within \(0.001\) of the value?

Solution. Here \(t=5\), \(r=-\frac45\), and the value is \(S=\dfrac5{1+4/5}=\dfrac{25}9\). By Theorem 10.3.3(c), \(S-S_n=\dfrac{5(-4/5)^n}{9/5}=\dfrac{25}9\left(-\dfrac45\right)^n\). Its absolute value is \(\frac{25}9\left(\frac45\right)^n\), and we need this to be less than \(0.001\): \(\left(\frac45\right)^n<0.00036\), so \(n>\dfrac{\ln0.00036}{\ln0.8}\approx35.5\). The smallest integer is \(n=36\). (The sign of the error alternates with \(n\), but only its size matters here.) Check: \(\frac{25}9(0.8)^{35}\approx0.0011\) and \(\frac{25}9(0.8)^{36}\approx0.00088\).

Group 6: Series with a variable, and positive terms¶

Example 10.3.26: A ratio that is a fraction in \(x\)

For which \(x\) does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac2{x-1}\right)^k\) converge? Find its value there.

Solution. This is geometric with \(t=1\) and \(r=\dfrac2{x-1}\) (\(x\ne1\)). It converges exactly when \(\left|\dfrac2{x-1}\right|<1\), that is \(|x-1|>2\), so \(x<-1\) or \(x>3\). The value is \(\dfrac1{1-\frac2{x-1}}=\dfrac{x-1}{x-3}\). Check: at \(x=5\) the ratio is \(\frac12\) and the value is \(2=\frac{4}{2}\); at \(x=-3\) the ratio is \(-\frac12\) and the value is \(\frac23=\frac{-4}{-6}\).

Example 10.3.27: Positive terms, bounded partial sums

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{2^k+k}\) converges, and find an interval that contains its value.

Solution. The terms are positive, and \(2^k+k>2^k\), so \(\dfrac1{2^k+k}<\dfrac1{2^k}\). Hence \(S_n<\sum_{k=1}^n2^{-k}=1-2^{-n}<1\) for all \(n\): the partial sums are bounded above by \(1\) and the series converges (Theorem 10.3.13). By part (a) of that theorem the value is at least every partial sum; \(S_3=\frac13+\frac16+\frac1{11}=\frac{13}{22}\approx0.59\). So the value lies between \(\frac{13}{22}\) and \(1\).

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.3.28: A series that diverges by the definition

Prove that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^{k+1}k=1-2+3-4+\cdots\) diverges.

Plan. Find a formula for the partial sums and show that they are unbounded; a convergent sequence is bounded (Theorem B.2).

Proof. Claim: \(S_{2j-1}=j\) and \(S_{2j}=-j\) for every \(j\ge1\). For \(j=1\): \(S_1=1\) and \(S_2=1-2=-1\). If the claim holds for \(j\), then \(S_{2j+1}=S_{2j}+(2j+1)=-j+2j+1=j+1\) and \(S_{2j+2}=S_{2j+1}-(2j+2)=j+1-2j-2=-(j+1)\), which is the claim for \(j+1\). By induction it holds for all \(j\). Therefore \(|S_n|\ge\frac n2\) for every \(n\), so \(\{S_n\}\) is unbounded, hence divergent. ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to say “the signs alternate, so the series converges”. Alternating signs alone guarantee nothing: here the terms grow, and the partial sums swing ever more widely.

Example 10.3.29: Using the properties of series in a proof

Prove that if \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k\) converges to \(A\), then \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty(a_k+a_{k+1})\) converges to \(2A-a_1\).

Plan. The series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k+1}\) has the terms \(a_2,a_3,\dots\), which is the tail of \(\sum a_k\). Use property (5) for the tail and property (3) to add.

Proof. By property (5) with \(M=2\), \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty a_k\) converges and equals \(A-a_1\). The series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_{k+1}\) has the same terms in the same order (\(a_2,a_3,\dots\)), hence the same partial sums, so it converges to \(A-a_1\). By property (3) the series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty(a_k+a_{k+1})\) converges to \(A+(A-a_1)=2A-a_1\). ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to answer \(2A\), forgetting that \(\sum a_{k+1}\) is missing the first term \(a_1\). A check: for \(a_k=\left(\frac12\right)^k\) we have \(A=1\), \(a_1=\frac12\), and \(\sum\left(\frac1{2^k}+\frac1{2^{k+1}}\right)=\sum\frac3{2^{k+1}}=\frac32=2\cdot1-\frac12\).

Answers and discussion¶

Knowledge Check 10.3.1. (a) \(t=2\), \(r=\frac15\), converges, value \(\frac2{4/5}=\frac52\). (b) The first term is at \(k=3\): \(t=\left(-\frac12\right)^3=-\frac18\), \(r=-\frac12\), converges, value \(\frac{-1/8}{3/2}=-\frac1{12}\). (c) \(\frac{4^k}{3^{k+1}}=\frac13\left(\frac43\right)^k\), so \(r=\frac43>1\) and the series diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.3.2. (a) With \(b_k=\frac1{2k}\to0\) the term is \(b_k-b_{k+1}\), so the value is \(b_1=\frac12\). (b) \(\frac2{k^2-1}=\frac1{k-1}-\frac1{k+1}\); with \(b_k=\frac1{k-1}\to0\) and step \(2\) starting at \(k=2\), the value is \(b_2+b_3=1+\frac12=\frac32\). (c) \(S_n=\sqrt1-\sqrt{n+1}=1-\sqrt{n+1}\to-\infty\), so the series diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.3.3. (a) Both series are geometric with \(|r|<1\): \(\frac2{1-1/3}=3\) and \(\frac5{1-1/10}=\frac{50}9\), so the value is \(3+\frac{50}9=\frac{77}9\). (b) \(\sum\left(\frac9{10}\right)^k\) converges (value \(10\)), while \(\sum2\left(\frac{11}{10}\right)^k\) diverges (\(r=1.1\)), so \(\sum-2\left(\frac{11}{10}\right)^k\) diverges by property (2); by property (4) the combined series diverges. (c) \(t=\frac1{32}\), \(r=\frac12\), value \(\frac{1/32}{1/2}=\frac1{16}\); or \(2-\left(1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\frac1{16}\right)=\frac1{16}\) by property (5).
