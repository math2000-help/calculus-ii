10.1 An Overview¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

Zeno argued that you can never walk across a room: first you must cover half the distance, then half of what remains, then half of that, and so on, with infinitely many steps. Yet the sum \(\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots\) of those step lengths seems to be \(1\), the width of the room. Can an endless list of positive numbers have a finite total? What would “total” even mean for infinitely many terms? The answer in this chapter is to stop adding forever and to look instead at a list of running totals and where it is heading. That turns the question about an infinite sum into a question about a sequence, so we begin with sequences.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • write out terms of a sequence given by an explicit formula or by a recurrence, and find a formula that fits a pattern (and say why a pattern never proves a formula);

  • recognise arithmetic and geometric sequences and use their closed forms;

  • estimate the limit of a sequence from a table or a graph, and explain why such an estimate is evidence and not proof;

  • define the partial sums of a series and use them to decide, in simple cases, what a series adds up to;

  • recover the terms of a series from its partial sums, and use the finite geometric sum formula;

  • write a short induction proof.

Sequences: lists of numbers¶

Definition 10.1.1: Sequence

A sequence is an ordered list of real numbers

\[a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3,\ \dots,\ a_n,\ \dots\]

with one number \(a_n\) (the \(n\)th term) for each positive integer \(n\). We write the sequence as \(\{a_n\}\), or \(\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\) when we want to show where it starts. Equivalently, a sequence is a function whose inputs are the positive integers: \(n\mapsto a_n\). Sometimes it is convenient to start at \(n=0\) or at another integer.

Definition 10.1.2: Explicit and recursive definitions

A sequence is defined explicitly when a formula gives \(a_n\) directly from \(n\), for example \(a_n=n^2\). It is defined recursively (or by a recurrence) when the first term or terms are given and a rule gives each later term from the ones before it, for example \(a_1=1\) and \(a_{n+1}=2a_n+1\).

An explicit formula lets you jump straight to the thousandth term. A recurrence makes you walk there one step at a time, but it is often the more natural description: “each year the balance grows by \(3\%\)” is a recurrence long before it is a formula.

Example 10.1.3: Terms from an explicit formula

List the first five terms of \(a_n=\dfrac{2n-1}{n+2}\).

Solution. Substitute \(n=1,2,3,4,5\):

\[a_1=\frac13,\quad a_2=\frac34,\quad a_3=\frac55=1,\quad a_4=\frac76,\quad a_5=\frac97 .\]

Notice that the terms seem to creep upward without passing \(2\); the numerator is nearly twice the denominator when \(n\) is large. We return to this kind of question below.

Example 10.1.4: Terms from a recurrence

A sequence has \(a_1=2\) and \(a_{n+1}=3a_n-1\). List the first five terms.

Solution. The rule says: to get the next term, triple the current one and subtract \(1\). Starting from \(a_1=2\):

\[a_2=3(2)-1=5,\quad a_3=3(5)-1=14,\quad a_4=3(14)-1=41,\quad a_5=3(41)-1=122 .\]

Each term uses the one just before it, so we cannot find \(a_5\) without computing \(a_2\), \(a_3\) and \(a_4\) first. A recurrence is useless without its starting value: with \(a_1=0\) the same rule gives \(-1,-4,-13,\dots\)

Example 10.1.5: A recurrence that uses two earlier terms

Let \(a_1=1\), \(a_2=3\) and \(a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n\). List the first six terms.

Solution. Two starting values are needed because each term uses the previous two:

\[a_3=3+1=4,\quad a_4=4+3=7,\quad a_5=7+4=11,\quad a_6=11+7=18 .\]

With starting values \(1,1\) the same rule produces the Fibonacci numbers \(1,1,2,3,5,8,\dots\)

Finding a formula from a pattern¶

Given a few terms, we often want a rule that produces them. This is guesswork informed by experience: look for a common difference, a common ratio, squares, factorials, alternating signs.

Example 10.1.6: Four patterns

Find an explicit formula \(a_n\) (\(n=1,2,3,\dots\)) that fits each list.

(a) \(5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14,\ 17,\dots\) (b) \(3,\ 6,\ 12,\ 24,\dots\) (c) \(\frac12,\ -\frac14,\ \frac18,\ -\frac1{16},\dots\) (d) \(2,\ 6,\ 12,\ 20,\ 30,\dots\)

Solution. (a) Each term is \(3\) more than the one before, so \(a_n=5+3(n-1)=3n+2\). (b) Each term is twice the one before, starting at \(3\): \(a_n=3\cdot2^{n-1}\). (c) The denominators are \(2^n\) and the signs alternate, starting positive at \(n=1\): \(a_n=\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{2^n}\). (d) The differences \(4,6,8,10\) increase by \(2\) each time, which suggests a quadratic; comparing with \(n^2\) (\(1,4,9,16,25\)) shows each term is \(n\) more than \(n^2\), so \(a_n=n^2+n=n(n+1)\). Always substitute \(n=1,2,3,\dots\) back into your formula to check that it reproduces the list.

Pause and think 10.1.1: Does a list determine a sequence?

The list \(1,2,4,8,16,\dots\) looks like \(a_n=2^{n-1}\). The number of regions into which \(n\) points on a circle, joined in all possible ways, cut the disk is \(1,2,4,8,16\) for \(n=1,\dots,5\). What do you predict for \(n=6\)? Then check the formula \(r_n=\dfrac{n^4-6n^3+23n^2-18n+24}{24}\) at \(n=1,\dots,6\). What does this say about guessing a formula from a finite list?

Answer and discussion

The doubling pattern predicts \(32\) for \(n=6\). The formula gives \(1,2,4,8,16\) at \(n=1,\dots,5\), and at \(n=6\) it gives \(\frac{744}{24}=31\) (the numerator is \(1296-1296+828-108+24\)). So two different rules agree on five terms and disagree on the sixth: finitely many terms never determine a sequence. A pattern is a conjecture to be proved, for instance by induction, as in Example 10.1.22.

Arithmetic and geometric sequences¶

Definition 10.1.7: Arithmetic and geometric sequences

A sequence is arithmetic if \(a_{n+1}=a_n+d\) for a constant common difference \(d\), and geometric if \(a_{n+1}=r\,a_n\) for a constant common ratio \(r\).

Theorem 10.1.8: Closed forms

(a) If \(a_{n+1}=a_n+d\) for all \(n\ge1\), then \(a_n=a_1+(n-1)d\) for all \(n\ge1\).

(b) If \(a_{n+1}=r\,a_n\) for all \(n\ge1\), then \(a_n=a_1\,r^{\,n-1}\) for all \(n\ge1\).

Proof. Both are proved by induction on \(n\).

(a) Base case. For \(n=1\) the formula says \(a_1=a_1+0\cdot d\), which is true. Inductive step. Suppose \(a_n=a_1+(n-1)d\) for some \(n\ge1\). Then

\[a_{n+1}=a_n+d=a_1+(n-1)d+d=a_1+n\,d=a_1+\big((n+1)-1\big)d,\]

which is the formula for \(n+1\). By induction the formula holds for every \(n\ge1\).

(b) Base case. For \(n=1\) the formula says \(a_1=a_1r^0\), which is true. Inductive step. Suppose \(a_n=a_1r^{n-1}\). Then \(a_{n+1}=r\,a_n=r\cdot a_1r^{n-1}=a_1r^{n}=a_1r^{(n+1)-1}\), which is the formula for \(n+1\). By induction it holds for every \(n\ge1\). ◻ ∎

Example 10.1.9: Using the closed forms

(a) An arithmetic sequence has \(a_1=7\) and \(d=-4\). Find \(a_{20}\). (b) A geometric sequence has \(a_1=5\) and \(r=\frac12\). Find \(a_8\). (c) A geometric sequence has \(a_4=24\) and \(a_7=192\). Find \(a_1\) and \(r\).

Solution. (a) By Theorem 10.1.8(a), \(a_{20}=7+19(-4)=-69\). (b) By part (b) of the theorem, \(a_8=5\left(\frac12\right)^7=\frac5{128}\). (c) Write the two given terms with the closed form: \(a_4=a_1r^3=24\) and \(a_7=a_1r^6=192\). Dividing the second equation by the first, \(r^3=8\), so \(r=2\) (the real cube root). Then \(a_1=24/2^3=3\). Check: \(3\cdot2^6=192\).

Knowledge Check 10.1.1: Terms and formulas

(a) A sequence has \(b_1=4\) and \(b_{n+1}=2b_n-3\). List \(b_1,\dots,b_4\). (b) Find a formula for \(10,7,4,1,\dots\) (c) A geometric sequence has \(a_1=64\) and \(r=\frac12\). Find \(a_7\).

Limits of sequences: what happens for large \(n\)?¶

The question that drives this chapter is about the long run: as \(n\) grows, do the terms settle down to a single number?

Definition 10.1.10: Limit of a sequence (informal)

If the terms \(a_n\) get and stay as close as we like to a number \(L\) once \(n\) is large enough, we say the sequence converges to \(L\) and write \(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L\). A sequence that does not converge to any number diverges. The precise version, with \(\varepsilon\) and \(N\), is Definition A.2 in the appendix and is the basis of Section 10.2.

Example 10.1.11: Estimating a limit from a table

Estimate \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{3n+1}{n+4}\).

Solution. Compute some terms: \(a_{10}=\frac{31}{14}\approx2.2143\), \(a_{100}=\frac{301}{104}\approx2.8942\), \(a_{1000}=\frac{3001}{1004}\approx2.9890\). The terms increase toward \(3\). That is no accident: for large \(n\) the \(+1\) and the \(+4\) hardly matter, and the ratio is close to \(\frac{3n}{n}=3\). We estimate that the limit is \(3\). This is evidence, not proof; Section 10.2 shows how to prove it with limit laws.

Example 10.1.12: A famous limit

Estimate \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\dfrac1n\right)^n\).

Solution. The base tends to \(1\) and the exponent tends to infinity, so it is not clear what happens. Tabulate: \(a_1=2\), \(a_{10}\approx2.5937\), \(a_{100}\approx2.7048\), \(a_{1000}\approx2.7169\), \(a_{10^6}\approx2.71828\). The terms increase and appear to level off near \(2.718\ldots\), the number \(e\). The limit is indeed \(e\); proving that needs more than a table (a bounded, increasing sequence converges, by the Monotone Convergence Theorem A.5 of the appendix, but identifying the limit as \(e\) comes with the calculus of logarithms). Notice what the table does not tell us: neither factor alone, \(1+\frac1n\to1\) or exponent \(\to\infty\), gives the answer. The two effects compete and the compromise is \(e\).

A table is not a proof

A table can suggest a limit and can never prove one. Terms that agree with a number to six decimal places for \(n\le10^6\) may still wander off later (Pause and think 10.1.2). Treat tables and graphs as a way to form a conjecture and to catch errors, and use limit laws, the Squeeze Theorem and the theory of Section 10.2 to prove it.

Knowledge Check 10.1.2: Estimating limits

(a) Compute \(a_{10}\), \(a_{100}\) and \(a_{1000}\) for \(a_n=\dfrac{4n-1}{n+5}\) and estimate the limit. (b) Why can the estimate in part (a) not be called a proof? (c) Decide, by looking at the even- and odd-numbered terms, whether \(c_n=(-1)^n\dfrac{n}{n+1}\) converges.

Pause and think 10.1.2: How far can a table be trusted?

Suppose \(a_n=5+\dfrac{n}{10^{10}}\). Compute \(a_n\) for \(n=10,\ 10^3\) and \(10^6\) and describe what a table of those values suggests. What does the sequence actually do as \(n\to\infty\)?

Answer and discussion

\(a_{10}=5.000000001\), \(a_{1000}=5.0000001\) and \(a_{10^6}=5.0001\). A table of these values suggests the limit \(5\). But \(a_n=5+\frac n{10^{10}}\) grows without bound: once \(n>10^{11}\) the term exceeds \(15\). So the sequence diverges, and a table that stops at \(10^6\) cannot see it.

Infinite series and partial sums¶

Definition 10.1.13: Series and partial sums

Given a sequence \(\{a_k\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\), the infinite series \(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_k=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots\) is the formal sum of all its terms. Its \(n\)th partial sum is the sum of the first \(n\) terms,

\[S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_k=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n .\]

The numbers \(S_1,S_2,S_3,\dots\) form the sequence of partial sums \(\{S_n\}\). If \(S_n\to S\) we say the series converges, call \(S\) its sum and write \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_k=S\); otherwise the series diverges.

This is exactly how the guiding question gets answered: “what is an infinite sum?” means “what is the limit of the finite sums?”. Section 10.3 makes this the official definition and develops it; here we explore with simple cases.

Theorem 10.1.14: Terms and partial sums

Let \(S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n\) be the partial sums of the series \(\sum a_k\). Then \(a_1=S_1\) and \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\) for \(n\ge2\). If every \(a_k>0\), then \(S_1<S_2<S_3<\cdots\), that is, the partial sums are increasing.

Proof. \(S_1=a_1\) by definition. For \(n\ge2\),

\[S_n-S_{n-1}=(a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1}+a_n)-(a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1})=a_n .\]

If all \(a_k>0\), then \(S_n-S_{n-1}=a_n>0\), so \(S_n>S_{n-1}\) for every \(n\ge2\). ◻ ∎

Theorem 10.1.15: Finite geometric sum

For every real number \(r\ne1\) and every integer \(n\ge1\),

\[1+r+r^2+\cdots+r^{n-1}=\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}.\]

Proof. Let \(G=1+r+\cdots+r^{n-1}\). Multiply by \(1-r\) and let the terms cancel in pairs:

\[(1-r)G=(1+r+\cdots+r^{n-1})-(r+r^2+\cdots+r^{n})=1-r^n ,\]

because every power \(r^1,\dots,r^{n-1}\) appears once with each sign. Since \(r\ne1\) we may divide by \(1-r\). ◻ ∎

Consequently the partial sums of the geometric series \(\sum_{k=1}^n a r^{k-1}\) are \(S_n=a\,\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r}\) when \(r\ne1\).

Example 10.1.16: Zeno’s series

Find the partial sums of \(\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac12\right)^k\) and decide what the series adds up to.

Solution. The first partial sums are \(S_1=\frac12\), \(S_2=\frac34\), \(S_3=\frac78\): each time we fill half of the remaining gap to \(1\). This suggests \(S_n=1-\frac1{2^n}\). To prove it, use the finite geometric sum with first term \(a=\frac12\) and ratio \(r=\frac12\):

\[S_n=\frac12\cdot\frac{1-(1/2)^n}{1-1/2}=1-\frac1{2^n}.\]

As \(n\to\infty\), \(\frac1{2^n}\to0\), so \(S_n\to1\) and the series converges to \(1\), in agreement with Zeno’s room.

Example 10.1.17: A telescoping series

Find a formula for \(S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac1{k(k+1)}\) and the sum of the series.

Solution. The key is the partial fraction identity \(\dfrac1{k(k+1)}=\dfrac1k-\dfrac1{k+1}\), which holds because \(\dfrac1k-\dfrac1{k+1}=\dfrac{(k+1)-k}{k(k+1)}\). Then the sum collapses:

\[S_n=\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)=1-\frac1{n+1}=\frac n{n+1}.\]

Every term except the first and the last cancels with a neighbour. Since \(\frac n{n+1}=1-\frac1{n+1}\to1\), the series converges to \(1\).

Example 10.1.18: A repeating decimal as a series

Write \(0.333\ldots\) as a series and find its sum.

Solution. \(0.333\ldots=\frac3{10}+\frac3{100}+\frac3{1000}+\cdots\), a geometric series with \(a=\frac3{10}\) and \(r=\frac1{10}\). By the finite geometric sum,

\[S_n=\frac3{10}\cdot\frac{1-10^{-n}}{1-\frac1{10}}=\frac{1-10^{-n}}{3}.\]

Since \(10^{-n}\to0\), \(S_n\to\frac13\). So “\(0.333\ldots=\frac13\)” is a true statement about the limit of the partial sums \(0.3,\ 0.33,\ 0.333,\dots\)

Example 10.1.19: From partial sums back to terms

A series has partial sums \(S_n=2n^2+n\). Find its terms \(a_n\) and decide whether the series converges.

Solution. By Theorem 10.1.14, \(a_1=S_1=3\) and, for \(n\ge2\),

\[a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}=(2n^2+n)-\big(2(n-1)^2+(n-1)\big)=4n-1 .\]

The formula \(4n-1\) also gives \(a_1=3\), so it works for every \(n\ge1\): the series is \(3+7+11+15+\cdots\). The partial sums \(2n^2+n\) grow without bound, so the series diverges. Check: \(3+7+11=21=S_3=2(9)+3\).

Knowledge Check 10.1.3: Partial sums

(a) Use the finite geometric sum to find \(S_n\) for \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}3\left(\frac14\right)^{k-1}\) and compute \(S_3\) both from the formula and directly. (b) A series has \(S_n=n^2+2n\). Find \(a_n\). (c) Is the series \(1+2+3+\cdots\) (whose partial sums are \(S_n=\frac{n(n+1)}2\)) convergent?

Pause and think 10.1.3: Is \(0.999\ldots\) equal to \(1\)?

Write \(0.999\ldots\) as a series \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac9{10^k}\), find a formula for its partial sums, and say what the series equals. Then explain, in your own words, why this is not a contradiction of the feeling that \(0.999\ldots\) is “just below” \(1\).

Answer and discussion

The partial sums are \(S_n=9\left(\frac1{10}+\cdots+\frac1{10^n}\right)=\frac9{10}\cdot\frac{1-10^{-n}}{1-\frac1{10}}=1-10^{-n}\), and \(1-10^{-n}\to1\), so the series equals \(1\). Each partial sum (\(0.9,\ 0.99,\ 0.999,\dots\)) is below \(1\), and that is the source of the feeling; but the series is not any partial sum. Its value is the limit of the partial sums, and the limit of numbers that creep up to \(1\), getting and staying as close to it as we like, is \(1\). Nothing sits strictly between \(0.999\ldots\) and \(1\).

Looking ahead¶

Everything in the rest of the chapter grows out of these two ideas. Section 10.2 develops the limit of a sequence rigorously, including a theorem that decides many questions at once: a bounded, increasing sequence converges (Theorem A.5). Since the partial sums of a series with positive terms are increasing (Theorem 10.1.14), the same theorem will say that such a series converges exactly when its partial sums stay bounded. Later sections build tests that decide convergence without finding a formula for \(S_n\), and Chapter 11 turns series into the road to power series: infinite sums of powers of \(x\) that represent functions.

Group 1: Explicit formulas¶

Example 10.1.20: Terms and a target value

For \(a_n=n^2-3n\), list \(a_1,\dots,a_5\) and find the \(n\) for which \(a_n=130\).

Solution. Substituting, \(a_1=-2\), \(a_2=-2\), \(a_3=0\), \(a_4=4\), \(a_5=10\); the sequence dips below zero at the start because \(3n\) beats \(n^2\) for \(n=1,2\). To solve \(n^2-3n=130\), move everything to one side: \(n^2-3n-130=0\), which factors as \((n-13)(n+10)=0\). The root \(n=-10\) is not a positive integer, so it is rejected, and \(n=13\). Check: \(13^2-39=169-39=130\).

Example 10.1.21: Alternating signs and factorials

List the first four terms of \(a_n=\dfrac{(-1)^n2^n}{n!}\) and say what happens to the size of the terms.

Solution. The factor \((-1)^n\) gives the sign: negative for odd \(n\), positive for even \(n\). The size is \(2^n/n!\). So

\[a_1=-\frac21=-2,\quad a_2=\frac42=2,\quad a_3=-\frac86=-\frac43,\quad a_4=\frac{16}{24}=\frac23 .\]

The size at first grows (from \(2\) to \(2\)) and then shrinks, because each step multiplies the size by \(\frac2{n+1}\): the ratio \(\frac{2^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{2^n/n!}=\frac{2}{n+1}\) is less than \(1\) once \(n\ge2\). For example \(\frac{2^6}{6!}=\frac{64}{720}=\frac4{45}\). The factorial eventually beats any power.

Group 2: Recurrences¶

Example 10.1.22: A recurrence with a surprise

Let \(a_1=1\) and \(a_{n+1}=\dfrac{a_n}{1+a_n}\). Find \(a_2,a_3,a_4\) and guess a formula.

Solution. \(a_2=\dfrac{1}{1+1}=\dfrac12\). Then \(a_3=\dfrac{1/2}{1+1/2}=\dfrac{1/2}{3/2}=\dfrac13\) and \(a_4=\dfrac{1/3}{4/3}=\dfrac14\). The pattern suggests \(a_n=\dfrac1n\). This is only a guess from four terms (compare Pause and think 10.1.1); we can prove it by induction: if \(a_n=\frac1n\) then \(a_{n+1}=\dfrac{1/n}{1+1/n}=\dfrac{1}{n+1}\). The guess is therefore correct.

Example 10.1.23: Repeated square roots

Let \(a_1=1\) and \(a_{n+1}=\sqrt{6+a_n}\). Compute \(a_2,a_3,a_4,a_5\) to four decimals and guess the limit.

Solution. Use a calculator at each step: \(a_2=\sqrt7\approx2.6458\), \(a_3=\sqrt{6+2.6458}\approx2.9404\), \(a_4\approx2.9900\), \(a_5\approx2.9983\). The terms increase and approach \(3\). A reason for the guess: if \(a_n\to L\) then also \(a_{n+1}\to L\), so \(L=\sqrt{6+L}\); squaring, \(L^2-L-6=0\), that is \((L-3)(L+2)=0\). Since all terms are positive, \(L=3\). (This argument assumes the limit exists; Section 10.2 shows when that is justified.)

Group 3: Formulas from patterns¶

Example 10.1.24: A quadratic pattern

Find a formula for \(2,\ 5,\ 10,\ 17,\ 26,\dots\)

Solution. The differences are \(3,5,7,9\), increasing by \(2\) each time, so the pattern is quadratic with leading term \(n^2\). Comparing with \(1,4,9,16,25\), each term is exactly \(1\) larger: \(a_n=n^2+1\). Check: \(a_5=26\).

Example 10.1.25: Powers in the denominator and a shift

Find formulas for (a) \(\frac13,\ \frac29,\ \frac3{27},\ \frac4{81},\dots\) and (b) \(\frac32,\ \frac54,\ \frac98,\ \frac{17}{16},\dots\)

Solution. (a) Numerators \(1,2,3,4\) and denominators \(3,9,27,81=3^n\): \(a_n=\dfrac n{3^n}\). (b) The denominators are \(2^n\); the numerators \(3,5,9,17\) are \(2^n+1\) (that is, \(2+1,4+1,8+1,16+1\)). So \(b_n=\dfrac{2^n+1}{2^n}=1+\dfrac1{2^n}\), which also shows the terms decrease toward \(1\).

Group 4: Limits from tables and graphs¶

Example 10.1.26: A convergent sequence from a table

Estimate \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{5n}{2n+3}\).

Solution. \(a_{10}=\frac{50}{23}\approx2.1739\), \(a_{100}=\frac{500}{203}\approx2.4631\), \(a_{1000}=\frac{5000}{2003}\approx2.4963\). The terms increase toward \(2.5\). Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n\) gives \(\dfrac{5}{2+3/n}\), and \(\frac3n\to0\), so the limit is \(\frac52\), which matches the table.

Example 10.1.27: A divergent sequence

Examine \(a_n=(-1)^n+\dfrac1n\) for large \(n\).

Solution. \(a_2=1.5\), \(a_3\approx-0.6667\), \(a_{100}=1.01\), \(a_{101}\approx-0.9901\). The even-numbered terms are near \(1\) and the odd-numbered terms are near \(-1\), so the terms do not settle down to one number: the sequence diverges. A graph shows two strings of dots, one hugging the line \(y=1\) and one hugging \(y=-1\). (If a limit \(L\) existed, all large terms would be near \(L\), and no number is within \(\frac12\) of both \(1\) and \(-1\); this is made precise in Appendix B.9.)

Group 5: Models¶

Example 10.1.28: Compound growth

An account holds $2000 and earns \(3\%\) per year. Write the balance as a sequence and find when it first exceeds $3000.

Solution. Let \(A_n\) be the balance after \(n\) years. Each year the balance is multiplied by \(1.03\), so \(A_{n+1}=1.03A_n\) with \(A_0=2000\): a geometric sequence, \(A_n=2000(1.03)^n\). We need \(2000(1.03)^n>3000\), that is \((1.03)^n>1.5\). Trying values, \(A_{13}\approx2937.07<3000\) and \(A_{14}\approx3025.18>3000\). So the balance first exceeds $3000 after \(14\) years. (Taking logarithms, \(n>\ln1.5/\ln1.03\approx13.72\).)

Example 10.1.29: A repeated dose

A patient takes 100 mg of a drug each morning, and each day the body removes \(20\%\) of the drug present. Let \(D_n\) be the amount just after the \(n\)th dose. Find \(D_1,\dots,D_4\), and check that \(D_n=500(1-0.8^n)\) fits.

Solution. \(D_1=100\). Each day \(80\%\) remains, then \(100\) mg is added: \(D_{n+1}=0.8D_n+100\). So \(D_2=0.8(100)+100=180\), \(D_3=0.8(180)+100=244\), \(D_4=0.8(244)+100=295.2\). The formula gives \(500(1-0.8)=100\), \(500(1-0.64)=180\), \(500(1-0.512)=244\), \(500(1-0.4096)=295.2\), all matching. As \(n\) grows, \(0.8^n\to0\), so the amounts approach \(500\) mg: the dose never builds up without limit, because the body removes more each day as the level rises. (Proof that the formula is correct for all \(n\): Example 10.1.33.)

Group 6: Partial sums¶

Example 10.1.30: A telescoping series with step \(2\)

Find \(S_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\dfrac1{(2k-1)(2k+1)}\) and the sum of the series.

Solution. Partial fractions: \(\dfrac1{(2k-1)(2k+1)}=\dfrac12\left(\dfrac1{2k-1}-\dfrac1{2k+1}\right)\), since the bracket equals \(\dfrac{(2k+1)-(2k-1)}{(2k-1)(2k+1)}=\dfrac2{(2k-1)(2k+1)}\). The sum telescopes:

\[S_n=\frac12\left[\left(1-\frac13\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac15\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1{2n-1}-\frac1{2n+1}\right)\right]=\frac12\left(1-\frac1{2n+1}\right)=\frac n{2n+1}.\]

Check with \(n=3\): \(\frac11\!\cdot\!\frac13+\frac13\!\cdot\!\frac15+\frac15\!\cdot\!\frac17=\frac13+\frac1{15}+\frac1{35}=\frac37\), and \(\frac3{2\cdot3+1}=\frac37\). Since \(\frac n{2n+1}\to\frac12\), the series converges to \(\frac12\).

Example 10.1.31: A geometric series

Find \(S_n\) for \(\sum_{k=1}^n5\left(\frac23\right)^{k-1}\), check \(S_4\), and decide what the series adds up to.

Solution. With \(a=5\) and \(r=\frac23\) the finite geometric sum gives

\[S_n=5\cdot\frac{1-(2/3)^n}{1-2/3}=15\left(1-\left(\frac23\right)^n\right).\]

Directly, \(S_4=5\left(1+\frac23+\frac49+\frac8{27}\right)=5\cdot\frac{65}{27}=\frac{325}{27}\), and the formula gives \(15\left(1-\frac{16}{81}\right)=15\cdot\frac{65}{81}=\frac{325}{27}\).  \(\checkmark\) Because \((2/3)^n\to0\), \(S_n\to15\), so the series converges and its sum is \(15\).

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.1.32: Induction: the sum of the first \(n\) odd numbers

Prove that \(1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2\) for every integer \(n\ge1\).

Proof. Base case \(n=1\): the left side is \(1\) and the right side is \(1^2=1\).

Inductive step. Assume the formula holds for some \(n\ge1\), that is \(1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2\). The next term is \(2(n+1)-1=2n+1\). Adding it to both sides,

\[1+3+\cdots+(2n-1)+(2n+1)=n^2+2n+1=(n+1)^2 ,\]

which is the formula for \(n+1\). By induction the formula holds for all \(n\ge1\). ◻ ∎

In terms of partial sums, this says the series \(1+3+5+\cdots\) has \(S_n=n^2\), and then Theorem 10.1.14 recovers \(a_n=n^2-(n-1)^2=2n-1\).

Example 10.1.33: Induction on a recurrence

Prove that if \(D_1=100\) and \(D_{n+1}=0.8D_n+100\), then \(D_n=500(1-0.8^n)\) for all \(n\ge1\).

Proof. Base case \(n=1\): \(500(1-0.8)=500(0.2)=100=D_1\).

Inductive step. Assume \(D_n=500(1-0.8^n)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned} D_{n+1}&=0.8D_n+100=0.8\cdot500(1-0.8^n)+100\\ &=400-500(0.8^{n+1})+100=500-500(0.8^{n+1})=500\left(1-0.8^{n+1}\right), \end{aligned}\]

using \(0.8\cdot0.8^n=0.8^{n+1}\) and \(0.8\cdot500=400\). This is the formula for \(n+1\), so by induction it holds for all \(n\ge1\). ◻ ∎

This is the formula guessed in Example 10.1.29; the proof shows that it was not an accident of the first four terms. A general formula of this type is proved in Section 10.2.
