6.7 Physical Applications¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

How much does a bar weigh if it gets denser toward one end? How much work does it take to stretch a spring, to wind in a chain, to pump a tank empty? What is the total force of the water on a dam or on a window of a pool? In every one of these problems something that is easy for a small piece (mass is density times length, work is force times distance, force is pressure times area) must be added over a variable situation. We do this once and for all with a single idea: cut into slices, bound each slice between its least and greatest value, add, and squeeze. We state plainly the few physical facts we assume (the mass of a uniform bar, work equals force times distance for a constant force, the pressure of water at depth \(d\) is \(\rho g d\)), prove that the formulas follow, and then solve the standard problems: mass of a thin bar, springs, chains, buckets, pumping tanks of every shape, and force on dams and windows. Each answer is checked against a case that can be done without calculus.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • say what physical facts are assumed (mass, work, pressure) and what is proved, and prove that mass is \(\int\rho\), that work is \(\int F\), that lifting work is \(g\int\lambda\,D\) and that fluid force is \(\rho g\int(\text{depth})\,(\text{width})\);

  • find the mass and the average density of a bar with a variable or piecewise density;

  • find the work to stretch or compress a spring, also from a position that is not the equilibrium, and the work to lift a chain, a cable or a leaking bucket;

  • set up and evaluate a pumping problem for a tank of any shape: choose the origin, find the cross-sectional area \(A(y)\) and the distance \(D(y)\) that each layer is lifted;

  • find the force on a dam or a window from its width function, and explain how the orientation of a plate changes the force;

  • check an answer against a bound (a bar between two constant densities; work between “all water lifted from the top” and “all water lifted from the bottom”; force between the pressures at the top and bottom of a plate) and against a case of “force \(=\) pressure \(\times\) area” or “work \(=\) weight \(\times\) height”.

What is assumed and what is proved¶

Sections 6.1 to 6.6 took the meaning of area, volume and length for granted at a few places and said so. We do the same for physics, and we say plainly what is assumed. Masses are in kilograms (kg), lengths in metres (m), forces in newtons (N, the force that gives \(1\,\text{kg}\) an acceleration of \(1\,\text{m/s}^2\)), work in joules (J, \(1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m}\)), and \(g=9.8\,\text{m/s}^2\), so a mass of \(\mu\) kg weighs \(\mu g\) newtons. Water has density \(\rho=1000\,\text{kg/m}^3\).

Definition 6.7.1: Physical assumptions

Mass. (M1) A straight bar of length \(\ell\) and uniform linear density \(\sigma\) (kg per metre) has mass \(\sigma\ell\). (M2) If a bar is cut into pieces, the masses of the pieces add up to the mass of the bar. (M3) The density \(\rho(x)\) of a bar is its mass per unit length at \(x\): if on a piece \(I\) of the bar the density is everywhere between \(m\) and \(M\), then the mass of that piece is between \(m\,\abs I\) and \(M\,\abs I\), where \(\abs I\) is the length of \(I\).

Work. (W1) A constant force \(F\) along the \(x\)-axis (positive if it points in the positive direction) that moves an object from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) does the work \(F\,(b-a)\). (W2) Work adds when a motion is cut into stages, and the work for the reversed motion has the opposite sign. (W3) If during a motion over a stretch \(I\) in the positive direction the force is everywhere between \(m\) and \(M\), then the work is between \(m\,\abs I\) and \(M\,\abs I\). (W4) If a body of mass \(\mu\) is lifted vertically and each of its points is lifted through a distance between \(d_1\) and \(d_2\), the work is between \(\mu g\,d_1\) and \(\mu g\,d_2\), and it is exactly \(\mu g\,d\) if every point is lifted through the same distance \(d\) (a rigid body).

Fluid. (P1) The force that a fluid of density \(\rho\) exerts on a flat horizontal surface of area \(A\) at depth \(d\) is the weight \(\rho g\,d\,A\) of the column of fluid above it; the pressure (force per unit area) is \(\rho g d\) and it has the same magnitude in all directions, so the pressure on a vertical wall at depth \(d\) is also \(\rho g d\). (P2) If the pressure on a flat piece of surface of area \(A\) is everywhere between \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), the force on it is between \(p_1A\) and \(p_2A\) (it is \(pA\) if the pressure is the constant \(p\)). (P3) Forces on pieces of a surface add.

What is assumed and what is proved

Assumed (the physics): (M1) to (M3), (W1) to (W4), (P1) to (P3), and the meaning of area (A1) to (A3) of Section 6.2 and of volume (V1) to (V3) of Section 6.3. These say how mass, work and pressure behave for a uniform bar, a constant force and a flat surface; Pascal’s observation that the pressure at a point is the same in all directions is part of (P1) and is not proved here. Proved below: that mass is \(\int\rho\,dx\) (Theorem 6.7.4), work is \(\int F\,dx\) (Theorem 6.7.8), the work to lift a body is \(g\int\lambda(y)D(y)\,dy\) (Theorem 6.7.11), the force on a vertical plate is \(\int p(y)\,w(y)\,dy\) (Theorem 6.7.21), the bounds, and the consistency checks of Theorem 6.7.31. Calculus I facts used: the Extreme Value Theorem, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the substitution rule, that the integral is additive and preserves inequalities (Theorem 6.1.6), the area \(\pi r^2\) of a disk, and (F4) of Section 6.5: for a continuous \(h\), every Riemann sum \(\sum h(\xi_k)\Delta x_k\) (with \(\xi_k\) in the \(k\)th subinterval) is as close as we like to \(\int_a^bh\) once every \(\Delta x_k\) is small enough. Water is assumed to be at rest (hydrostatics) and incompressible, so \(\rho\) is constant.

Slice, add, squeeze¶

All four formulas of this section come from one idea, which we prove twice (once for a density per unit length, once for a product of a density and a distance). A quantity \(Q(I)\) belongs to every closed subinterval \(I=[u,v]\) of \([a,b]\) (the mass of a piece of bar, the work to lift a layer, the force on a strip). We say \(Q\) is additive if \(Q([u,w])=Q([u,v])+Q([v,w])\) whenever \(u<v<w\). We bound each \(Q(I)\) between a lower and an upper value, cut \([a,b]\) into \(n\) pieces of equal length \(\Delta=(b-a)/n\), add the bounds, and let \(n\to\infty\).

Theorem 6.7.2: Slice, add, squeeze: density form

Let \(\rho\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and let \(Q\) be additive. Suppose that for every subinterval \(I\) we have \(m_I\,\abs I\le Q(I)\le M_I\,\abs I\), where \(m_I\) and \(M_I\) are the least and the greatest value of \(\rho\) on \(I\). Then

\[Q([a,b])=\int_a^b\rho(x)\,dx .\]

Proof. Let \(n\) be a positive integer, \(\Delta=(b-a)/n\), and \(I_k=[a+(k-1)\Delta,\,a+k\Delta]\) for \(k=1,\dots,n\). By the Extreme Value Theorem there are points \(s_k\) and \(s_k'\) in \(I_k\) with \(\rho(s_k)=m_{I_k}\) and \(\rho(s_k')=M_{I_k}\). Since \(Q\) is additive, \(Q([a,b])=\sum_kQ(I_k)\), and the hypothesis gives

\[\sum_{k=1}^n\rho(s_k)\,\Delta\;\le\;Q([a,b])\;\le\;\sum_{k=1}^n\rho(s_k')\,\Delta .\]

Both sums are Riemann sums of the continuous function \(\rho\). By (F4), given \(\varepsilon>0\) both are within \(\varepsilon\) of \(\int_a^b\rho\) once \(n\) is large, so \(\int_a^b\rho-\varepsilon<Q([a,b])<\int_a^b\rho+\varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, \(Q([a,b])=\int_a^b\rho\). (This is the squeeze used for arc length in Theorem 6.5.4 and for surface area in Theorem 6.6.4.) ◻ ∎

The next form is for quantities in which a distance or a depth multiplies a mass or an area. A function \(D\) is called \(K\)-Lipschitz if \(\abs{D(y)-D(z)}\le K\,\abs{y-z}\) for all \(y,z\); a linear function \(D(y)=\pm y+c\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz, and \(D(y)=\rho g\,(H-y)\) is \(\rho g\)-Lipschitz.

Theorem 6.7.3: Slice, add, squeeze: weighted form

Let \(\lambda\ge0\) be continuous on \([a,b]\), let \(D\) be \(K\)-Lipschitz on \([a,b]\), and let \(Q\) be additive. For a subinterval \(I\) put \(\Lambda(I)=\int_I\lambda\) and let \(D_{\min}(I)\), \(D_{\max}(I)\) be the least and the greatest value of \(D\) on \(I\). Suppose that for every subinterval \(I\)

\[D_{\min}(I)\,\Lambda(I)\;\le\;Q(I)\;\le\;D_{\max}(I)\,\Lambda(I).\]

Then

\[Q([a,b])=\int_a^b\lambda(y)\,D(y)\,dy .\]

Proof. Let \(n\), \(\Delta\) and \(I_k\) be as before. Two points of \(I_k\) are at most \(\Delta\) apart, so \(D_{\min}(I_k)\ge D(y)-K\Delta\) and \(D_{\max}(I_k)\le D(y)+K\Delta\) for every \(y\in I_k\). Because \(\lambda\ge0\), multiplying by \(\lambda(y)\) and integrating over \(I_k\) (the integral preserves inequalities) gives

\[\int_{I_k}D\lambda-K\Delta\,\Lambda(I_k)\le D_{\min}(I_k)\Lambda(I_k)\le Q(I_k)\le D_{\max}(I_k)\Lambda(I_k)\le\int_{I_k}D\lambda+K\Delta\,\Lambda(I_k).\]

Add over \(k=1,\dots,n\), using that \(Q\) is additive and that the integral is additive over adjacent intervals:

\[\int_a^bD\lambda-K\Delta\int_a^b\lambda\;\le\;Q([a,b])\;\le\;\int_a^bD\lambda+K\Delta\int_a^b\lambda .\]

The number \(\int_a^b\lambda\) does not depend on \(n\) and \(\Delta\to0\), so \(Q([a,b])=\int_a^bD\lambda\). ◻ ∎

Figure. The idea of Theorem 6.7.2 for \(\rho(x)=\dfrac4{(1+x)^2}\) on \([0,3]\) with three pieces. On each piece the mass lies between the lower rectangle (least density, shaded) and the upper rectangle (greatest density, dashed): \(1+\frac49+\frac14=\frac{61}{36}\) and \(4+1+\frac49=\frac{49}9\). With more pieces the two sums squeeze together on the mass, \(3\) (Example 6.7.6).

Mass of a thin bar¶

Theorem 6.7.4: The mass of a thin bar

A thin bar or wire lies along the interval \(a\le x\le b\) and has a continuous linear density \(\rho(x)\) (in kilograms per metre). Then its mass is

\[m=\int_a^b\rho(x)\,dx .\]

If the density is continuous on each of finitely many adjacent intervals but jumps at their end points, the mass is the sum of the integrals over the intervals.

Proof. For a piece \(I=[u,v]\) let \(Q(I)\) be its mass. By (M2) \(Q\) is additive, and by (M3) \(m_I\abs I\le Q(I)\le M_I\abs I\), where \(m_I\) and \(M_I\) are the least and the greatest value of \(\rho\) on \(I\). By Theorem 6.7.2, \(Q([a,b])=\int_a^b\rho\). If the density is continuous on each of \([a,c]\) and \([c,b]\) but jumps at \(c\), apply this to each interval and add the masses by (M2). ◻ ∎

Theorem 6.7.5: Bounds and the average density

Let \(\rho\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) with \(\rho_1\le\rho(x)\le\rho_2\) and let \(\ell=b-a\). The mass satisfies \(\rho_1\ell\le m\le\rho_2\ell\). The average density \(\bar\rho=m/\ell\) lies between the least and the greatest density, and \(\bar\rho=\rho(\xi)\) for some \(\xi\) in \([a,b]\). A bar of constant density \(\sigma\) has \(m=\sigma\ell\), as in (M1).

Proof. The integral preserves inequalities (Theorem 6.1.6), so \(\rho_1\ell=\int_a^b\rho_1\,dx\le\int_a^b\rho\,dx\le\int_a^b\rho_2\,dx=\rho_2\ell\). Taking \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) to be the least and the greatest value of \(\rho\) (they exist by the Extreme Value Theorem), \(\bar\rho=m/\ell\) lies between them; by the Intermediate Value Theorem the continuous function \(\rho\) takes the value \(\bar\rho\) at some \(\xi\in[a,b]\). If \(\rho\equiv\sigma\) then \(m=\int_a^b\sigma\,dx=\sigma\ell\). ◻ ∎

Example 6.7.6: A bar that thins out: a rational density

A bar occupies \(0\le x\le3\) (metres) and has density \(\rho(x)=\dfrac4{(1+x)^2}\) kg/m. Find its mass and its average density, and the point where the density equals the average.

Solution. Mass. By Theorem 6.7.4 (the density is continuous), \(m=\int_0^3\frac4{(1+x)^2}\,dx\). Substitute \(u=1+x\), \(du=dx\); the limits \(x=0,3\) become \(u=1,4\):

\[m=\int_1^44u^{-2}\,du=4\Bigl[-\frac1u\Bigr]_1^4=4\Bigl(1-\frac14\Bigr)=3\ \text{kg}.\]

Average density. \(\bar\rho=\dfrac m\ell=\dfrac33=1\) kg/m. The density at the ends is \(\rho(0)=4\) and \(\rho(3)=\frac14\), so \(\frac14\le\bar\rho\le4\), as Theorem 6.7.5 requires. Where is it average? Solve \(\dfrac4{(1+\xi)^2}=1\): \((1+\xi)^2=4\), so \(1+\xi=2\) (the root \(-2\) is not on the bar) and \(\xi=1\).

Figure. The mass is the area under the density curve (shaded). The rectangle of height \(\bar\rho=1\) and width \(3\) has the same area.

Answer. \(m=3\) kg, \(\bar\rho=1\) kg/m, attained at \(x=1\).

Example 6.7.7: A bar with an exponential density

A wire occupies \(0\le x\le4\) with density \(\rho(x)=6e^{-x/2}\) g/cm (lengths in cm). Find its mass, bound it, and find its average density.

Solution. Mass. Substitute \(u=-x/2\), \(du=-\frac12dx\), so \(dx=-2\,du\); the limits become \(u=0\) and \(u=-2\):

\[m=\int_0^46e^{-x/2}\,dx=-12\int_0^{-2}e^u\,du=-12\bigl(e^{-2}-1\bigr)=12\bigl(1-e^{-2}\bigr)\approx10.38\ \text{g}.\]

Bounds. The density decreases from \(\rho(0)=6\) to \(\rho(4)=6e^{-2}\approx0.81\), so \(0.81\cdot4\le m\le6\cdot4\), that is \(3.25\le m\le24\): true, but not sharp. Average. \(\bar\rho=m/4\approx2.59\) g/cm, which lies between \(0.81\) and \(6\).

A common mistake. Integrating \(e^{-x/2}\) as \(e^{-x/2}/(-1)\) and forgetting the factor \(2\) from the inner function \(-x/2\): always differentiate your antiderivative to check it.

Knowledge check: mass¶

Knowledge Check 6.7.1: Mass of a bar

A bar lies on \(0\le x\le2\) with density \(\rho(x)=3+x\) kg/m. (a) Find its mass. (b) Find its average density and a point where the density equals it. (c) Give the lower and upper bound for the mass from the least and the greatest density.

Pause and think 6.7.1: Why an integral and not a sum?

For a bar of varying density, the sum \(\sum\rho(x_k)\Delta x\) is only an approximation of the mass. Why does the squeeze of Theorem 6.7.2 show that the integral is not an approximation but the mass itself? What would go wrong if the density jumped inside a piece?

Answer and discussion

The squeeze shows that the lower and upper sums bracket the exact mass of every piece, and that the pieces add; as the pieces shrink, both bounds tend to the integral. If the density jumps inside a piece the bounds \(m_I,M_I\) are still valid (use the integral of the piecewise function), but the extreme values are not attained by a continuous function, so the proof is done piece by piece.

Work and springs¶

Theorem 6.7.8: Work is the integral of the force

A force \(F(x)\) along the \(x\)-axis, continuous on \([a,b]\), moves an object from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\), where \(a<b\). The work done by the force is

\[W=\int_a^bF(x)\,dx ,\]

in joules if \(F\) is in newtons and \(x\) in metres. If the object moves from \(x=b\) back to \(x=a\) the work is \(-\int_a^bF\,dx\), that is, \(\int_b^aF(x)\,dx\) with the usual convention for reversed limits. For a constant force \(F\) this is \(F(b-a)\), as in (W1).

Proof. For a stretch \(I=[u,v]\) let \(Q(I)\) be the work for the motion over \(I\). By (W2) \(Q\) is additive, and by (W3) \(m_I\abs I\le Q(I)\le M_I\abs I\) with \(m_I\), \(M_I\) the least and the greatest value of \(F\) on \(I\). Theorem 6.7.2 gives \(Q([a,b])=\int_a^bF\). The reversed motion has the opposite sign by (W2). For \(F\equiv F_0\) we get \(\int_a^bF_0\,dx=F_0(b-a)\). ◻ ∎

Springs. Attach an object to a spring on a frictionless surface and let \(x\) be the position of the object, with \(x=0\) at the equilibrium (the spring neither stretched nor compressed), \(x>0\) for a stretched and \(x<0\) for a compressed spring. Hooke’s law says that the force needed to hold the spring at position \(x\) is \(F(x)=kx\), where the spring constant \(k>0\) (in N/m) measures its stiffness. This is a law of the material, valid for many springs when \(x\) is not too large; it is an assumption, not a theorem. The force \(F\) is the one that you apply, and it points the way the spring is displaced: positive for \(x>0\) and negative for \(x<0\).

Theorem 6.7.9: Work on a Hooke spring

For a spring with constant \(k\), the work you do in moving the end from \(x=a\) to \(x=b\) (any real \(a\), \(b\)) is

\[W=\int_a^bkx\,dx=\frac k2\,\bigl(b^2-a^2\bigr).\]

In particular, to stretch the spring from equilibrium to \(x=s>0\), or to compress it from equilibrium to \(x=-s\), takes the same work \(\frac k2s^2\). The work to go from \(a\) to \(b\) is the area of a trapezoid: \(W=\dfrac{F(a)+F(b)}2\,(b-a)\) when \(0\le a<b\).

Proof. By Theorem 6.7.8 (and, if \(b<a\), its statement for the reversed motion) \(W=\int_a^bkx\,dx=\bigl[\frac k2x^2\bigr]_a^b=\frac k2(b^2-a^2)\). For \(a=0\), \(b=s\) this is \(\frac k2s^2\), and for \(a=0\), \(b=-s\) it is \(\frac k2s^2\) as well, because \((-s)^2=s^2\). If \(0\le a<b\) then \(\frac k2(b^2-a^2)=\frac k2(b+a)(b-a)=\frac{ka+kb}2(b-a)=\frac{F(a)+F(b)}2(b-a)\), the area of the trapezoid under the graph of \(F\) between \(a\) and \(b\). ◻ ∎

Example 6.7.10: Springs: from equilibrium and from a stretched position

A force of \(15\) N holds a spring stretched \(0.06\) m beyond its equilibrium. Assume Hooke’s law. (a) Find \(k\). (b) How much work stretches it \(0.10\) m from equilibrium? (c) How much additional work stretches it from \(0.06\) m to \(0.12\) m? (d) How much work compresses it \(0.05\) m from equilibrium?

Solution. (a) \(F(0.06)=k\cdot0.06=15\), so \(k=250\) N/m and \(F(x)=250x\).

(b) \(W=\int_0^{0.10}250x\,dx=125x^2\Big|_0^{0.10}=125\cdot0.01=1.25\) J.

(c) The spring already stretched to \(a=0.06\) is taken to \(b=0.12\): \(W=\int_{0.06}^{0.12}250x\,dx=125\bigl(0.12^2-0.06^2\bigr)=125\,(0.0144-0.0036)=1.35\) J. Check: the force rises from \(F(0.06)=15\) N to \(F(0.12)=30\) N, whose average is \(22.5\) N, and \(22.5\cdot0.06=1.35\) J.

(d) Compressing from \(0\) to \(-0.05\): \(W=\int_0^{-0.05}250x\,dx=125x^2\Big|_0^{-0.05}=125\cdot0.0025=0.3125\) J, the same as stretching \(0.05\) m.

Figure. The force graph \(F=250x\). The work from \(x=0.06\) to \(x=0.12\) is the shaded trapezoid, area \(\frac{15+30}2\cdot0.06=1.35\) J.

Answer. (a) \(250\) N/m; (b) \(1.25\) J; (c) \(1.35\) J; (d) \(0.3125\) J. Part (c) costs more than stretching the spring \(0.06\) m from equilibrium (\(\frac{250}2\cdot0.06^2=0.45\) J) because the spring pulls back harder when it is already stretched.

Knowledge Check 6.7.2: A spring

A spring has \(k=200\) N/m. (a) What is the work to stretch it from \(x=0.1\) m to \(x=0.3\) m? (b) To compress it from \(0\) to \(-0.2\) m? (c) Is the work from \(0.1\) to \(0.3\) equal to the work from \(0\) to \(0.2\)? Explain.

Lifting: chains, cables and buckets¶

A rigid body lifted a height \(h\) needs the work \(\mu g h\) (W4). A chain or a cable is not rigid: the top of a hanging chain is lifted far less than its bottom. Place a vertical \(y\)-axis and let the part of the body between the heights \(u\) and \(v\) have mass \(\int_u^v\lambda(y)\,dy\), so \(\lambda(y)\) is the mass per unit height. Every point of the body is raised to the same final level, and \(D(y)\) is the distance through which the point at height \(y\) is raised.

Theorem 6.7.11: Work to lift a body

A body occupies the heights \(a\le y\le b\) on a vertical axis (up or down). The part between heights \(u\) and \(v\) has mass \(\int_u^v\lambda\), where \(\lambda\ge0\) is continuous, and the point at height \(y\) is lifted through the distance \(D(y)\ge0\), where \(D(y)=\abs{\text{(final level)}-y}\) is a \(1\)-Lipschitz function (for instance \(D(y)=h-y\) when all points are lifted up to the level \(h\)). The work to lift it is

\[W=g\int_a^b\lambda(y)\,D(y)\,dy .\]

Proof. For \(I=[u,v]\) let \(Q(I)\) be the work for the part of the body between the heights \(u\) and \(v\). Work on separate parts adds by (W2), so \(Q\) is additive. That part has mass \(\mu(I)=\Lambda(I)=\int_I\lambda\) and each of its points is lifted through a distance between \(D_{\min}(I)\) and \(D_{\max}(I)\), so by (W4) \(D_{\min}(I)\,g\Lambda(I)\le Q(I)\le D_{\max}(I)\,g\Lambda(I)\). Apply Theorem 6.7.3 to the weight function \(g\lambda\) and to \(D\) (with \(K=1\)). ◻ ∎

Procedure 6.7.12: Lifting problems

  1. Draw the body and a vertical axis (up or down); choose the origin where the numbers are simple. Write the interval \([a,b]\) that the body occupies.

  2. Find the mass per unit height \(\lambda(y)\) (kg per metre): for a chain or cable it is its density.

  3. Find the distance \(D(y)\ge0\) through which the point at height \(y\) is lifted: the final level minus \(y\) for an upward axis, \(y\) minus the final level for a downward axis.

  4. Evaluate \(W=g\int_a^b\lambda(y)D(y)\,dy\) (in joules). Parts that are rigid (a load) are added separately by \(\mu g d\). Check the answer with a bracket \(\mu g D_{\min}\le W\le\mu g D_{\max}\), where \(\mu=\int\lambda\).

Example 6.7.13: A hanging chain with a load

A chain of length \(12\) m and density \(1.8\) kg/m hangs from a platform. A bucket of mass \(20\) kg is attached to its lower end. Find the work to pull the chain and the bucket up to the platform, with two different origins.

Solution. Origin at the bottom. Let \(y=0\) be the bottom end and \(y\) point up, so the chain occupies \(0\le y\le12\) and the platform is at \(y=12\). Here \(\lambda=1.8\) and \(D(y)=12-y\). By Theorem 6.7.11,

\[W_{\text{chain}}=9.8\int_0^{12}1.8\,(12-y)\,dy=9.8\cdot1.8\Bigl[12y-\frac{y^2}2\Bigr]_0^{12}=9.8\cdot1.8\cdot72=1270.08\ \text{J}.\]

The bucket is rigid and every point is lifted \(12\) m, so \(W_{\text{bucket}}=20\cdot9.8\cdot12=2352\) J. The total is \(1270.08+2352=3622.08\) J. Origin at the platform, axis downward. Let \(s=0\) at the platform and \(s\) increase downward: the chain occupies \(0\le s\le12\) and the point at \(s\) is lifted \(D(s)=s\). Then \(W_{\text{chain}}=9.8\int_0^{12}1.8\,s\,ds=9.8\cdot1.8\cdot\frac{144}2=1270.08\) J, the same.

Figure. The chain hangs from the platform. The point at height \(y\) must rise \(D(y)=12-y\); the bottom rises \(12\) m, the top not at all.

Answer. \(3622.08\) J (about \(3.6\) kJ). Check: a chain of mass \(1.8\cdot12=21.6\) kg, weight \(211.68\) N, has its centre of mass \(6\) m below the platform, and \(211.68\cdot6=1270.08\) J.

Example 6.7.14: A leaking bucket

A bucket of mass \(2\) kg holds \(10\) kg of water. It is lifted straight up \(15\) m by a rope of negligible weight while water leaks out at a constant rate of \(0.4\) kg per metre of height. Find the work.

Solution. At height \(y\) (metres above the start, \(0\le y\le15\)) the bucket and water have mass \(12-0.4y\) kg, so the force needed to lift it slowly is its weight \(F(y)=9.8\,(12-0.4y)\) N, which is positive (at \(y=15\) the mass is still \(6\) kg). The force is continuous, so by Theorem 6.7.8

\[W=\int_0^{15}9.8\,(12-0.4y)\,dy=9.8\Bigl[12y-0.2y^2\Bigr]_0^{15}=9.8\,(180-45)=1323\ \text{J}.\]

Check. If no water leaked, the work would be \(12\cdot9.8\cdot15=1764\) J; if the bucket had its final mass of \(6\) kg all the way, \(6\cdot9.8\cdot15=882\) J. The answer \(1323\) J lies between them, and it equals \(9\cdot9.8\cdot15\): the average mass \(\frac{12+6}2=9\) kg times \(g\) times the height, because the mass is a linear function of the height.

Answer. \(1323\) J.

Pumping fluid out of a tank¶

Water in a tank is a body whose mass per unit height is easy to find: a horizontal layer of the water at height \(y\) has the shape of the tank there, and its volume is the area \(A(y)\) of the horizontal cross section times the thickness. So a pumping problem is a lifting problem with \(\lambda(y)=\rho A(y)\).

Theorem 6.7.15: Work to pump a tank

A tank holds a fluid of density \(\rho\) occupying the heights \(a\le y\le b\) on an upward vertical axis, and \(A(y)\) is the area of the horizontal cross section of the fluid at height \(y\), continuous on \([a,b]\). The work to pump all of the fluid up to the level \(y=h\), where \(h\ge b\), is

\[W=\rho g\int_a^bA(y)\,(h-y)\,dy .\]

Proof. By Theorem 6.3.2 (slicing along the \(y\)-axis) the fluid between the heights \(u\) and \(v\) has volume \(\int_u^vA(y)\,dy\), so by (M1) (mass is density times volume) its mass is \(\int_u^v\rho A(y)\,dy\). Thus \(\lambda=\rho A\) is continuous and \(\ge0\), and every point at height \(y\) is lifted the distance \(D(y)=h-y\ge0\), which is \(1\)-Lipschitz. Theorem 6.7.11 gives the formula. ◻ ∎

The distance \(h-y\) is the one that depends on where the water goes, not on the shape of the tank. If \(y\) is measured downward from the outlet instead (so the outlet is at \(s=0\) and the water at depths \(s\)), the layer at \(s\) is lifted \(D(s)=s\) and the integral is \(\rho g\int A(s)\,s\,ds\) over the depths that the water occupies. The two choices give the same work (Knowledge Check 6.7.3).

Procedure 6.7.16: Pumping problems

  1. Draw the tank, a vertical axis and the outlet level. Choose the origin where \(A(y)\) is simple (the bottom, the apex of a cone, the centre of a sphere or of a circular cross section).

  2. Find the interval \([a,b]\) occupied by the water (a half-full tank has \(b\) below the top of the tank) and the outlet level \(h\).

  3. Find \(A(y)\) from the shape: a rectangle (length \(\times\) width of the chord), a disk \(\pi r(y)^2\) (find \(r(y)\) by similar triangles or from the equation of the circle).

  4. Find \(D(y)=h-y\).

  5. Compute \(W=\rho g\int_a^bA(y)D(y)\,dy\) with \(\rho=1000\) kg/m\(^3\) and \(g=9.8\) m/s\(^2\) for water. Check with a bracket (Theorem 6.7.30): the mass \(M=\rho V\) times \(g\) times the smallest and the greatest lift.

Example 6.7.17: A cylinder with the outlet above the top

A cylindrical tank of radius \(3\) m and height \(8\) m stands on its end and is full of water. The water is pumped to an outlet \(2\) m above the top of the tank. Find the work.

Solution. Let \(y\) be the height above the bottom, so the water occupies \(0\le y\le8\) and the outlet is at \(h=10\). Every cross section is a disk of radius \(3\), so \(A(y)=9\pi\), and the layer at height \(y\) is lifted \(D(y)=10-y\) (from \(10\) m for the bottom layer down to \(2\) m for the top layer). Then

\[\begin{aligned} W&=1000\cdot9.8\int_0^89\pi\,(10-y)\,dy=9800\cdot9\pi\Bigl[10y-\frac{y^2}2\Bigr]_0^8\\ &=9800\cdot9\pi\cdot48=4\,233\,600\,\pi\approx1.33\times10^{7}\ \text{J}. \end{aligned}\]

Check. The volume is \(9\pi\cdot8=72\pi\) m\(^3\), the mass \(72\,000\pi\) kg and the weight \(705\,600\pi\) N. The layers are lifted between \(2\) m and \(10\) m, so \(705\,600\pi\cdot2\le W\le705\,600\pi\cdot10\). And \(W=705\,600\pi\cdot6\): the water behaves as if it were lifted \(6\) m, the lift of its middle layer (\(y=4\)).

Figure. A layer at height \(y\) is a disk of area \(9\pi\). It is lifted \(10-y\) metres to the outlet.

Half full. If the tank is only half full (\(0\le y\le4\)) the lift is still \(10-y\): \(W=9800\cdot9\pi\bigl[10y-\frac{y^2}2\bigr]_0^4=9800\cdot9\pi\cdot32=2\,822\,400\,\pi\), which is two thirds of the full work, not half. The top half of the water (\(4\le y\le8\)) costs \(9800\cdot9\pi\cdot16=1\,411\,200\,\pi\), half of the bottom half.

Answer. \(W=4\,233\,600\,\pi\approx1.33\times10^7\) J (\(13.3\) MJ).

Example 6.7.18: A conical tank

A tank has the shape of an inverted cone (apex down) with height \(6\) m and top radius \(1.5\) m. It is full of water, which is pumped to the top of the tank. Find the work. How much work is needed when the water is only \(3\) m deep?

Solution. Cross section. Put the origin at the apex, \(y\) up. At height \(y\) the cross section is a disk whose radius \(r(y)\) is proportional to \(y\) (similar triangles): \(\dfrac{r}{y}=\dfrac{1.5}6\), so \(r=\dfrac y4\) and \(A(y)=\pi\dfrac{y^2}{16}\). The outlet is at the top, \(h=6\), so \(D(y)=6-y\).

Full tank. The water occupies \(0\le y\le6\):

\[W=9800\int_0^6\pi\frac{y^2}{16}(6-y)\,dy=\frac{9800\pi}{16}\Bigl[2y^3-\frac{y^4}4\Bigr]_0^6=\frac{9800\pi}{16}\,(432-324)=66\,150\,\pi\approx2.08\times10^5\ \text{J}.\]

Check. The volume is \(\frac13\pi\cdot1.5^2\cdot6=4.5\pi\) m\(^3\), so the weight is \(44\,100\pi\) N and \(W/(\text{weight})=1.5\) m. This lies between \(0\) and \(6\) and it is small because most of the water of a cone is near the wide top.

Half the depth. If the water is \(3\) m deep it occupies \(0\le y\le3\) and the same integrand (the outlet is still at \(6\)) gives \(\frac{9800\pi}{16}\bigl[2y^3-\frac{y^4}4\bigr]_0^3=\frac{9800\pi}{16}\cdot33.75\). The ratio to the full tank is \(\dfrac{33.75}{108}=\dfrac5{16}\). Half the depth is \(\frac18\) of the volume, and the water that remains is the lowest water, lifted farthest, so the work is \(\frac5{16}\) of the full work.

Figure. The cone, with the layer at height \(y\) of radius \(y/4\). The layer at \(y=3\) has radius \(\frac34\) m and is lifted \(D=6-3=3\) m to the outlet.

Answer. \(66\,150\,\pi\approx2.08\times10^5\) J.

Example 6.7.19: A hemispherical bowl

A bowl is a hemisphere of radius \(3\) m, flat side up and full of water. Find the work to pump the water up to the rim.

Solution. Put the origin at the centre of the rim and \(y\) up. The bowl is the part of the ball \(x^2+y^2\le9\) with \(y\le0\); the water occupies \(-3\le y\le0\) and the rim is at \(h=0\). The layer at height \(y\) is a disk of radius \(\sqrt{9-y^2}\), so \(A(y)=\pi\,(9-y^2)\), and \(D(y)=0-y=-y\) (\(\ge0\) since \(y\le0\)). Only powers of \(y\) appear:

\[\begin{aligned} W&=9800\pi\int_{-3}^0(9-y^2)(-y)\,dy=9800\pi\int_{-3}^0(y^3-9y)\,dy\\ &=9800\pi\Bigl[\frac{y^4}4-\frac{9y^2}2\Bigr]_{-3}^0=9800\pi\cdot\frac{81}4=198\,450\,\pi\approx6.23\times10^5\ \text{J}. \end{aligned}\]

Check. The volume is \(\frac23\pi\cdot27=18\pi\) m\(^3\), the weight \(176\,400\pi\) N, and \(W/\text{weight}=\frac{81/4}{18}=\frac98=1.125\) m, between \(0\) and \(3\): the water is lifted on average \(1.125\) m, because the bowl is widest near the rim.

Figure. The bowl. At height \(y=-2\) the layer has radius \(\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5\) and is lifted \(2\) m to the rim.

Answer. \(W=198\,450\,\pi\approx6.23\times10^5\) J.

Example 6.7.20: A tank on its side

A cylindrical tank of radius \(3\) m and length \(6\) m lies on its side and is half full of oil of density \(850\) kg/m\(^3\). The oil is pumped out through a pipe at the top of the tank. Find the work.

Solution. Set-up. Put the origin on the axis of the cylinder, \(y\) up. The end of the tank is the circle \(x^2+y^2=9\), the oil occupies \(-3\le y\le0\) and the pipe is at the top, \(h=3\). The layer at height \(y\) is a rectangle \(6\) m long and \(2\sqrt{9-y^2}\) m wide (the chord of the circle at height \(y\)): \(A(y)=12\sqrt{9-y^2}\). The layer is lifted \(D(y)=3-y\), which runs from \(6\) to \(3\). With \(\rho g=850\cdot9.8=8330\),

\[W=8330\int_{-3}^012\sqrt{9-y^2}\,(3-y)\,dy=99\,960\Bigl[3\int_{-3}^0\sqrt{9-y^2}\,dy-\int_{-3}^0y\sqrt{9-y^2}\,dy\Bigr].\]

First integral (geometry). The region \(0\le x\le\sqrt{9-y^2}\), \(-3\le y\le0\) is a quarter of the disk of radius \(3\), with area \(\frac14\pi\cdot3^2=\frac{9\pi}4\), so \(\int_{-3}^0\sqrt{9-y^2}\,dy=\dfrac{9\pi}4\).

Second integral (substitution). Let \(u=9-y^2\), \(du=-2y\,dy\), so \(y\,dy=-\frac12du\); \(y=-3\) gives \(u=0\) and \(y=0\) gives \(u=9\):

\[\int_{-3}^0y\sqrt{9-y^2}\,dy=-\frac12\int_0^9\sqrt u\,du=-\frac12\cdot\frac23u^{3/2}\Big|_0^9=-\frac13\cdot27=-9.\]

Together. \(W=99\,960\bigl(3\cdot\frac{9\pi}4+9\bigr)=99\,960\bigl(\frac{27\pi}4+9\bigr)=674\,730\pi+899\,640\approx3.02\times10^6\) J. Check. The oil is half of the tank, volume \(\frac12\pi\cdot9\cdot6=27\pi\) m\(^3\), mass \(22\,950\pi\) kg and weight \(224\,910\pi\approx7.07\times10^5\) N. Then \(W/\text{weight}\approx4.27\) m, between the smallest lift \(3\) m (oil at the top, \(y=0\)) and the greatest lift \(6\) m (oil at the bottom).

Figure. The circular end of the tank. At \(y=-1\) the chord has half-width \(\sqrt{9-1}=2\sqrt2\) and the layer is lifted \(3-(-1)=4\) m.

Answer. \(W=674\,730\pi+899\,640\approx3.02\times10^6\) J.

Force and pressure¶

Fluid presses on everything it touches. A fluid of density \(\rho\) at depth \(d\) below its surface has pressure \(p=\rho g d\) (newtons per square metre), the same in every direction (P1). A horizontal plate of area \(A\) at depth \(d\) carries the weight of the column above it, \(\rho g d\,A\). On a vertical plate the depth, hence the pressure, changes from point to point, so the plate has to be cut into horizontal strips.

Theorem 6.7.21: Hydrostatic force on a vertical plate

A flat plate lies in a vertical plane under fluid of density \(\rho\). Let \(d\) be the depth below the surface. The plate occupies the depths \(c\le d\le e\), and the width of the plate at depth \(d\) is \(w(d)\), continuous and \(\ge0\). The force of the fluid on one side of the plate is

\[F=\rho g\int_c^e d\;w(d)\,\mathrm dd ,\]

in newtons for \(\rho\) in kg/m\(^3\), \(g=9.8\) m/s\(^2\) and lengths in metres. (Equivalently, with an upward axis \(y\) and the surface at \(y=H\), \(F=\rho g\int w(y)\,(H-y)\,dy\).)

Proof. For \(I=[u,v]\subseteq[c,e]\) let \(Q(I)\) be the force on the part of the plate between the depths \(u\) and \(v\); by (P3) \(Q\) is additive. That part has area \(\Lambda(I)=\int_I w\) (Theorem 6.2.12, area by horizontal strips), and every point of it has pressure between \(\rho g u\) and \(\rho g v\), so by (P2) \(\rho g\,u\,\Lambda(I)\le Q(I)\le\rho g\,v\,\Lambda(I)\). The depth function \(D(d)=d\) is \(1\)-Lipschitz and \(u,v\) are its least and greatest values on \(I\), so Theorem 6.7.3 (with the weight \(\rho g\,w\)) gives \(Q([c,e])=\rho g\int_c^e d\,w(d)\,\mathrm dd\). The form with \(y\) follows from \(d=H-y\). ◻ ∎

Procedure 6.7.22: Force on a dam or window

  1. Draw the plate and a depth axis pointing down from the surface (\(d=0\) at the surface). Mark the depths of the top and the bottom edge.

  2. Find the width \(w(d)\) of the plate at depth \(d\) (a line, a circle or a parabola: write the edge as a function of \(d\) and double it if the plate is symmetric).

  3. Evaluate \(F=\rho g\int d\,w(d)\,\mathrm dd\) over the depths of the plate. The depth is measured from the water surface, not from the top of the plate.

  4. Check with Theorem 6.7.30: \(\rho g\,d_{\min}A\le F\le\rho g\,d_{\max}A\).

Example 6.7.23: A rectangular window

A rectangular window \(2\) m wide and \(1.5\) m tall lies in a vertical wall of an aquarium. Its top edge is \(1\) m below the surface. Find the force of the water on it.

Solution. The depths run from \(1\) to \(2.5\) and \(w=2\) for all \(d\):

\[F=9800\int_1^{2.5}2d\,\mathrm dd=9800\bigl[d^2\bigr]_1^{2.5}=9800\,(6.25-1)=51\,450\ \text{N}.\]

Check. The area is \(3\) m\(^2\) and the centre is at depth \(1.75\) m, so \(\rho g\cdot1.75\cdot3=9800\cdot5.25=51\,450\) N. This agrees with the average-depth rule of Theorem 6.7.28.

Answer. \(F=51\,450\) N.

Example 6.7.24: A triangular plate: two orientations

A plate in the shape of an isosceles triangle with base \(2\) m and height \(3\) m is submerged vertically with its top at depth \(1\) m. Find the force on it (a) when the base is on top and the apex points down, and (b) when the apex is on top and the base is below.

Solution. In both cases the plate occupies the depths \(1\le d\le4\). (a) The width is \(2\) at \(d=1\) and \(0\) at \(d=4\), linear in \(d\): \(w(d)=\frac23\,(4-d)\). Then

\[F=9800\int_1^4\frac23\,(4-d)\,d\;\mathrm dd=9800\cdot\frac23\Bigl[2d^2-\frac{d^3}3\Bigr]_1^4=9800\cdot\frac23\Bigl(\frac{32}3-\frac53\Bigr)=9800\cdot6=58\,800\ \text{N}.\]

(b) The width is \(0\) at \(d=1\) and \(2\) at \(d=4\): \(w(d)=\frac23\,(d-1)\).

\[F=9800\int_1^4\frac23\,(d-1)\,d\;\mathrm dd=9800\cdot\frac23\Bigl[\frac{d^3}3-\frac{d^2}2\Bigr]_1^4=9800\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac{27}2=88\,200\ \text{N}.\]

Check. The area is \(3\) m\(^2\). The centre of a triangle is one third of the height from the base, so it is at depth \(2\) in (a) and \(3\) in (b): \(9800\cdot2\cdot3=58\,800\) and \(9800\cdot3\cdot3=88\,200\).

Answer. (a) \(58\,800\) N; (b) \(88\,200\) N. The force is larger in (b) because more of the plate lies deeper.

Example 6.7.25: A trapezoidal dam

A dam is a vertical trapezoid \(20\) m high, \(16\) m wide at the bottom and \(36\) m wide at the top. The water is level with the top of the dam. Find the force on the dam.

Solution. Let \(y\) be the height above the bottom. The width grows linearly from \(16\) to \(36\) over \(20\) m, so \(w(y)=16+y\). The depth is \(H-y=20-y\). By Theorem 6.7.21 (form with \(y\))

\[F=9800\int_0^{20}(16+y)(20-y)\,dy=9800\int_0^{20}\bigl(320+4y-y^2\bigr)\,dy=9800\Bigl[320y+2y^2-\frac{y^3}3\Bigr]_0^{20}.\]

The bracket is \(6400+800-\frac{8000}3=\frac{13\,600}3\), so \(F=\dfrac{133\,280\,000}3\approx4.44\times10^7\) N. Check. The area is \(\frac{16+36}2\cdot20=520\) m\(^2\) and the depths run from \(0\) to \(20\), so \(0\le F\le9800\cdot20\cdot520=1.02\times10^8\). The force is \(\rho g\bar dA\) with \(\bar d=\frac{F}{\rho gA}=\frac{13\,600/3}{520}\approx8.72\) m, below the middle depth of \(10\) m because the dam is wider at the top.

Answer. \(F=\dfrac{133\,280\,000}3\approx4.44\times10^7\) N.

Example 6.7.26: A parabolic dam

A dam is \(10\) m high and its face is bounded by the parabola with width \(w(y)=2\sqrt{10y}\) at height \(y\) above the bottom (so it is \(20\) m wide at the top \(y=10\)). The water is level with the top. Find the force.

Solution. The depth is \(10-y\), so

\[\begin{aligned} F&=9800\int_0^{10}2\sqrt{10}\,\sqrt y\,(10-y)\,dy=9800\cdot2\sqrt{10}\int_0^{10}\bigl(10y^{1/2}-y^{3/2}\bigr)\,dy\\ &=9800\cdot2\sqrt{10}\Bigl[\frac{20}3y^{3/2}-\frac25y^{5/2}\Bigr]_0^{10}. \end{aligned}\]

At \(y=10\): \(y^{3/2}=10\sqrt{10}\) and \(y^{5/2}=100\sqrt{10}\), so the bracket is \(\sqrt{10}\,\bigl(\frac{200}3-40\bigr)=\frac{80\sqrt{10}}3\) and

\[F=9800\cdot2\sqrt{10}\cdot\frac{80\sqrt{10}}3=9800\cdot\frac{1600}3=\frac{15\,680\,000}3\approx5.23\times10^6\ \text{N}.\]

Check. The area is \(\int_0^{10}2\sqrt{10y}\,dy=2\sqrt{10}\cdot\frac23\,10^{3/2}=\frac{400}3\) m\(^2\), so \(\bar d=\frac{1600/3}{400/3}=4\) m. This is the average depth, smaller than \(5\), because the face is wide near the top.

Answer. \(F=\dfrac{15\,680\,000}3\approx5.23\times10^6\) N.

Example 6.7.27: A half-disk window

A window is a half-disk of radius \(2\) m, with its diameter horizontal at the top, \(1\) m below the surface. Find the force of the water on it.

Solution. Let \(t\) be the depth below the diameter, \(0\le t\le2\). The depth below the surface is \(d=1+t\) and the width of the half-disk at \(t\) is the chord \(2\sqrt{4-t^2}\). Then

\[F=9800\int_0^22\sqrt{4-t^2}\,(1+t)\,dt=19\,600\Bigl[\int_0^2\sqrt{4-t^2}\,dt+\int_0^2t\sqrt{4-t^2}\,dt\Bigr].\]

The first integral is a quarter of a disk of radius \(2\) (geometry): \(\frac14\pi\cdot4=\pi\). For the second let \(u=4-t^2\), \(t\,dt=-\frac12du\); \(t=0\) gives \(u=4\) and \(t=2\) gives \(u=0\):

\[\int_0^2t\sqrt{4-t^2}\,dt=\frac12\int_0^4\sqrt u\,du=\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot8=\frac83 .\]

Hence \(F=19\,600\,\bigl(\pi+\frac83\bigr)\approx113\,842\) N. Check. The area is \(2\pi\) and the depths lie between \(1\) and \(3\), so \(9800\cdot2\pi\cdot1\approx61\,575\le F\le9800\cdot2\pi\cdot3\approx184\,726\). The result lies between them; the average depth is \(\bar d=\frac F{\rho gA}=1+\frac8{3\pi}\approx1.85\) m.

Figure. The half-disk window. The diameter is at depth \(1\), the lowest point at depth \(3\).

Answer. \(F=19\,600\bigl(\pi+\tfrac83\bigr)\approx1.14\times10^5\) N.

Theorem 6.7.28: Force equals pressure at the average depth

Under the conditions of Theorem 6.7.21 let \(A=\int_c^ew\) be the area of the plate and \(\bar d=\frac1A\int_c^ed\,w(d)\,\mathrm dd\) the width-weighted average depth. Then \(F=\rho g\,\bar d\,A\), and \(c\le\bar d\le e\). For a plate that is symmetric about a horizontal line at depth \(d_0\) (a rectangle, a disk, an ellipse), \(\bar d=d_0\), so \(F=\rho g\,d_0\,A\): the force is the pressure at the centre times the area.

Proof. The first statement is the definition of \(\bar d\) in Theorem 6.7.21. Since \(c\le d\le e\) and \(w\ge0\), \(c\,A\le\int d\,w\le e\,A\) (Theorem 6.1.6), so \(c\le\bar d\le e\). If the plate is symmetric about the depth \(d_0\), write \(d=d_0+t\) with \(-r\le t\le r\) and \(w\) even in \(t\). Then \(\int d\,w=d_0\int w+\int t\,w(t)\,dt=d_0A\), because \(t\,w(t)\) is odd and its integral over \([-r,r]\) is \(0\). ◻ ∎

Warning. For a circle of radius \(R\) with centre at depth \(d_0\) we get \(F=\rho g\,d_0\,\pi R^2\). This is not the force that you would get by treating the pressure as that of the lowest point or of the top: only the centre depth works, and only for symmetric plates.

Example 6.7.29: A circular window

A round porthole of radius \(0.5\) m has its centre \(4\) m below the surface. Find the force of the water.

Solution. The plate is symmetric about its centre, so by Theorem 6.7.28 \(F=9800\cdot4\cdot\pi\cdot0.5^2=9800\pi\approx30\,788\) N. By hand: with \(d=4+t\) and \(w=2\sqrt{\frac14-t^2}\), \(F=9800\bigl[4\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}w\,dt+\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}t\,w\,dt\bigr]\). The first integral is the area of the disk and the second is odd, so \(F=9800\,\bigl(4\cdot\frac\pi4+0\bigr)=9800\pi\approx30\,788\) N. Answer. \(9800\pi\approx3.08\times10^4\) N.

Pause and think 6.7.3: Why horizontal strips?

Pressure depends only on depth, so on a horizontal strip it is (nearly) constant. What goes wrong if you cut a dam into vertical strips and give every strip the pressure at its middle?

Answer and discussion

On a vertical strip the depth changes from the top of the strip to the bottom, so the pressure is far from constant, and “the pressure at the middle” gives only a rough value for each strip; more fundamentally, the squeeze needs strips on which the pressure varies little, which is true only for horizontal strips.

Bounds and consistency checks¶

Theorem 6.7.30: Bracketing the work and the force

(a) In Theorem 6.7.15, let \(V=\int_a^bA\) be the volume of the fluid. Then \(\rho g V\,(h-b)\le W\le\rho g V\,(h-a)\): the work lies between lifting all the fluid from its top surface and lifting all of it from the bottom. (b) In Theorem 6.7.21, \(\rho g\,c\,A\le F\le\rho g\,e\,A\), where \(A\) is the area of the plate.

Proof. (a) For \(a\le y\le b\) we have \(h-b\le h-y\le h-a\) and \(\rho gA(y)\ge0\), so \(\rho gA(y)(h-b)\le\rho gA(y)(h-y)\le\rho gA(y)(h-a)\). Integrating (Theorem 6.1.6) gives the bounds, since \(\int_a^b\rho gA\,dy=\rho gV\). (b) The same argument applies to \(d\,w(d)\) with \(c\le d\le e\). ◻ ∎

Theorem 6.7.31: Consistency checks

(a) A uniform bar of length \(\ell\) and constant density \(\sigma\) has mass \(\int_0^\ell\sigma=\sigma\ell\), as in (M1). (b) A rigid body of mass \(M\) lifted through \(h\) needs \(W=Mgh\). (c) A Hooke spring needs \(W=\frac k2s^2\) to stretch it \(s\), and this equals the area of the triangle under the force line \(F=kx\) between \(0\) and \(s\). (d) A thin horizontal plate of area \(A\) at depth \(H\) has force \(F\approx\rho gHA\): for a vertical window of height \(\delta\) whose bottom edge is at depth \(H\) and which has constant width \(w\), \(F/(\text{area})\to\rho gH\) as \(\delta\to0\).

Proof. (a) is the integral of a constant. (b) With \(\lambda\) the mass per unit height and \(D\equiv h\), Theorem 6.7.11 gives \(W=g\,h\int\lambda=g\,h\,M\). (c) The integral \(\int_0^skx\,dx=\frac k2s^2\) is the area of the triangle with base \(s\) and height \(ks\) (Theorem 6.7.9). (d) The window occupies the depths \(H-\delta\le d\le H\), with area \(w\delta\); Theorem 6.7.30(b) gives \(\rho g(H-\delta)\le F/(w\delta)\le\rho gH\), and the left side tends to \(\rho gH\) as \(\delta\to0\) (squeeze). ◻ ∎

Pascal’s principle (in words): pressure applied to a confined fluid is passed on in all directions without loss; it is the reason why the pressure \(\rho gd\) in (P1) does not depend on the direction of the plate.

Looking ahead¶

Mass was found from a density on a line. For a flat plate or a solid body the density depends on two or three variables, and the mass and the centre of mass come from double and triple integrals, in a later chapter. Work done by a variable force is also the key to the work–energy theorem of physics: the net work on an object equals the change of its kinetic energy. The work to lift a satellite far above the Earth uses the force \(GMm/r^2\) and leads to an improper integral and to the idea of an escape speed; the limit uses the methods for improper integrals in the chapter on techniques of integration (words only). The differential equations of a later chapter describe, for example, Newton’s law of cooling and the motion of a spring with friction.

Group 1: Mass¶

Example 6.7.32: A piecewise density

A rod \(5\) m long has density \(3\) kg/m on \(0\le x<2\) and \(1+x\) kg/m on \(2\le x\le5\). Find its mass and its average density.

Solution. By Theorem 6.7.4 the mass is the sum of the integrals over the two pieces:

\[m=\int_0^23\,dx+\int_2^5(1+x)\,dx=6+\Bigl[x+\frac{x^2}2\Bigr]_2^5=6+\Bigl(5+\frac{25}2\Bigr)-(2+2)=6+13.5=19.5\ \text{kg}.\]

The average density is \(19.5/5=3.9\) kg/m, between the least density, \(3\), and the greatest, \(1+5=6\).

Answer. \(m=19.5\) kg; \(\bar\rho=3.9\) kg/m.

Example 6.7.33: A rational density

A rod on \(0\le x\le2\) has density \(\rho(x)=\dfrac{12x}{(1+x^2)^2}\) kg/m. Find its mass.

Solution. Let \(u=1+x^2\), \(du=2x\,dx\), so \(12x\,dx=6\,du\); \(x=0\) gives \(u=1\) and \(x=2\) gives \(u=5\):

\[m=\int_1^56u^{-2}\,du=6\Bigl[-\frac1u\Bigr]_1^5=6\Bigl(1-\frac15\Bigr)=\frac{24}5=4.8\ \text{kg}.\]

Check. The density is \(0\) at \(x=0\), is largest at \(x=\frac1{\sqrt3}\) (about \(3.9\)), and is \(\frac{24}{25}\) at \(x=2\); a mass of \(4.8\) on a \(2\) m rod means an average density of \(2.4\), which is plausible.

Answer. \(m=\frac{24}5\) kg.

Group 2: Springs¶

Example 6.7.34: Finding \(k\) from the work

It takes \(18\) J of work to stretch a spring \(0.3\) m from equilibrium. Find \(k\), and the additional work to stretch it a further \(0.2\) m.

Solution. By Theorem 6.7.9 \(18=\frac k2\cdot0.3^2=0.045\,k\), so \(k=400\) N/m. The further stretch is from \(0.3\) to \(0.5\): \(W=\frac{400}2\,(0.5^2-0.3^2)=200\cdot0.16=32\) J. Check. The force goes from \(120\) N to \(200\) N, average \(160\) N, and \(160\cdot0.2=32\) J.

Answer. \(k=400\) N/m; \(32\) J.

Example 6.7.35: A nonlinear spring

A spring has restoring force \(F(x)=30x-5x^3\) N for \(0\le x\le1.5\) m. Find the work to stretch it from \(0\) to \(1.5\) m and compare it with a Hooke spring with the same \(k=30\).

Solution. By Theorem 6.7.8 \(W=\int_0^{1.5}(30x-5x^3)\,dx=\bigl[15x^2-\frac54x^4\bigr]_0^{1.5}=33.75-6.328125=27.421875=\frac{1755}{64}\) J. The cubic term makes the spring softer, so it needs less work than the Hooke spring, which needs \(15\cdot2.25=33.75=\frac{135}4\) J.

Answer. \(\frac{1755}{64}\approx27.4\) J, against \(33.75\) J for the linear spring.

Group 3: Lifting¶

Example 6.7.36: A cable with variable density

A \(20\) m cable hangs from a crane. Its density increases with height: \(\lambda(y)=2+0.1y\) kg/m at height \(y\) above its lower end. Find the work to lift it until the lower end reaches the crane.

Solution. The point at height \(y\) is lifted \(D(y)=20-y\). By Theorem 6.7.11, \(W=9.8\int_0^{20}(2+0.1y)(20-y)\,dy\). The product is \(40-2y+2y-0.1y^2=40-0.1y^2\), so

\[W=9.8\Bigl[40y-\frac{y^3}{30}\Bigr]_0^{20}=9.8\Bigl(800-\frac{800}3\Bigr)=9.8\cdot\frac{1600}3\approx5226.7\ \text{J}.\]

Check. The mass is \(\int_0^{20}(2+0.1y)\,dy=40+20=60\) kg. The bracket \(60\cdot9.8\cdot0\le W\le60\cdot9.8\cdot20\) is \(0\le W\le11\,760\), and \(W/(60g)\approx8.9\) m: the cable is heavier near the top, which is lifted less.

Answer. \(W\approx5227\) J.

Example 6.7.37: Winding up part of a chain

A chain of length \(10\) m and density \(4\) kg/m hangs from a platform. A winch pulls up \(6\) m of it onto the platform (the lower \(4\) m is still hanging at the end). Find the work.

Solution. Let \(s\) be the distance below the platform, \(0\le s\le10\). The point at \(s\le6\) is lifted all the way, \(D=s\); the points at \(s>6\) are lifted only \(6\) m. So \(D\) is piecewise defined (and \(1\)-Lipschitz), and

\[W=9.8\cdot4\Bigl[\int_0^6s\,ds+\int_6^{10}6\,ds\Bigr]=39.2\,(18+24)=1646.4\ \text{J}.\]

Check. The result is below the work for lifting the whole chain \(6\) m, \(40\cdot9.8\cdot6=2352\) J. By parts: the top \(6\) m has mass \(24\) kg and is lifted on average \(3\) m, giving \(705.6\) J, and the lower \(4\) m has mass \(16\) kg lifted \(6\) m, giving \(940.8\) J; \(705.6+940.8=1646.4\) J.

Figure. The chain of length \(10\) m. Points above \(s=6\) are lifted \(s\); the lower \(4\) m rise \(6\) m.

Answer. \(1646.4\) J.

Group 4: Pumping¶

Example 6.7.38: A triangular trough

A trough \(4\) m long has a cross section that is an isosceles triangle, \(1.5\) m wide at the top and \(2\) m deep (apex down). It is full of water, which is pumped over the top edge. Find the work.

Solution. Let \(y\) be the height above the apex. The cross-section width at \(y\) is \(\frac{1.5}2y\), so the horizontal layer is a rectangle \(4\) m by \(0.75y\): \(A(y)=3y\). The lift is \(D=2-y\):

\[W=9800\int_0^23y\,(2-y)\,dy=9800\cdot3\Bigl[y^2-\frac{y^3}3\Bigr]_0^2=9800\cdot3\cdot\frac43=39\,200\ \text{J}.\]

Check. The volume is \(\frac12\cdot1.5\cdot2\cdot4=6\) m\(^3\), the weight \(58\,800\) N, and \(W/\text{weight}=\frac23\) m: the water is lifted on average \(\frac23\) m, small because the trough is wide at the top.

Figure. Cross section of the trough (length \(4\) m). The layer at height \(y\) is a rectangle \(4\) m long and \(0.75y\) m wide, so \(A=3y\), and it is lifted \(D=2-y\) to the outlet at the top edge (\(y=2\)).

Answer. \(39\,200\) J.

Example 6.7.39: A horizontal tank with an outlet above it

A cylindrical tank of radius \(2\) m and length \(5\) m lies on its side and is full of water, which is pumped to a point \(1\) m above the top of the tank. Find the work.

Solution. Put the origin on the axis, so the water occupies \(-2\le y\le2\), the outlet is at \(h=3\) and \(A(y)=5\cdot2\sqrt{4-y^2}=10\sqrt{4-y^2}\), \(D=3-y\):

\[W=98\,000\int_{-2}^2\sqrt{4-y^2}\,(3-y)\,dy=98\,000\Bigl[3\int_{-2}^2\sqrt{4-y^2}\,dy-\int_{-2}^2y\sqrt{4-y^2}\,dy\Bigr].\]

The first integral is the area of a half-disk of radius \(2\), which is \(2\pi\). The second is \(0\): with \(u=4-y^2\) the limits become \(u=0\) and \(u=0\). So \(W=98\,000\cdot3\cdot2\pi=588\,000\pi\approx1.85\times10^6\) J. Check. The water has weight \(9800\cdot\pi\cdot4\cdot5=196\,000\pi\) N and every point is lifted \(3\) m on average (the water is symmetric about the axis, which is \(3\) m below the outlet): \(196\,000\pi\cdot3=588\,000\pi\) J.

Figure. Circular end (radius \(2\) m; the tank is \(5\) m long). The layer at height \(y\) has half-width \(\sqrt{4-y^2}\) (at \(y=1\): \(\sqrt3\)) and is lifted \(3-y\).

Answer. \(588\,000\pi\approx1.85\times10^6\) J.

Group 5: Force¶

Example 6.7.40: A wind load

Wind blows on a wall \(40\) m wide and \(30\) m high. The pressure at height \(y\) above the ground is \(p(y)=100+3y\) N/m\(^2\). Find the total force.

Solution. A horizontal strip at height \(y\) has area \(40\,dy\) and pressure \(p(y)\). The proof of Theorem 6.7.21 uses only (P2), (P3) and the fact that the pressure is Lipschitz, so \(F=\int_0^{30}p(y)\cdot40\,dy=40\bigl[100y+\frac32y^2\bigr]_0^{30}=40\,(3000+1350)=174\,000\) N. Check. The pressure runs from \(100\) to \(190\) N/m\(^2\); the average pressure is \(145\) and the area is \(1200\) m\(^2\): \(145\cdot1200=174\,000\) N.

Answer. \(174\,000\) N.

Example 6.7.41: A round window by two methods

A circular window of radius \(1\) m has its centre at depth \(5\) m. Find the force of the water (a) with the formula of Theorem 6.7.28, (b) with a bracket.

Solution. (a) \(F=9800\cdot5\cdot\pi\cdot1^2=49\,000\pi\approx153\,938\) N. (b) The depths run from \(4\) to \(6\) and the area is \(\pi\), so \(9800\cdot4\pi\le F\le9800\cdot6\pi\), so \(F\) lies in the bracket and the bracket is centred on it: \(123\,150\le F\le184\,726\) (rounded).

Figure. The window: radius \(1\) m, centre at depth \(5\) m, so the depths run from \(4\) to \(6\).

Answer. \(49\,000\pi\approx1.54\times10^5\) N.

Group 6: Comparing¶

Example 6.7.42: Top half and bottom half of a dam

A rectangular dam \(10\) m high and \(20\) m wide holds water level with its top. Compare the force on its top half with the force on its bottom half.

Solution. With depth \(d\), \(w=20\): top half \(F_1=9800\cdot20\int_0^5d\,\mathrm dd=196\,000\cdot12.5=2\,450\,000\) N; bottom half \(F_2=196\,000\int_5^{10}d\,\mathrm dd=196\,000\cdot37.5=7\,350\,000\) N. The ratio is \(F_2/F_1=3\). Discussion. Both halves have the same area, but the average depth of the bottom half is \(7.5\) m against \(2.5\) m for the top, a ratio of \(3\).

Figure. The dam (\(20\) m wide, \(10\) m high) cut at depth \(5\) m.

Answer. \(2.45\times10^6\) N and \(7.35\times10^6\) N.

Example 6.7.43: Buoyancy of a cube

A cube of side \(2\) m is fully submerged in water with its top face horizontal at depth \(3\) m. Find the net vertical force of the water on it.

Solution. The top face has area \(4\) and depth \(3\): \(F_{\text{down}}=9800\cdot3\cdot4=117\,600\) N. The bottom face is at depth \(5\): \(F_{\text{up}}=9800\cdot5\cdot4=196\,000\) N. The forces on the side faces are horizontal and cancel in pairs. The net force is \(196\,000-117\,600=78\,400\) N upward, which equals \(\rho gV=9800\cdot8\), the weight of the displaced water.

Figure. The cube. The side forces cancel; the net force is \(196\,000-117\,600=78\,400\) N up.

Answer. \(78\,400\) N upward.

Example 6.7.44: Work to lift a hanging chain

Prove that the work to pull a hanging uniform chain of length \(L\) and mass \(M\) up to its support is \(\frac12MgL\), that is, the work to lift the whole weight through half its length.

Proof. Let \(s\) be the distance below the support, \(0\le s\le L\). The mass per unit length is \(\lambda=M/L\) and the point at \(s\) is lifted \(D=s\). By Theorem 6.7.11, \(W=g\int_0^L\frac ML\,s\,ds=\frac{gM}{L}\cdot\frac{L^2}2=\frac12MgL\). 0◻

Example 6.7.45: Work of a linear force

Prove that if \(F(x)=p+qx\) is the force on \(a\le x\le b\), the work is \(\frac{F(a)+F(b)}2\,(b-a)\): the average of the end forces times the distance.

Proof. \(W=\int_a^b(p+qx)\,dx=p(b-a)+\frac q2(b^2-a^2)=(b-a)\bigl[p+\frac q2(a+b)\bigr]\). And \(\frac{F(a)+F(b)}2=\frac{2p+q(a+b)}2=p+\frac q2(a+b)\). Thus \(W=\frac{F(a)+F(b)}2(b-a)\). 0◻

Answers and discussion¶

Knowledge Check 6.7.1. (a) \(m=\int_0^2(3+x)\,dx=6+2=8\) kg. (b) \(\bar\rho=8/2=4\) kg/m and \(3+x=4\) at \(x=1\). (c) The density lies between \(3\) and \(5\), so \(6\le m\le10\); \(m=8\) is inside.

Knowledge Check 6.7.2. (a) \(\frac{200}2(0.3^2-0.1^2)=100\cdot0.08=8\) J. (b) \(100\cdot0.2^2=4\) J. (c) No: the work from \(0\) to \(0.2\) is \(4\) J. The spring pulls back harder when already stretched, so the same extra length costs more.

Knowledge Check 6.7.3. (a) \(W=9800\int_0^5(2y+1)(8-y)\,dy\). (b) With \(s=8-y\) (so \(y=5\) is \(s=3\) and \(y=0\) is \(s=8\)) and \(A=2(8-s)+1=17-2s\), \(W=9800\int_3^8(17-2s)\,s\,ds\). Both give the same number.
