11.2 Properties of Power Series¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

A Taylor polynomial stops at some degree. What if it never stops? A polynomial with infinitely many terms is a power series: a function, but only at those \(x\) where the series converges. This section asks for which \(x\) it converges (the Ratio and Root Tests from Chapter 10 do most of the work), and what we can build from one power series we know (add, substitute, differentiate, integrate). The geometric series will generate almost everything. Which power series represent which functions is the subject of a later section on Taylor series.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • find the radius and the interval of convergence of a power series, using the Ratio or Root Test for the interior and the tests of Chapter 10 for the endpoints;

  • state the three ways a power series can converge, and explain why the radius exists;

  • build new power series from a known one by sums, powers of \(x\), substitution, differentiation and integration, find where they converge, and use them to evaluate numerical series.

From polynomials to power series¶

Definition 11.2.1: Power series

A power series centered at \(a\) is a series

\[\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k(x-a)^k=c_0+c_1(x-a)+c_2(x-a)^2+\cdots,\]

where \(a\) and the coefficients \(c_k\) are real numbers and \(x\) is a variable (with \((x-a)^0=1\) even for \(x=a\)). For each fixed \(x\) it is an ordinary series of Chapter 10. It defines a function \(f(x)=\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) on the set of \(x\) where it converges, its convergence set.

At \(x=a\) every term except \(c_0\) is \(0\), so the convergence set always contains \(a\). The partial sums \(c_0+c_1(x-a)+\cdots+c_n(x-a)^n\) are polynomials in \(x-a\), and when \(c_k=\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\) they are exactly the Taylor polynomials of the previous section.

The first example is the geometric series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k\), a power series with \(a=0\) and every \(c_k=1\). By Theorem 10.3.3 it converges to \(\frac1{1-x}\) exactly when \(\abs x<1\). Its partial sums \(1+x+\cdots+x^n\) are the Taylor polynomials \(p_n\) of \(\frac1{1-x}\) in Example 11.1.17, whose remainder \(R_n(x)=\frac{x^{n+1}}{1-x}\) (Definition 11.1.10) tends to \(0\) exactly when \(\abs x<1\). So the series converges at \(x\) when the polynomials settle down, and it converges to \(f(x)\) when the remainder tends to \(0\).

Pause and think 11.2.1: Which points converge?

In Example 11.1.17 the polynomials \(p_n(x)\) approached \(f(x)=\frac1{1-x}\) at \(x=\frac12\) but not at \(x=-1\) or \(x=2\). Which of the three numbers lie in the convergence set of \(\sum x^k\)? What happens to the remainder at the other two?

Answer and discussion

Only \(x=\frac12\) lies in \(\abs x<1\). At \(x=-1\) the partial sums are \(1,0,1,0,\dots\) and at \(x=2\) they are \(2^{n+1}-1\to\infty\): both diverge. The remainder \(R_n(x)=\frac{x^{n+1}}{1-x}\) tends to \(0\) if and only if \(\abs x<1\): at \(x=-1\) it has absolute value \(\frac12\) for every \(n\), and at \(x=2\) it is \(-2^{n+1}\). Since \(p_n(x)=f(x)-R_n(x)\), convergence to \(f(x)\) means \(R_n(x)\to0\).

Knowledge Check 11.2.1: Center and coefficients

Write \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(3x-6)^k}{k+1}\) in the form \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\). What are \(a\) and \(c_k\)?

Where does a power series converge?¶

Theorem 11.2.2: Convergence of power series

A power series \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k(x-a)^k\) converges in exactly one of three ways.

  1. It converges absolutely for every \(x\).

  2. There is a number \(R>0\) such that it converges absolutely for \(\abs{x-a}<R\) and diverges for \(\abs{x-a}>R\).

  3. It converges only at \(x=a\).

The proof is in Appendix F (Theorems F.1 and F.2); it needs the least upper bound property of Appendix A, because \(R\) is a least upper bound. The idea: if the series converges at \(x_0\), its terms \(c_k(x_0-a)^k\) tend to \(0\), so they are bounded, and then \(\abs{c_k(x-a)^k}\) is below a geometric series for every \(x\) closer to \(a\) than \(x_0\).

Definition 11.2.3: Radius and interval of convergence

In Theorem 11.2.2 the number \(R\) is the radius of convergence: \(R=\infty\) in case 1, \(R=0\) in case 3. The set of \(x\) where the series converges is its interval of convergence. It contains \((a-R,a+R)\) and possibly one or both endpoints \(a\pm R\) (and is \(\R\) if \(R=\infty\) and \(\{a\}\) if \(R=0\)).

The theorem says nothing about the endpoints. For example, \((2,6)\), \([2,6)\), \((2,6]\) and \([2,6]\) all have center \(4\) and radius \(2\).

Knowledge Check 11.2.2: What the theorem tells us

A power series \(\sum c_k(x-4)^k\) converges at \(x=7\) and diverges at \(x=-1\). What can you say about \(R\)? For each of \(x=2\), \(8\), \(10\), \(-2\), decide whether the series converges, diverges, or this cannot be decided.

How do we find \(R\)? Theorem 11.2.2 is about absolute convergence, and the Ratio and Root Tests (Theorems 10.7.1 and 10.7.5) are exactly tests for absolute convergence.

Theorem 11.2.4: Radius from the Ratio or Root Test

Let \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) be a power series, and let \(L\) be a number \(\ge0\) or \(\infty\).

  1. If every \(c_k\ne0\) and \(\abs{c_{k+1}/c_k}\to L\), then \(R=1/L\).

  2. If \(\abs{c_k}^{1/k}\to L\), then \(R=1/L\).

Here \(1/0=\infty\) and \(1/\infty=0\).

Proof. Fix \(x\ne a\) and put \(a_k=c_k(x-a)^k\). In case 1 all \(a_k\ne0\) and \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\abs{c_{k+1}/c_k}\,\abs{x-a}\to L\abs{x-a}\); in case 2, \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\abs{c_k}^{1/k}\abs{x-a}\to L\abs{x-a}\). By the Ratio Test (case 1) or the Root Test (case 2), the series converges absolutely if \(L\abs{x-a}<1\) and diverges if \(L\abs{x-a}>1\) (including \(L=\infty\)). If \(L=0\) then \(L\abs{x-a}=0<1\) for every \(x\), so the series converges for all \(x\): \(R=\infty\). If \(0<L<\infty\) it converges absolutely for \(\abs{x-a}<\frac1L\) and diverges for \(\abs{x-a}>\frac1L\), so \(R=\frac1L\). If \(L=\infty\) it diverges for every \(x\ne a\), so \(R=0\). ◻ ∎

Gaps in the exponents

If some \(c_k=0\) (a series such as \(\sum x^{2k}/4^k\) has no odd powers), the quotient \(c_{k+1}/c_k\) is undefined or \(0\) and \(\abs{c_k}^{1/k}\) may have no limit, so Theorem 11.2.4 does not apply directly. Apply the Ratio or Root Test to the terms \(a_k\) of the series for fixed \(x\) instead (Example 11.2.10).

Procedure 11.2.5: Radius and interval of convergence

  1. Interior. Apply the Ratio Test (factorials, products of consecutive terms) or the Root Test (\(k\)-th powers) to the terms. The test gives \(r=\abs{x-a}/R\); solve \(r<1\) for the open interval \((a-R,a+R)\). For \(\abs{x-a}>R\), \(r>1\) and the series diverges.

  2. Endpoints. At \(x=a\pm R\) both tests give \(r=1\) and say nothing. Substitute each endpoint and test the resulting numerical series with the tests of Chapter 10: the Divergence Test (Theorem 10.4.1), \(p\)-series and the Integral Test (Theorems 10.4.11, 10.4.6), comparison (Theorems 10.5.1, 10.5.5), the Alternating Series Test (Theorem 10.6.2).

  3. Conclude with the interval, including exactly the endpoints that converge.

Example 11.2.6: Factorials: radius \(\infty\) and radius \(0\)

Find the radius and interval of convergence of (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{3^kx^k}{k!}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{k!\,(x+1)^k}{10^k}\).

Solution. (a) The center is \(0\) and \(c_k=\frac{3^k}{k!}\ne0\). Then

\[\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac{3^{k+1}\,k!}{(k+1)!\,3^k}=\frac3{k+1}\to0 ,\]

so \(L=0\) and \(R=\infty\) (Theorem 11.2.4): the interval is \((-\infty,\infty)\). (b) The center is \(-1\) and \(c_k=\frac{k!}{10^k}\):

\[\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac{(k+1)!\,10^k}{10^{k+1}\,k!}=\frac{k+1}{10}\to\infty ,\]

so \(L=\infty\) and \(R=0\). The series converges only at the center \(x=-1\), where it equals \(c_0=1\). In (a) the factorial in the denominator wins against any power; in (b) it is in the numerator and wins the other way.

Example 11.2.7: The Root Test, and endpoints that diverge

Find the radius and interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac k{3k+1}\right)^k(x+2)^k\).

Solution. The \(k\)-th power suggests the Root Test. With \(a_k\) the \(k\)-th term, \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac k{3k+1}\abs{x+2}\to\frac{\abs{x+2}}3\). So the series converges absolutely when \(\abs{x+2}<3\), that is \(-5<x<1\), and diverges when \(\abs{x+2}>3\): \(R=3\). At the endpoints the Root Test gives \(r=1\), so we substitute. At \(x=1\) the terms are \(\left(\frac{3k}{3k+1}\right)^k=\left(1+\frac1{3k}\right)^{-k}\to e^{-1/3}\approx0.7165\ne0\) (because \(\left(1+\frac1{3k}\right)^{3k}\to e\)); at \(x=-5\) the terms are \((-1)^k\) times these, so they do not tend to \(0\) either. Both endpoints diverge by the Divergence Test (Theorem 10.4.1), and the interval is \((-5,1)\).

Example 11.2.8: One endpoint converges conditionally

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(x-3)^k}{k\,2^k}\).

Solution. The center is \(3\) and \(c_k=\frac1{k2^k}\), so \(\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac k{2(k+1)}\to\frac12\). Hence \(R=2\) and the series converges absolutely on \((1,5)\). At \(x=5\) the series is \(\sum\frac1k\), the harmonic series (Theorem 10.4.4): divergent. At \(x=1\) it is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k\), which converges by the Alternating Series Test (Theorem 10.6.2) (the terms \(\frac1k\) decrease to \(0\)), but not absolutely. The interval of convergence is \([1,5)\).

Example 11.2.9: Both endpoints converge

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(x+1)^k}{k^2\,2^k}\).

Solution. Center \(-1\), \(c_k=\frac1{k^22^k}\), \(\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac{k^2}{2(k+1)^2}\to\frac12\), so \(R=2\) and the open interval is \((-3,1)\). At \(x=1\) the series is \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\), a convergent \(p\)-series (Theorem 10.4.11). At \(x=-3\) it is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{k^2}\), which converges absolutely, because \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges. So the interval is \([-3,1]\).

Pause and think 11.2.2: Why the Ratio Test cannot settle the endpoints

In Examples 11.2.8 and 11.2.9 the Ratio Test gave \(r=\frac{\abs{x-3}}2\) and \(r=\frac{\abs{x+1}}2\). What is \(r\) at each endpoint? Why does this not contradict the fact that one of the series (\(\sum\frac1k\)) diverges and the other (\(\sum\frac1{k^2}\)) converges?

Answer and discussion

At an endpoint \(\abs{x-a}=R\), so \(r=\frac{\abs{x-a}}R=1\) (here \(\frac22=1\)). The Ratio Test (Theorem 10.7.1) is inconclusive when \(r=1\): both \(\sum\frac1k\) and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) have \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\to1\) and one diverges while the other converges. So endpoints need other tests (harmonic series, \(p\)-series, Alternating Series Test).

Example 11.2.10: A series with gaps

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^{2k+1}}{4^k(2k+1)}\).

Solution. Only odd powers of \(x\) occur, so we cannot use \(c_{k+1}/c_k\) (Warning above). Apply the Ratio Test to the terms \(a_k=\frac{x^{2k+1}}{4^k(2k+1)}\):

\[\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\frac{x^2}4\cdot\frac{2k+1}{2k+3}\to\frac{x^2}4 .\]

The series converges absolutely when \(\frac{x^2}4<1\), that is \(\abs x<2\), and diverges when \(\abs x>2\): \(R=2\). At \(x=2\) the terms are \(\frac{2^{2k+1}}{4^k(2k+1)}=\frac2{2k+1}\); since \(\frac{2/(2k+1)}{1/k}\to1\), the Limit Comparison Test (Theorem 10.5.5) with the harmonic series shows divergence. At \(x=-2\) every term changes sign, so the series is \(-1\) times the divergent one. The interval is \((-2,2)\).

Combining power series¶

Theorem 11.2.11: Combining power series

Let \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) and \(\sum d_k(x-a)^k\) converge to \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) for all \(x\) in a set \(I\).

  1. Sum and difference. \(\sum(c_k\pm d_k)(x-a)^k\) converges to \(f(x)\pm g(x)\) for all \(x\) in \(I\). If the two series have different radii, the combined series has the smaller radius.

  2. Multiplying by a power. For an integer \(m\ge0\), \(\sum c_k(x-a)^{k+m}\) converges at exactly the same points \(x\ne a\) as \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\), and then to \((x-a)^mf(x)\). In particular it has the same radius.

  3. Substitution. Suppose \(\sum c_kt^k\) converges to \(F(t)\) exactly for \(t\) in a set \(J\). For any expression \(u(x)\) (for example \(bx^m\) with \(b\ne0\) and \(m\ge1\) an integer, or \(x-s\)), \(\sum c_k\,u(x)^k\) converges to \(F(u(x))\) exactly for those \(x\) with \(u(x)\) in \(J\).

Proof. (1) The partial sums of the new series are the sum or difference of the partial sums of the two series, so they converge to \(f\pm g\) at every \(x\) in \(I\) (Theorem 10.3.11). Suppose the radii are \(R_1<R_2\). Choose \(x\) with \(R_1<\abs{x-a}<R_2\): the series \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) diverges and \(\sum d_k(x-a)^k\) converges. If \(\sum(c_k+d_k)(x-a)^k\) converged, then so would \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\), the difference of two convergent series. So the new series diverges for \(\abs{x-a}>R_1\) and converges for \(\abs{x-a}<R_1\): its radius is \(R_1\). (When the radii are equal, the combined radius is at least that number and can be larger: take \(d_k=-c_k\).)

(2) \(\sum_{k=0}^nc_k(x-a)^{k+m}=(x-a)^m\sum_{k=0}^nc_k(x-a)^k\) for every \(n\). For \(x\ne a\) the factor \((x-a)^m\ne0\), so the left side converges if and only if the sum on the right does, and the limit is \((x-a)^mf(x)\).

(3) For a given \(x\), \(\sum c_k\,u(x)^k\) is the series \(\sum c_kt^k\) at the single point \(t=u(x)\). It converges to \(F(t)\) exactly when \(t\in J\). ◻ ∎

For \(\sum c_kx^k\) with radius \(R\), part 3 with \(u(x)=bx^m\) gives radius \((R/\abs b)^{1/m}\) and part 2 gives radius \(R\).

Example 11.2.12: Building series from \(\frac1{1-x}\)

Use \(\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k\) \((\abs x<1)\) to find power series, centered at \(0\), and the interval of convergence for (a) \(\dfrac{6x^3}{1-x}\), (b) \(\dfrac1{1-5x}\), (c) \(\dfrac1{9+x^2}\).

Solution. (a) By Theorem 11.2.11(2) with \(m=3\) and the constant factor \(6\): \(\frac{6x^3}{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty6x^{k+3}\), same interval \(\abs x<1\). (b) Substitute \(u=5x\): \(\sum(5x)^k\) converges exactly when \(\abs{5x}<1\), so the interval is \(\abs x<\frac15\). (c) Write \(\frac1{9+x^2}=\frac19\cdot\frac1{1-(-x^2/9)}\) and substitute \(u=-\frac{x^2}9\):

\[\frac1{9+x^2}=\frac19\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(-\frac{x^2}9\right)^k=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^{2k}}{9^{k+1}},\qquad\abs{\tfrac{x^2}9}<1\iff\abs x<3 .\]

Example 11.2.13: One function, two centers

Find power series for \(f(x)=\dfrac1{3-x}\) centered at \(0\) and at \(1\), with their intervals of convergence. Compare them at \(x=-2.5\).

Solution. At \(0\): \(\frac1{3-x}=\frac13\cdot\frac1{1-x/3}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^k}{3^{k+1}}\) for \(\abs{x/3}<1\), that is \(\abs x<3\). At \(1\): write \(3-x=2-(x-1)\), so \(\frac1{3-x}=\frac12\cdot\frac1{1-(x-1)/2}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(x-1)^k}{2^{k+1}}\) for \(\abs{x-1}<2\). Each radius is the distance from the center to \(x=3\), where \(f\) is not defined. At \(x=-2.5\), \(f=\frac2{11}\). The center-\(0\) series converges there (\(\abs{-2.5}<3\)): its sum is \(\frac13\cdot\frac1{1+5/6}=\frac2{11}\). The center-\(1\) series diverges there (\(\abs{-3.5}>2\)): its terms do not tend to \(0\). Same function, but each series represents it only where it converges.

Products. Multiplying two power series “as if they were polynomials” gives the coefficient \(\sum_{j=0}^nc_jd_{n-j}\) of \(x^n\); for \(\frac1{1-x}\cdot\frac1{1-x}\) it is \(n+1\), as in Example 11.2.15 below. That this series converges to \(f(x)g(x)\) inside both intervals is true; the proof (it uses rearrangement, compare Theorem D.6) is not given here.

Differentiating and integrating power series¶

Polynomials can be differentiated and integrated term by term. Power series can too, inside the interval of convergence.

Theorem 11.2.14: Differentiating and integrating power series

Let \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) have radius of convergence \(R>0\) and let \(f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k(x-a)^k\) for \(\abs{x-a}<R\).

  1. \(f\) is differentiable (so continuous) on \((a-R,a+R)\) and \(f'(x)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty kc_k(x-a)^{k-1}\).

  2. \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx=C+\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{c_k}{k+1}(x-a)^{k+1}\) on \((a-R,a+R)\).

  3. Both new series have the same radius \(R\). At the endpoints \(a\pm R\) they may converge when the original does not, or diverge when it converges.

The proof is not given in the notes. It takes more room than a lecture allows. Here is a sketch; the complete argument, which needs nothing beyond the Mean Value Theorem, is in Appendix F (Theorems F.3, F.4 and F.5).

  1. The new series converge. Fix \(x\) with \(\abs{x-a}<R\) and \(s\) with \(\abs{x-a}<s<R\). The terms \(c_ks^k\) tend to \(0\), so \(\abs{c_k}s^k\le B\), and \(k\abs{c_k}\abs{x-a}^{k-1}\le\frac Bs\,k\,t^{k-1}\) with \(t=\frac{\abs{x-a}}s<1\); \(\sum kt^{k-1}\) converges (Ratio Test). The integrated series is handled the same way.

  2. It converges to \(f'\). With \(u=x-a\) and \(v=u+h\), two uses of the Mean Value Theorem give \(\left|\frac{v^k-u^k}h-ku^{k-1}\right|\le k(k-1)\rho^{k-2}\abs h\) when \(\abs u,\abs v\le\rho<R\). Summing against \(\abs{c_k}\), the difference quotient of \(f\) differs from the differentiated series by at most \(C\abs h\to0\).

  3. Integration. The integrated series has the original as its differentiated series, so by step 2 it is an antiderivative of \(f\).

The theorem claims nothing at the endpoints, not even that a new series converging there converges to \(f'\) or to the integral.

Example 11.2.15: Differentiating a geometric series to sum numerical series

Starting from \(\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k\) \((\abs x<1)\): (a) find a series for \(\frac1{(1-x)^2}\); (b) find \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac k{3^k}\); (c) find \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^2}{2^k}\).

Solution. (a) Differentiate term by term (Theorem 11.2.14), \(\frac d{dx}\frac1{1-x}=\frac1{(1-x)^2}\):

\[\frac1{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{k=1}^\infty kx^{k-1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(k+1)x^k=1+2x+3x^2+\cdots,\qquad\abs x<1 .\]

The radius is still \(1\); at \(x=\pm1\) the terms \((k+1)(\pm1)^k\) do not tend to \(0\). (b) Multiply by \(x\) (Theorem 11.2.11(2)): \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty kx^k=\frac x{(1-x)^2}\) for \(\abs x<1\). At \(x=\frac13\): \(\sum\frac k{3^k}=\frac{1/3}{(2/3)^2}=\frac34\). (c) Differentiate (a) again, \(\frac2{(1-x)^3}=\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)x^{k-2}\), and multiply by \(x^2\): \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)x^k=\frac{2x^2}{(1-x)^3}\). At \(x=\frac12\) this is \(\frac{2/4}{1/8}=4\), and (b) at \(x=\frac12\) gives \(\sum\frac k{2^k}=\frac{1/2}{1/4}=2\). Since \(k^2=k(k-1)+k\), \(\sum\frac{k^2}{2^k}=4+2=6\).

Example 11.2.16: Integrating a geometric series: a logarithm

Find a power series for \(\ln(1+2x)\) centered at \(0\) and its interval of convergence. Use it to estimate \(\ln1.2\).

Solution. \(\frac d{dx}\ln(1+2x)=\frac2{1+2x}=2\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-2x)^k\) for \(\abs{2x}<1\) (substitution \(u=-2x\)). Integrate term by term (Theorem 11.2.14); at \(x=0\) both sides are \(0\), so the constant is \(C=0\):

\[\ln(1+2x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{2(-2)^kx^{k+1}}{k+1}=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{k+1}(2x)^k}k=2x-2x^2+\tfrac83x^3-\cdots,\qquad\abs x<\tfrac12 .\]

The radius is \(\frac12\). Endpoints: at \(x=\frac12\) the series is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}k\), the alternating harmonic series, which converges; at \(x=-\frac12\) it is \(-\sum\frac1k\), which diverges. The interval is \((-\frac12,\frac12]\). The theorem guarantees equality with \(\ln(1+2x)\) only for \(\abs x<\frac12\) (whether the series equals \(\ln2\) at \(x=\frac12\) is a question for the section on Taylor series). To estimate \(\ln1.2\) put \(x=0.1\): the series is \(0.2-0.02+0.002667-0.0004+\cdots\), alternating with decreasing terms, so by Theorem 10.6.5 the error after four terms is at most the next term \(\frac{0.2^5}5=0.000064\). The four terms sum to \(0.182267\), and \(\ln1.2=0.182322\) (actual error \(5.5\times10^{-5}\)).

Knowledge Check 11.2.3: Differentiate and integrate

Let \(f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{x^k}k\) for \(\abs x<1\). Find \(f'(x)\) as a power series and as a function, and use it to identify \(f(x)\). What is \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k\,3^k}\)?

Pause and think 11.2.3: Endpoints change

Consider \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{x^k}{k^2}\), its derivative \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{x^{k-1}}k\) and the derivative of that. Find the radius and the interval of convergence of each.

Answer and discussion

\(\sum\frac{x^k}{k^2}\): \(\abs{c_{k+1}/c_k}=\frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2}\to1\), \(R=1\), and at \(x=\pm1\) it converges (\(p\)-series with \(p=2\), absolutely): interval \([-1,1]\). Derivative \(\sum\frac{x^{k-1}}k\): \(R=1\); at \(x=1\) it is the harmonic series (diverges), at \(x=-1\) it is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}k\) (converges): interval \([-1,1)\). Second derivative \(\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{(k-1)x^{k-2}}k\): \(R=1\), and at \(x=\pm1\) the terms have absolute value \(\frac{k-1}k\to1\ne0\): interval \((-1,1)\). The radius is unchanged, but each differentiation can lose an endpoint (and each integration gain one).

Example 11.2.17: Series that are not power series

Find where each series converges, and its sum: (a) \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty e^{-kx}\), (b) \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\left(\frac{x^2-1}3\right)^k\).

Solution. (a) A geometric series with ratio \(e^{-x}\). It converges exactly when \(e^{-x}<1\), that is \(x>0\), to \(\frac1{1-e^{-x}}\) (at \(x=\ln2\): \(\sum2^{-k}=2\)). This is not a power series (no \((x-a)^k\)), and its convergence set \((0,\infty)\) is not symmetric about any center. (b) It is \(\sum u^k\) with \(u=\frac{x^2-1}3\) (substitution, Theorem 11.2.11(3)), so it converges exactly when \(\abs u<1\), that is \(-2<x^2<4\), so \(\abs x<2\), with sum \(\frac1{1-u}=\frac3{4-x^2}\). Here the convergence set is an interval around \(0\), but the series is a power series in \(x^2-1\), not in \(x\).

Group 1: Radius and interval by the Ratio Test¶

Example 11.2.18: A polynomial factor

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k\,(x-3)^k}{4^k}\).

Solution. The center is \(3\) and \(c_k=\frac k{4^k}\ne0\). Then \(\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac{k+1}{4k}\to\frac14\), so \(R=4\) and the series converges absolutely on \((-1,7)\). At \(x=7\) the terms are \(k\cdot1^k=k\), and at \(x=-1\) they are \(k(-1)^k\). In both cases \(\abs{a_k}=k\not\to0\), so the Divergence Test shows divergence. The interval is \((-1,7)\).

Example 11.2.19: Factorials in a quotient

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(k!)^2}{(2k)!}\,x^k\).

Solution. Here \(c_k=\frac{(k!)^2}{(2k)!}\) and, cancelling factorials (\((k+1)!=(k+1)k!\) and \((2k+2)!=(2k+2)(2k+1)(2k)!\)),

\[\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}=\frac{(k+1)^2}{(2k+2)(2k+1)}=\frac{k+1}{2(2k+1)}\to\frac14 ,\]

so \(R=4\). At \(x=\pm4\) the Ratio Test is inconclusive (\(r=1\)), so look at \(t_k=4^kc_k=\abs{a_k}\) directly: \(\frac{t_{k+1}}{t_k}=4\cdot\frac{k+1}{2(2k+1)}=\frac{2k+2}{2k+1}>1\). The numbers \(t_k\) are increasing, starting at \(t_1=\frac{4\cdot1}2=2\), so they cannot tend to \(0\). Both endpoints diverge by the Divergence Test, and the interval is \((-4,4)\).

Group 2: Radius and interval by the Root Test¶

Example 11.2.20: A rational base

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac{2k+3}{5k+1}\right)^k(x-1)^k\).

Solution. The \(k\)-th root of the term is \(\frac{2k+3}{5k+1}\abs{x-1}\to\frac25\abs{x-1}\). This is \(<1\) for \(\abs{x-1}<\frac52\), so \(R=\frac52\) and the open interval is \(\left(-\frac32,\frac72\right)\). At an endpoint \(\abs{x-1}=\frac52\) and \(\abs{a_k}=\left(\frac52\cdot\frac{2k+3}{5k+1}\right)^k=\left(\frac{10k+15}{10k+2}\right)^k=\left(1+\frac{13}{10k+2}\right)^k\). Since \(\left(1+\frac{13}m\right)^m\to e^{13}\) with \(m=10k+2\), and the exponent \(\frac k{10k+2}\to\frac1{10}\), we get \(\abs{a_k}\to e^{13/10}\ne0\). Divergence at both endpoints, interval \(\left(-\frac32,\frac72\right)\).

Example 11.2.21: The base \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\)

Find the radius and interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(1+\frac1k\right)^{k^2}x^k\).

Solution. \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\abs x\to e\abs x\) (the definition of \(e\)), so \(R=\frac1e\). The endpoints need care, since \(\abs{a_k}=\left(\frac{(1+1/k)^k}e\right)^k\) is a number close to \(1\) raised to the power \(k\). Taking logarithms, \(\ln\abs{a_k}=k\left(k\ln\left(1+\tfrac1k\right)-1\right)\). Taylor’s Theorem (Theorem 11.1.11) for \(\ln(1+u)\) with \(n=2\) gives \(\ln(1+u)=u-\frac{u^2}2+\frac{u^3}{3(1+c)^3}\) for some \(c\) between \(0\) and \(u\). With \(u=\frac1k\): \(k\ln(1+\frac1k)-1=-\frac1{2k}+\frac1{3k^2(1+c)^3}\) and

\[\ln\abs{a_k}=-\frac12+\frac1{3k(1+c)^3}\longrightarrow-\frac12 ,\]

because \(0<\frac1{3k(1+c)^3}<\frac1{3k}\). So \(\abs{a_k}\to e^{-1/2}\ne0\) at \(x=\pm\frac1e\), and both endpoints diverge. The interval is \(\left(-\frac1e,\frac1e\right)\).

Group 3: Endpoints¶

Example 11.2.22: An interval open on the left

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k(x-4)^k}{k\,3^k}\).

Solution. \(\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac k{3(k+1)}\to\frac13\), so \(R=3\) and the open interval is \((1,7)\). At \(x=7\): \(\frac{(-1)^k3^k}{k3^k}=\frac{(-1)^k}k\), which converges (Alternating Series Test). At \(x=1\): \((-1)^k(-3)^k=3^k\), so the series is \(\sum\frac1k\), divergent. The interval is \((1,7]\): the sign \((-1)^k\) in the coefficients moves the convergent endpoint to the right.

Example 11.2.23: An endpoint decided by the Integral Test

Find the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{(x+1)^k}{2^k\,k\ln k}\).

Solution. \(\abs{\frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k}}=\frac12\cdot\frac{k\ln k}{(k+1)\ln(k+1)}\to\frac12\), so \(R=2\) and the open interval is \((-3,1)\). At \(x=1\) the series is \(\sum_{k\ge2}\frac1{k\ln k}\). The function \(\frac1{x\ln x}\) is positive and decreasing for \(x\ge2\) and \(\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln x}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left[\ln\ln x\right]_2^b=\infty\), so the series diverges by the Integral Test (Theorem 10.4.6). At \(x=-3\) the series is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{k\ln k}\): the terms \(\frac1{k\ln k}\) decrease to \(0\), so it converges by the Alternating Series Test. The interval is \([-3,1)\).

Group 4: Combining and substituting¶

Example 11.2.24: Shifting and scaling

Find power series centered at \(0\), with the interval of convergence, for (a) \(\dfrac{x^2}{1+3x}\) and (b) \(\dfrac4{2-x}\).

Solution. (a) \(\frac1{1+3x}=\sum(-3x)^k\) for \(\abs{3x}<1\) (substitution \(u=-3x\)), and multiplying by \(x^2\) does not change the interval: \(\frac{x^2}{1+3x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-3)^kx^{k+2}\), \(\abs x<\frac13\). (b) \(\frac4{2-x}=2\cdot\frac1{1-x/2}=2\sum\left(\frac x2\right)^k=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^k}{2^{k-1}}\), valid for \(\abs{x/2}<1\), that is \(\abs x<2\).

Example 11.2.25: Partial fractions

Find the power series of \(f(x)=\dfrac1{(1-x)(1+2x)}\) centered at \(0\) and its interval of convergence.

Solution. Partial fractions: \(\frac1{(1-x)(1+2x)}=\frac13\left(\frac1{1-x}+\frac2{1+2x}\right)\) (check: \(\frac1{1-x}+\frac2{1+2x}=\frac{(1+2x)+2(1-x)}{(1-x)(1+2x)}=\frac3{(1-x)(1+2x)}\)). The two series are \(\sum x^k\) (radius \(1\)) and \(2\sum(-2x)^k\) (radius \(\frac12\)). By Theorem 11.2.11(1), since the radii differ the sum has the smaller radius, \(\frac12\), and

\[f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{1+2(-2)^k}3\,x^k=1-x+3x^2-5x^3+11x^4-\cdots,\qquad\abs x<\tfrac12 .\]

Group 5: Differentiating and integrating¶

Example 11.2.26: A derivative series

Find a power series for \(\dfrac1{(3+x)^2}\) centered at \(0\), and its radius.

Solution. First \(\frac1{3+x}=\frac13\cdot\frac1{1+x/3}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^k}{3^{k+1}}\) for \(\abs x<3\). Since \(\frac d{dx}\frac1{3+x}=-\frac1{(3+x)^2}\), differentiating term by term gives \(-\frac1{(3+x)^2}=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kk\,x^{k-1}}{3^{k+1}}\), so (change the sign and put \(j=k-1\))

\[\frac1{(3+x)^2}=\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^j(j+1)\,x^j}{3^{j+2}},\qquad\abs x<3 .\]

The radius stays \(3\). (At \(x=\pm3\) the terms have absolute value \(\frac{j+1}9\not\to0\), so the new series diverges at both endpoints.)

Example 11.2.27: A definite integral with an error bound

Use a power series to estimate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dt}{1+t^4}\) with error below \(10^{-6}\).

Solution. \(\frac1{1+t^4}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-1)^kt^{4k}\) for \(\abs t<1\) (substitution \(u=-t^4\)). Integrating from \(0\) to \(x\) term by term (Theorem 11.2.14; the constant is \(0\) because the integral from \(0\) to \(0\) is \(0\)), for \(\abs x<1\):

\[\int_0^x\frac{dt}{1+t^4}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^{4k+1}}{4k+1}=x-\frac{x^5}5+\frac{x^9}9-\cdots .\]

At \(x=\frac12\) this is an alternating series with decreasing terms \(\frac{(1/2)^{4k+1}}{4k+1}\): \(\frac12,\ \frac1{160},\ \frac1{4608},\ \frac1{106496},\ \frac1{2228224},\dots\) By Theorem 10.6.5 the error after four terms (\(k=0,\dots,3\)) is at most the next term \(\frac1{2228224}\approx4.5\times10^{-7}<10^{-6}\). So the integral is \(\frac12-\frac1{160}+\frac1{4608}-\frac1{106496}=0.493958\) to six places.

Group 6: From series to functions and numbers¶

Example 11.2.28: A series in \(2x-3\)

Find the sum and the interval of convergence of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty(2x-3)^k\). Write it as a power series centered at \(a=\frac32\).

Solution. It is a geometric series with ratio \(2x-3\), which converges exactly when \(\abs{2x-3}<1\), that is \(1<x<2\), to \(\frac1{1-(2x-3)}=\frac1{4-2x}\). Since \((2x-3)^k=2^k\left(x-\frac32\right)^k\) it is a power series with center \(\frac32\) and \(c_k=2^k\), whose radius is \(\frac1L=\frac12\) with \(L=2\) (Theorem 11.2.4); this agrees with the interval \((1,2)\). At \(x=1\) the terms are \((-1)^k\) and at \(x=2\) they are \(1\), so neither endpoint converges.

Example 11.2.29: The arctangent and a numerical series

Find a power series for \(\tan^{-1}x\) and use it to sum \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)3^k}\).

Solution. \(\frac d{dx}\tan^{-1}x=\frac1{1+x^2}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(-1)^kx^{2k}\) for \(\abs x<1\) (substitution \(u=-x^2\)). Integrate (the constant is \(0\) since \(\tan^{-1}0=0\)): \(\tan^{-1}x=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^{2k+1}}{2k+1}\) for \(\abs x<1\). Now take \(x=\frac1{\sqrt3}\), which lies inside the interval: \(x^{2k+1}=\frac1{\sqrt3\,3^k}\) and \(\tan^{-1}\frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac\pi6\), so \(\frac1{\sqrt3}\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)3^k}=\frac\pi6\) and

\[\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)\,3^k}=\frac{\pi\sqrt3}6\approx0.9069 .\]

Check: the alternating series \(1-\frac19+\frac1{45}-\frac1{189}+\frac1{729}\) has sum \(0.90719\), and the error is at most the next term \(\frac1{2673}\approx3.7\times10^{-4}\); indeed \(0.90719-0.90690=2.9\times10^{-4}\).

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 11.2.30: Bounded coefficients

Prove: if \(\abs{c_k}\le M\) for all \(k\), then the radius of convergence of \(\sum c_k(x-a)^k\) is at least \(1\).

Plan. Compare with a geometric series at every point with \(\abs{x-a}<1\), then use Theorem 11.2.2.

Proof. Let \(\abs{x-a}<1\). Then \(\abs{c_k(x-a)^k}\le M\abs{x-a}^k\), and \(\sum M\abs{x-a}^k\) is a geometric series with ratio \(\abs{x-a}<1\), which converges (Theorem 10.3.3). By the Comparison Test (Theorem 10.5.1) \(\sum\abs{c_k(x-a)^k}\) converges, so the power series converges (absolutely) at every \(x\) with \(\abs{x-a}<1\). If \(R\) were less than \(1\), the series would diverge at points with \(R<\abs{x-a}<1\) (Theorem 11.2.2), a contradiction. Hence \(R\ge1\). ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to conclude \(R=1\). The bound is attained for \(c_k=1\) (\(R=1\)), but \(c_k=\frac1{k!}\) is also bounded by \(1\) and has \(R=\infty\) (its coefficient ratio \(\frac1{k+1}\to0\)).

Example 11.2.31: A power series determines its coefficients

Prove: if \(\sum c_kx^k=\sum d_kx^k\) for all \(x\) with \(\abs x<r\) (\(r>0\), both series convergent there), then \(c_k=d_k\) for every \(k\).

Plan. Subtract (Theorem 11.2.11(1)) to get a series equal to \(0\), then differentiate \(j\) times (Theorem 11.2.14) and evaluate at \(0\).

Proof. Let \(e_k=c_k-d_k\). By Theorem 11.2.11(1), \(h(x)=\sum e_kx^k\) converges to \(f(x)-g(x)=0\) for \(\abs x<r\), so \(h\equiv0\) on \((-r,r)\) and the radius of \(\sum e_kx^k\) is at least \(r\). By Theorem 11.2.14, applied \(j\) times (the differentiated series has the same radius each time; see Theorem F.6), for \(\abs x<r\)

\[h^{(j)}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^\infty k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)\,e_kx^{k-j}.\]

At \(x=0\) every term with \(k>j\) contains the factor \(x^{k-j}=0\), and the term \(k=j\) is \(j!\,e_j\). So \(h^{(j)}(0)=j!\,e_j\). But \(h\equiv0\) on \((-r,r)\), so \(h^{(j)}(0)=0\), and \(e_j=0\) for every \(j\). ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to put \(x=0\) only, which gives \(c_0=d_0\) and nothing more; the other coefficients are reached by differentiating first. The theorem is why “the power series of a function about a given center” makes sense: there is at most one.
