10.2 Sequences¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

In Definition 10.1.10 we estimated limits from tables and warned that tables prove nothing (Pause and think 10.1.2). Two questions now need honest answers. First, what does “the terms get and stay as close as we like to \(L\)” mean in a sentence that can be checked? Second, once we have that, how do we compute limits without a table: by combining limits we already know, by trapping a sequence between two others, by recognising which of two competing quantities grows faster, and, for a sequence defined by a recurrence, by deciding whether it settles down at all before we go looking for where?

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • state the \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) definition of a limit and use it to show that a simple sequence converges or diverges;

  • compute limits of sequences with the limit laws, with limits of functions as \(x\to\infty\), with the Squeeze Theorem and by applying a continuous function to a limit;

  • find the limit of \(r^n\) for every \(r\) and compare the growth of \(\ln^q n\), \(n^p\), \(b^n\), \(n!\) and \(n^n\);

  • decide whether a sequence is bounded and whether it is monotonic, and use the Monotone Convergence Theorem to prove convergence;

  • find the limit of a recursively defined sequence by the fixed-point rule after showing that it converges, and solve \(a_{n+1}=ra_n+d\) in closed form;

  • prove the main theorems of this section.

The definition of a limit¶

Definition 10.2.1: Limit of a sequence

The sequence \(\{a_n\}\) converges to \(L\), written \(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L\), if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an integer \(N\) such that

\[|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\quad\text{for every } n>N.\]

A sequence that does not converge diverges. We write \(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\infty\) if for every real number \(K\) there is an integer \(N\) with \(a_n>K\) for all \(n>N\) (and \(-\infty\) similarly); such a sequence diverges, and we say it diverges to infinity.

Read it as a game. Someone names a tolerance \(\varepsilon\), however small. You must answer with a cutoff \(N\) such that every term after the \(N\)th is within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\). If you can always win, the sequence converges to \(L\). A smaller \(\varepsilon\) usually forces a larger \(N\). This is the precise form of Definition 10.1.10, and it is the definition used in Appendix B (Definition A.2), where all the limit facts of this section are proved from it.

Example 10.2.2: Winning the game

Prove that \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2+1}{2n^2-1}=\frac12\) and find an \(N\) that works for \(\varepsilon=0.01\).

Plan. Simplify \(|a_n-L|\) to something we can make small by choosing \(n\) large.

Solution. For \(n\ge1\),

\[\left|\frac{n^2+1}{2n^2-1}-\frac12\right|=\left|\frac{2(n^2+1)-(2n^2-1)}{2(2n^2-1)}\right|=\frac{3}{2(2n^2-1)} .\]

This is less than \(\varepsilon\) exactly when \(2n^2-1>\frac3{2\varepsilon}\). Given any \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N\) an integer with \(N^2>\frac{3}{4\varepsilon}+\frac12\); then every \(n>N\) has \(2n^2-1>\frac3{2\varepsilon}\) and so \(|a_n-\frac12|<\varepsilon\). That proves the limit.

For \(\varepsilon=0.01\) we need \(2n^2-1>150\), so \(n^2>75.5\) and \(n\ge9\): \(N=8\) works. Check: at \(n=9\) the distance is \(\frac3{2\cdot161}\approx0.0093<0.01\), while at \(n=8\) it is \(\frac3{2\cdot127}\approx0.0118>0.01\), so \(N=7\) would not do.

Note that if \(N\) works for a given \(\varepsilon\), so does every larger integer, since the inequality \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\) is required for all \(n>N\). The definition therefore asks only that some cutoff exists, not the smallest one.

Computing limits¶

Theorem 10.2.3: Limit laws

Let \(a_n\to A\) and \(b_n\to B\), and let \(c\) be a constant. Then \(a_n\pm b_n\to A\pm B\), \(ca_n\to cA\), \(a_nb_n\to AB\), and \(a_n/b_n\to A/B\) if \(B\ne0\).

Proof. Here is the proof of the sum; the other three use the same idea and are done in full in Theorem B.4. Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(N_1,N_2\) with \(|a_n-A|<\varepsilon/2\) for \(n>N_1\) and \(|b_n-B|<\varepsilon/2\) for \(n>N_2\). For \(n>\max\{N_1,N_2\}\), the triangle inequality gives \(|(a_n+b_n)-(A+B)|\le|a_n-A|+|b_n-B|<\varepsilon\). For the product the new idea is the identity \(a_nb_n-AB=a_n(b_n-B)+B(a_n-A)\), together with the fact that a convergent sequence is bounded (Theorem B.2); for the quotient one shows first that \(1/b_n\to1/B\). ◻ ∎

Theorem 10.2.4: Limits of \(f(n)\)

If \(f(n)=a_n\) for every positive integer \(n\) and \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L\) (where \(L\) is a number, \(\infty\) or \(-\infty\)), then \(\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L\).

Proof. Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\to\infty\), there is a number \(X\) with \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\) for all \(x>X\). Choose an integer \(N\ge X\). For \(n>N\) we have \(n>X\), so \(|a_n-L|=|f(n)-L|<\varepsilon\). The cases \(L=\pm\infty\) are the same with “\(f(x)>K\)” or “\(f(x)<K\)” in place of “\(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\)”. ◻ ∎

The converse is false: \(a_n=\cos(2\pi n)=1\) converges although \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\cos(2\pi x)\) does not exist. So this theorem lets us use Calculus I tools (such as l’Hôpital’s Rule) on a function, but a failure for the function says nothing about the sequence.

Theorem 10.2.5: Squeeze Theorem for sequences

If \(a_n\le b_n\le c_n\) for all \(n\) beyond some index, and \(a_n\to L\) and \(c_n\to L\), then \(b_n\to L\).

Proof. Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(N\) so that \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\) and \(|c_n-L|<\varepsilon\) (and the inequalities \(a_n\le b_n\le c_n\) hold) for \(n>N\). Then \(L-\varepsilon<a_n\le b_n\le c_n<L+\varepsilon\), so \(|b_n-L|<\varepsilon\). ◻ ∎

Theorem 10.2.6: A continuous function of a limit

If \(a_n\to L\) and \(f\) is continuous at \(L\) and defined at every \(a_n\), then \(f(a_n)\to f(L)\).

Proof. Let \(\varepsilon>0\). By continuity there is \(\delta>0\) with \(|f(y)-f(L)|<\varepsilon\) whenever \(|y-L|<\delta\). Choose \(N\) with \(|a_n-L|<\delta\) for \(n>N\). Then \(|f(a_n)-f(L)|<\varepsilon\) for \(n>N\). ◻ ∎

A consequence used constantly: \(|a_n|\to0\) if and only if \(a_n\to0\) (Pause and think 10.2.1 and Example 10.2.35 use related ideas), since \(|a_n-0|=\big||a_n|-0\big|\).

Example 10.2.7: Limit laws on a rational expression

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n^2+n}{2n^2-5}\).

Solution. Each of \(3n^2+n\) and \(2n^2-5\) tends to infinity, so the limit laws cannot be applied directly (“\(\infty/\infty\)”). Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power, \(n^2\):

\[\frac{3n^2+n}{2n^2-5}=\frac{3+1/n}{2-5/n^2}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{3+0}{2-0}=\frac32,\]

using \(\frac1n\to0\) and \(\frac5{n^2}\to0\), the sum, product and quotient laws, and \(2\ne0\).

Example 10.2.8: A difference of square roots

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+n}-n\right)\).

Solution. This is “\(\infty-\infty\)”. Multiply and divide by the conjugate \(\sqrt{n^2+n}+n\):

\[\sqrt{n^2+n}-n=\frac{(n^2+n)-n^2}{\sqrt{n^2+n}+n}=\frac{n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}+n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+1/n}+1}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac1{1+1}=\frac12 .\]

In the last step we used that the square root is continuous (Theorem 10.2.6) so \(\sqrt{1+1/n}\to\sqrt1=1\).

Example 10.2.9: A limit of \(f(n)\)

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\sin\frac1n\).

Solution. Let \(f(x)=x\sin\frac1x\). Substitute \(u=\frac1x\); as \(x\to\infty\), \(u\to0^+\), and \(f(x)=\dfrac{\sin u}{u}\to1\) by the basic limit from Calculus I. By Theorem 10.2.4, \(\lim_{n\to\infty}n\sin\frac1n=1\).

Example 10.2.10: Squeezing an oscillation

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\cos n}{n^2}\).

Solution. The numerator oscillates, so the quotient law does not apply (it needs \(\lim\cos n\) to exist). Instead, \(-1\le\cos n\le1\) gives

\[-\frac1{n^2}\le\frac{\cos n}{n^2}\le\frac1{n^2},\]

and both outer sequences tend to \(0\) (Theorem 10.2.4 with \(f(x)=1/x^2\)). By the Squeeze Theorem the limit is \(0\).

Knowledge Check 10.2.1: Computing limits

Find each limit or say that it diverges: (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{4n^3-n}{2n^3+7}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n+3}-\sqrt n\right)\); (c) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^2-1}{n+2}\).

Pause and think 10.2.1: When limit laws need hypotheses

(a) Give sequences \(a_n\) and \(b_n\) that both diverge while \(a_n+b_n\) and \(a_nb_n\) both converge. (b) If \(a_n\) diverges and \(a_n+b_n\) converges, must \(b_n\) diverge? Prove your answer using a limit law. (c) Why does the quotient law in Theorem 10.2.3 require \(B\ne0\), and what can happen when \(A=B=0\)?

Answer and discussion

(a) \(a_n=(-1)^n\) and \(b_n=-(-1)^n\): both diverge, \(a_n+b_n=0\) converges, and with \(b_n=(-1)^n\) the product \(a_nb_n=1\) converges. (b) Yes. If \(b_n\) converged, then \(a_n=(a_n+b_n)-b_n\) would converge by the difference law, contradicting the assumption. (c) The quotient law needs \(B\ne0\) since division by \(0\) is undefined and \(b_n\) may be \(0\) or tiny; when \(A=B=0\) the quotient can do anything: \(\frac1n\big/\frac1n\to1\), \(\frac1{n^2}\big/\frac1n\to0\), \(\frac1n\big/\frac1{n^2}\to\infty\). (This is the “\(0/0\)” form.)

Geometric sequences and growth rates¶

Theorem 10.2.11: The sequence \(r^n\)

Let \(r\) be a real number. Then

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}r^n=\begin{cases}0 & \text{if } |r|<1,\\ 1 & \text{if } r=1,\end{cases}\]

\(r^n\to\infty\) if \(r>1\), and \(\{r^n\}\) diverges if \(r\le-1\).

Proof. We use Bernoulli’s inequality: \((1+h)^n\ge1+nh\) for every \(h>0\) and every integer \(n\ge1\). This is proved by induction (Example 10.2.34).

Case \(r>1\). Write \(r=1+h\) with \(h>0\). Then \(r^n\ge1+nh\), and \(1+nh\to\infty\), so \(r^n\to\infty\) (given \(K\), choose \(N\ge K/h\); for \(n>N\), \(r^n\ge1+nh>K\)).

Case \(0<|r|<1\). Then \(1/|r|>1\); write \(1/|r|=1+h\) with \(h>0\). Then \(|r|^n=\dfrac1{(1+h)^n}\le\dfrac1{1+nh}\), so

\[0\le|r^n|\le\frac1{1+nh}\to0,\]

and the Squeeze Theorem gives \(|r^n|\to0\), hence \(r^n\to0\). For \(r=0\) the sequence is \(0,0,\dots\) and the claim is trivial.

Case \(r=1\). The sequence is constant, equal to \(1\).

Case \(r=-1\). The terms alternate between \(-1\) and \(1\); consecutive terms differ by \(2\), so no limit exists (the argument of Appendix Example B.9).

Case \(r<-1\). Then \(|r|>1\), so \(|r^n|=|r|^n\to\infty\) by the first case; a sequence with \(|a_n|\to\infty\) is unbounded, but a convergent sequence is bounded (Theorem B.2), so \(\{r^n\}\) diverges. ◻ ∎

Example 10.2.12: Using \(r^n\)

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3^n+2^n}{3^{n+1}}\).

Solution. Divide each term by \(3^n\) and write \(3^{n+1}=3\cdot3^n\):

\[\frac{3^n+2^n}{3^{n+1}}=\frac{1+(2/3)^n}{3}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{1+0}{3}=\frac13,\]

because \(|2/3|<1\), so \((2/3)^n\to0\) by Theorem 10.2.11.

Which grows faster, \(n^{10}\) or \(1.01^n\)? Or \(2^n\) or \(n!\)? The next theorem ranks the standard growth rates; each statement says that the second quantity eventually dwarfs the first.

Theorem 10.2.13: Growth rates

Let \(p>0\), \(q>0\) and \(b>1\). Then

(a) \(\dfrac{(\ln n)^q}{n^p}\to0\),

(b) \(\dfrac{n^p}{b^n}\to0\),

(c) \(\dfrac{b^n}{n!}\to0\),

(d) \(\dfrac{n!}{n^n}\to0\).

Consequently, for large \(n\),

\[(\ln n)^q\ll n^p\ll b^n\ll n!\ll n^n,\]

where \(u_n\ll v_n\) means \(u_n/v_n\to0\).

Proof. (d) \(\dfrac{n!}{n^n}=\dfrac1n\cdot\dfrac2n\cdots\dfrac nn\le\dfrac1n\cdot1\cdots1=\dfrac1n\), since each of the other factors is at most \(1\). So \(0<\frac{n!}{n^n}\le\frac1n\to0\), and the Squeeze Theorem gives the limit \(0\).

(c) Choose an integer \(M\ge2b\). For \(n>M\),

\[\frac{b^n}{n!}=\frac{b^M}{M!}\cdot\frac{b}{M+1}\cdot\frac{b}{M+2}\cdots\frac bn\le\frac{b^M}{M!}\left(\frac12\right)^{n-M},\]

because each of the \(n-M\) factors \(\frac bj\) (\(j>M\)) is at most \(\frac b{2b}=\frac12\). The right side is a constant times \((1/2)^n\), which tends to \(0\) by Theorem 10.2.11; squeezing gives \(b^n/n!\to0\).

(b) Write \(b=1+h\) with \(h>0\) and choose an integer \(m>p\). For \(n\ge2m\) the binomial theorem gives, keeping only one term,

\[b^n\ge\binom nm h^m=\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1)}{m!}h^m\ge\frac{(n/2)^m}{m!}h^m ,\]

since each of the \(m\) factors \(n-j\) (\(0\le j\le m-1\)) is at least \(n-m+1>n/2\) when \(n\ge2m\). Hence

\[0<\frac{n^p}{b^n}\le\frac{2^m\,m!}{h^m}\cdot n^{p-m}\to0,\]

because \(p-m<0\) means \(n^{p-m}=1/n^{m-p}\to0\) (Theorem 10.2.4). The Squeeze Theorem gives (b).

(a) First take \(q=1\). The function \(f(x)=\dfrac{\ln x}{x^p}\) has the form \(\infty/\infty\) as \(x\to\infty\), and by l’Hôpital’s Rule

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^p}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1/x}{p\,x^{p-1}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1{p\,x^{p}}=0,\]

so \(\frac{\ln n}{n^p}\to0\) for every \(p>0\) by Theorem 10.2.4. For general \(q>0\) apply this with \(p/q\) in place of \(p\): \(\frac{(\ln n)^q}{n^p}=\left(\frac{\ln n}{n^{p/q}}\right)^q\to0^q=0\), using Theorem 10.2.6 with the continuous function \(y\mapsto y^q\) at \(0\) (continuous from the right at \(0\), which is all that is needed since the terms are positive).

The chain follows by combining (a)–(d): for instance \(n^p/n!=\frac{n^p}{b^n}\cdot\frac{b^n}{n!}\) with \(b=2\) is a product of two sequences tending to \(0\). ◻ ∎

Example 10.2.14: Ranking growth rates in a quotient

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!+4^n}{n!+n^4}\).

Solution. The dominant term in both numerator and denominator is \(n!\). Divide by \(n!\):

\[\frac{n!+4^n}{n!+n^4}=\frac{1+4^n/n!}{1+n^4/n!}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{1+0}{1+0}=1,\]

because \(4^n/n!\to0\) by Theorem 10.2.13(c), and \(n^4/n!\to0\) because \(n^4/2^n\to0\) by (b) and \(2^n/n!\to0\) by (c). Numerically, \(4^{10}/10!\approx0.29\) and \(4^{20}/20!\approx4.5\times10^{-7}\), already small.

Knowledge Check 10.2.2: Squeeze and growth

Find each limit: (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(-1)^n\,n}{n^2+1}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2^n+5^n}{5^n+1}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n^{20}}{1.5^n}\) and say what a table of the first \(50\) terms would show.

Monotone sequences and boundedness¶

Definition 10.2.15: Monotone and bounded sequences

A sequence \(\{a_n\}\) is nondecreasing if \(a_{n+1}\ge a_n\) for all \(n\), increasing if \(a_{n+1}>a_n\), and nonincreasing or decreasing if the inequalities are reversed; it is monotonic if it is of one of these kinds. It is bounded above if there is \(M\) with \(a_n\le M\) for all \(n\), bounded below if \(a_n\ge m\) for all \(n\), and bounded if both hold.

To show a sequence is monotonic, compare \(a_{n+1}\) with \(a_n\) directly (by subtraction, by a ratio of positive terms, or, for \(a_n=f(n)\), from the sign of \(f'\)), or use induction on a recurrence. Bounds are usually proved by induction or by an inequality.

Theorem 10.2.16: Monotone Convergence Theorem

A nondecreasing sequence that is bounded above converges, to its least upper bound. A nonincreasing sequence that is bounded below converges, to its greatest lower bound. In particular a bounded monotonic sequence converges.

Proof. Let \(\{a_n\}\) be nondecreasing and bounded above. By the completeness of the real numbers (the least upper bound property; see the beginning of Appendix A) the set \(\{a_1,a_2,\dots\}\) has a least upper bound \(L\). Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(L-\varepsilon\) is not an upper bound, some \(a_N>L-\varepsilon\). For \(n>N\) we have \(a_n\ge a_N>L-\varepsilon\) because the sequence is nondecreasing, and \(a_n\le L\) because \(L\) is an upper bound. So \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\). The nonincreasing case follows by applying this to \(-a_n\). The complete argument is Theorem A.5. ◻ ∎

The theorem does not tell us the limit; it tells us there is one. For a recurrence, there is a good way to find it once existence is known.

Theorem 10.2.17: Fixed-point rule

Suppose \(a_{n+1}=f(a_n)\) for all \(n\), the function \(f\) is continuous, and \(a_n\to L\). Then \(f(L)=L\).

Proof. The sequence \(a_2,a_3,\dots\) also converges to \(L\) (it is the same sequence with the first term removed; Theorem B.3). On the other hand \(a_{n+1}=f(a_n)\to f(L)\) by Theorem 10.2.6. A sequence has only one limit (Theorem B.1), so \(f(L)=L\). ◻ ∎

The rule assumes the limit exists

The fixed-point rule says if the sequence converges then the limit solves \(f(L)=L\). It never says that the sequence converges. The sequence \(a_1=1\), \(a_{n+1}=2a_n+1\) gives \(L=2L+1\), so \(L=-1\), yet the terms \(1,3,7,15,\dots\) grow without bound: there is no limit at all (Pause and think 10.2.3). Prove convergence (monotone and bounded) first, then solve for \(L\).

Example 10.2.18: Monotone and bounded: repeated square roots

In Example 10.1.23 we guessed that \(a_1=1\), \(a_{n+1}=\sqrt{6+a_n}\) tends to \(3\). Prove it.

Bounded above by \(3\). \(a_1=1<3\). If \(a_n<3\) then \(a_{n+1}=\sqrt{6+a_n}<\sqrt{9}=3\). So \(a_n<3\) for all \(n\) (induction).

Increasing. Compare squares: \(a_{n+1}^2-a_n^2=6+a_n-a_n^2=(3-a_n)(2+a_n)>0\), because \(a_n<3\) and \(a_n>0\). Hence \(a_{n+1}>a_n\) for every \(n\) (all terms are positive).

Limit. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem the sequence converges, say to \(L\), and \(L\le3\). By the fixed-point rule with \(f(x)=\sqrt{6+x}\), \(L=\sqrt{6+L}\), so \(L^2-L-6=(L-3)(L+2)=0\); since \(L>0\), \(L=3\).

Pause and think 10.2.2: Which hypothesis does the work?

The Monotone Convergence Theorem has two hypotheses: “monotonic” and “bounded”. Give an example of (a) a monotonic sequence that is not bounded and diverges, and (b) a bounded sequence that is not monotonic and diverges. Then say, in words, why each hypothesis cannot be dropped.

Answer and discussion

(a) \(a_n=n\): increasing, unbounded, diverges to infinity. (b) \(a_n=(-1)^n\): bounded, not monotonic, diverges. The monotonic hypothesis prevents oscillation between two values; the bounded hypothesis prevents escape to infinity. The theorem needs both: monotone says the terms can only move one way, and bounded says they cannot move forever.

Recurrences \(a_{n+1}=ra_n+d\)¶

Theorem 10.2.19: Linear recurrences of first order

Let \(a_{n+1}=ra_n+d\) for all \(n\ge1\), with \(r\ne1\), and let \(L=\dfrac d{1-r}\). Then

\[a_n=L+(a_1-L)\,r^{\,n-1}\quad\text{for every } n\ge1 .\]

Consequently the sequence converges if \(|r|<1\) (to \(L\)) or if \(a_1=L\) (it is constant), and otherwise it diverges.

Proof. Note that \(L\) satisfies \(L=rL+d\), because \(L(1-r)=d\). By induction: for \(n=1\) the formula says \(a_1=L+(a_1-L)=a_1\). If \(a_n=L+(a_1-L)r^{n-1}\), then

\[a_{n+1}=ra_n+d=rL+(a_1-L)r^{n}+d=\big(rL+d\big)+(a_1-L)r^n=L+(a_1-L)r^{n},\]

which is the formula for \(n+1\). For the consequence, if \(|r|<1\) then \(r^{n-1}\to0\) by Theorem 10.2.11, so \(a_n\to L\). If \(a_1=L\) the formula gives \(a_n=L\) for all \(n\). If \(|r|\ge1\) and \(a_1\ne L\), the sequence \((a_1-L)r^{n-1}\) diverges by Theorem 10.2.11 (for \(r=-1\) it alternates; for \(|r|>1\) it is unbounded; \(r=1\) is excluded), so \(a_n=L+(a_1-L)r^{n-1}\) diverges (if \(a_n\) converged then so would \(a_n-L\)). ◻ ∎

Example 10.2.20: Solving a linear recurrence

In Example 10.1.29 the amount of a drug just after the \(n\)th dose satisfied \(D_1=100\), \(D_{n+1}=0.8D_n+100\). Find \(\lim D_n\).

Solution. Here \(r=0.8\), \(d=100\), so \(L=\dfrac{100}{1-0.8}=500\) and by Theorem 10.2.19

\[D_n=500+(100-500)(0.8)^{n-1}=500-400(0.8)^{n-1}.\]

This agrees with the closed form \(500(1-0.8^n)\) found earlier (since \(400\cdot0.8^{n-1}=500\cdot0.8^n\)). Because \(|0.8|<1\), \(D_n\to500\).

Knowledge Check 10.2.3: A first-order recurrence

Let \(a_1=1\) and \(a_{n+1}=\dfrac{a_n+3}{2}\). (a) Find \(L=\dfrac d{1-r}\) and the closed form of \(a_n\). (b) Find \(\lim a_n\). (c) For the recurrence \(b_1=2\), \(b_{n+1}=-2b_n+3\), find \(L\), the closed form, and say whether \(b_n\) converges.

Pause and think 10.2.3: Why does the fixed-point rule give a false answer here?

Apply the fixed-point rule to \(a_1=1\), \(a_{n+1}=2a_n+1\) and find \(L\). All the terms are positive. What does that say? Use Theorem 10.2.19 to decide what really happens, and say which hypothesis of the rule was false.

Answer and discussion

The rule gives \(L=2L+1\), so \(L=-1\). But all terms are positive, and a limit of positive numbers cannot be negative (see the remark on limits and inequalities), so something is wrong. Here \(r=2\), \(d=1\), \(L=\frac1{1-2}=-1\), and \(a_n=-1+(1-(-1))2^{n-1}=-1+2^n\), which tends to infinity. The hypothesis that failed is that \(a_n\) converges. The equation \(L=f(L)\) only locates the possible limits.

Example 10.2.21: Newton’s method for \(\sqrt5\)

Let \(a_1=3\) and \(a_{n+1}=\dfrac12\left(a_n+\dfrac5{a_n}\right)\). Show that \(\{a_n\}\) converges and find its limit.

Bounded below by \(\sqrt5\). For \(a_n>0\), \(a_{n+1}-\sqrt5=\dfrac{(a_n-\sqrt5)^2}{2a_n}\ge0\). [To check: \(\frac12\left(a+\frac5a\right)-\sqrt5=\frac{a^2-2\sqrt5a+5}{2a}\).] So \(a_n\ge\sqrt5\) for all \(n\ge2\), and \(a_1=3>\sqrt5\) too.

Nonincreasing. Since \(a_n^2\ge5\), we have \(\dfrac5{a_n}\le a_n\), so \(a_{n+1}-a_n=\dfrac12\left(\dfrac5{a_n}-a_n\right)\le0\).

Limit. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, \(a_n\to L\ge\sqrt5\). The fixed-point rule with \(f(x)=\frac12(x+5/x)\) (continuous for \(x>0\)) gives \(L=\frac12(L+\frac5L)\), so \(L^2=5\) and \(L=\sqrt5\approx2.2360680\). The first terms \(3,\ \frac73\approx2.3333,\ \frac{47}{21}\approx2.2381\) converge very quickly.

Limits and inequalities

If \(a_n\to L\) and \(a_n\ge m\) for all \(n\), then \(L\ge m\). (Suppose instead \(L<m\) and take \(\varepsilon=m-L>0\); for large \(n\), \(a_n<L+\varepsilon=m\), a contradiction.) But strict inequalities are not preserved: \(a_n=\frac1n>0\) for all \(n\), yet \(\lim a_n=0\), which is not greater than \(0\).

Group 1: Limit laws and algebra¶

Example 10.2.22: A rational expression with a lower-order tail

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{5n^2-3}{n^2+4n}\).

Solution. Divide the numerator and the denominator by \(n^2\), the highest power in the denominator: \(\dfrac{5-3/n^2}{1+4/n}\). Since \(\frac3{n^2}\to0\) and \(\frac4n\to0\), the limit laws give \(\dfrac{5-0}{1+0}=5\). Check: \(a_{1000}=\dfrac{5\cdot10^6-3}{10^6+4000}\approx4.9801\), below \(5\), as expected since the \(4n\) in the denominator pulls the value down.

Example 10.2.23: A hidden conjugate

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n\right)\).

Solution. Each term tends to infinity, so we use the conjugate: \(\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n=\dfrac{3n}{\sqrt{n^2+3n}+n}=\dfrac3{\sqrt{1+3/n}+1}\to\dfrac3{1+1}=\dfrac32\). Check: at \(n=10^4\), \(\sqrt{10^8+3\cdot10^4}-10^4\approx1.4999\).

Group 2: Limits of \(f(n)\) and the Squeeze Theorem¶

Example 10.2.24: A derivative in disguise

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n\left(e^{1/n}-1\right)\).

Solution. Let \(f(x)=x\left(e^{1/x}-1\right)=\dfrac{e^{u}-1}{u}\) with \(u=\frac1x\to0^+\). This is the difference quotient of \(e^u\) at \(u=0\), whose limit is the derivative \(\frac{d}{du}e^u\big|_{u=0}=1\). By Theorem 10.2.4, the sequence tends to \(1\). Check: at \(n=1000\), \(1000\left(e^{0.001}-1\right)\approx1.0005\).

Example 10.2.25: Squeezing with a bounded oscillation

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n+\sin n}{n+2}\).

Solution. Because \(-1\le\sin n\le1\), \(\dfrac{n-1}{n+2}\le\dfrac{n+\sin n}{n+2}\le\dfrac{n+1}{n+2}\). Both bounds tend to \(1\) (divide by \(n\): \(\frac{1-1/n}{1+2/n}\) and \(\frac{1+1/n}{1+2/n}\)). By the Squeeze Theorem the limit is \(1\). Notice that we could not split the fraction as \(\frac{n}{n+2}+\frac{\sin n}{n+2}\) and apply the sum law unless we knew each piece converges; here they do (\(\frac{\sin n}{n+2}\to0\) by squeezing), but the squeeze above avoids the extra step.

Group 3: Geometric sequences and growth rates¶

Example 10.2.26: Dominant exponentials

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3^n-2^n}{3^n+2^n}\).

Solution. Divide by \(3^n\): \(\dfrac{1-(2/3)^n}{1+(2/3)^n}\). Since \((2/3)^n\to0\) (Theorem 10.2.11), the limit is \(\dfrac{1-0}{1+0}=1\). Check at \(n=10\): \(\frac{3^{10}-2^{10}}{3^{10}+2^{10}}=\frac{58025}{60073}\approx0.966\).

Example 10.2.27: Factorials win eventually

(a) Show that \(\dfrac{n^{10}}{1.01^n}\to0\). (b) Find where \(a_n=\dfrac{100^n}{n!}\) stops increasing, and explain why \(a_n\to0\) anyway.

Solution. (a) This is Theorem 10.2.13(b) with \(p=10\) and \(b=1.01\): any exponential beats any power, however small the base excess. (It takes a very large \(n\): \(\frac{n^{10}}{1.01^n}\) is still huge at \(n=1000\), so a table of moderate size would suggest the opposite. Compare Pause and think 10.1.2.)

(b) The ratio of consecutive terms is \(\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\dfrac{100^{n+1}/(n+1)!}{100^n/n!}=\dfrac{100}{n+1}\), which is greater than \(1\) while \(n+1<100\), equal to \(1\) at \(n=99\), and less than \(1\) afterwards. So the terms increase up to \(a_{99}\), with \(a_{100}=a_{99}\), and then decrease. Indeed \(\frac{100^{100}}{100!}=\frac{100^{99}}{99!}\cdot\frac{100}{100}\). The maximum is astronomically large (\(a_{99}\approx10^{42}\)), yet by Theorem 10.2.13(c) with \(b=100\) the sequence tends to \(0\). The theorem is about the long run only.

Group 4: Recurrences \(a_{n+1}=ra_n+d\)¶

Example 10.2.28: A convergent linear recurrence

Let \(a_1=10\) and \(a_{n+1}=\frac12a_n+4\). Find \(a_n\) in closed form and its limit.

Solution. Here \(r=\frac12\), \(d=4\), \(L=\dfrac4{1-1/2}=8\), so Theorem 10.2.19 gives \(a_n=8+(10-8)\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}=8+2\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}\). Check: \(a_2=8+1=9\) and directly \(\frac12(10)+4=9\); \(a_3=8+\frac12=8.5\) and directly \(\frac12(9)+4=8.5\). As \(n\to\infty\), \(\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}\to0\) and \(a_n\to8\), decreasing from above.

Example 10.2.29: When \(|r|>1\)

Let \(a_{n+1}=-2a_n+3\). For which starting values \(a_1\) does \(\{a_n\}\) converge? Find the limit.

Solution. \(r=-2\), \(d=3\), \(L=\dfrac3{1-(-2)}=1\), so \(a_n=1+(a_1-1)(-2)^{n-1}\). Since \(|r|>1\), the term \((a_1-1)(-2)^{n-1}\) diverges unless \(a_1-1=0\). So the sequence converges only if \(a_1=1\), when it is constant \(1,1,1,\dots\) with limit \(1\). For example \(a_1=2\) gives \(2,\,-1,\,5,\,-7,\,17,\dots\) with \(a_n=1+(-2)^{n-1}\): the terms alternate in sign and grow. Check: \(a_2=-4+3=-1\), formula \(1+(-2)=-1\); \(a_3=2+3=5\), formula \(1+4=5\). So the fixed point \(L=1\) is repelling: a start anywhere else is pushed away (compare the warning about the fixed-point rule).

Group 5: Monotone and bounded¶

Example 10.2.30: A series-like sequence bounded by a geometric sum

Let \(a_n=1+1+\frac1{2!}+\frac1{3!}+\cdots+\frac1{n!}=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac1{k!}\). Show that \(\{a_n\}\) converges.

Solution. Increasing: \(a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac1{(n+1)!}>0\). Bounded: for \(k\ge1\), \(k!=1\cdot2\cdot3\cdots k\ge2^{k-1}\) (each factor from \(2\) on is at least \(2\)), so \(\frac1{k!}\le\frac1{2^{k-1}}\) and

\[a_n\le1+\left(1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots+\frac1{2^{n-1}}\right)=1+\frac{1-2^{-n}}{1-\frac12}<1+2=3 ,\]

using the finite geometric sum (Theorem 10.1.15). By the Monotone Convergence Theorem the sequence converges, to a number between \(a_3=\frac{8}{3}\approx2.667\) and \(3\). (It converges to \(e\).)

Example 10.2.31: A bounded sum by comparison with a telescoping sum

Let \(s_n=1+\dfrac1{2^2}+\dfrac1{3^2}+\cdots+\dfrac1{n^2}\). Show that \(\{s_n\}\) converges.

Solution. The sequence is increasing since each added term is positive. To bound it, compare each term with a telescoping one: for \(k\ge2\), \(\dfrac1{k^2}<\dfrac1{k(k-1)}=\dfrac1{k-1}-\dfrac1k\). Therefore

\[s_n<1+\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n\right)=2-\frac1n<2 .\]

So \(\{s_n\}\) is increasing and bounded above by \(2\), hence convergent by the Monotone Convergence Theorem. The terms are \(1,\ 1.25,\ 1.3611,\ 1.4236,\dots\), and the limit is in fact \(\frac{\pi^2}6\approx1.6449\), a fact we cannot get from this argument alone.

Group 6: The \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) definition¶

Example 10.2.32: Finding \(N\) for a rational sequence

Prove that \(\dfrac{3n}{n+1}\to3\) and find \(N\) for \(\varepsilon=0.01\).

Solution. \(\left|\dfrac{3n}{n+1}-3\right|=\left|\dfrac{3n-3n-3}{n+1}\right|=\dfrac3{n+1}\). This is less than \(\varepsilon\) when \(n+1>\frac3\varepsilon\), that is \(n>\frac3\varepsilon-1\). So for every \(\varepsilon>0\) any integer \(N\ge\frac3\varepsilon-1\) works. For \(\varepsilon=0.01\): \(\frac3\varepsilon-1=299\), so \(N=299\). Check at \(n=300\): \(\frac3{301}<0.01\).

Example 10.2.33: An alternating sequence

Prove that \(\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}\to0\) and find \(N\) for \(\varepsilon=0.001\).

Solution. The signs do not affect the distance: \(\left|\dfrac{(-1)^n}n-0\right|=\dfrac1n\). We need \(\frac1n<\varepsilon\), that is \(n>\frac1\varepsilon\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), take any integer \(N\ge\frac1\varepsilon\); then \(n>N\) gives \(\frac1n<\varepsilon\). For \(\varepsilon=0.001\), \(N=1000\). Check: for \(n=1001\) the distance is \(\frac1{1001}<0.001\), but at \(n=1000\) it is exactly \(0.001\), which is not less than \(\varepsilon\), so the cutoff \(N=999\) would fail: the definition requires strict inequality for every \(n>N\).

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.2.34: Bernoulli’s inequality

Prove that \((1+h)^n\ge1+nh\) for every real \(h>0\) and every integer \(n\ge1\).

Proof. Base case \(n=1\): \((1+h)^1=1+h\ge1+1\cdot h\).

Inductive step. Assume \((1+h)^n\ge1+nh\). Multiply both sides by \(1+h>0\) (this keeps the inequality):

\[(1+h)^{n+1}\ge(1+nh)(1+h)=1+(n+1)h+nh^2\ge1+(n+1)h,\]

because \(nh^2\ge0\). This is the statement for \(n+1\), so by induction it holds for all \(n\ge1\). ◻ ∎

The inequality is the key to Theorem 10.2.11: it says that \((1+h)^n\) grows at least linearly in \(n\).

Example 10.2.35: Absolute values and limits

Prove that if \(a_n\to L\), then \(|a_n|\to|L|\). Is the converse true?

Proof. By the reverse triangle inequality \(\big||a_n|-|L|\big|\le|a_n-L|\). (To see it, write \(|a_n|=|(a_n-L)+L|\le|a_n-L|+|L|\), so \(|a_n|-|L|\le|a_n-L|\); exchange the roles of \(a_n\) and \(L\) to get \(|L|-|a_n|\le|a_n-L|\).) Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(N\) with \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\) for \(n>N\). Then \(\big||a_n|-|L|\big|<\varepsilon\) for \(n>N\), which is the claim. (Alternatively, apply Theorem 10.2.6 with \(f(x)=|x|\), which is continuous.) ◻ ∎

The converse is false: \(a_n=(-1)^n\) has \(|a_n|=1\to1\), but \(\{a_n\}\) diverges. The converse does hold when the limit is zero: \(|a_n|\to0\Rightarrow a_n\to0\), as in the remark after Theorem 10.2.6.

Answers and discussion¶

Knowledge Check 10.2.1. (a) Divide by \(n^3\): \(\frac{4-1/n^2}{2+7/n^3}\to\frac42=2\). (b) Conjugate: \(\sqrt{n+3}-\sqrt n=\frac{3}{\sqrt{n+3}+\sqrt n}\to0\) since the denominator tends to infinity. (c) \(\frac{n^2-1}{n+2}=\frac{n-1/n}{1+2/n}\to\infty\) (the numerator tends to infinity and the denominator to \(1\)); the sequence diverges to infinity. Equivalently \(\frac{n^2-1}{n+2}=n-2+\frac3{n+2}\) by division.

Knowledge Check 10.2.2. (a) \(\left|\frac{(-1)^nn}{n^2+1}\right|\le\frac n{n^2+1}\to0\), so by squeezing the limit is \(0\). (b) Divide by \(5^n\): \(\frac{(2/5)^n+1}{1+5^{-n}}\to\frac{0+1}{1+0}=1\). (c) \(n^{20}/1.5^n\to0\) by Theorem 10.2.13(b), but the first \(50\) terms are large: for example \(50^{20}/1.5^{50}\approx1.5\times10^{25}\), so a table of \(50\) terms would show huge, growing values and mislead; the decrease begins only much later.

Knowledge Check 10.2.3. (a) \(r=\frac12\), \(d=\frac32\), \(L=\frac{3/2}{1/2}=3\), \(a_n=3+(1-3)\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}=3-2\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}\). Check \(a_2=\frac{1+3}2=2\) and the formula \(3-1=2\). (b) \(|r|<1\), so \(a_n\to3\). (c) \(r=-2\), \(d=3\), \(L=1\), \(b_n=1+(2-1)(-2)^{n-1}=1+(-2)^{n-1}\); since \(|r|>1\) and \(b_1\ne L\), \(b_n\) diverges.
