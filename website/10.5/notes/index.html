10.5 Comparison Tests¶

Guiding question

The Integral Test settles \(\sum\frac1{k^2+4}\) in two lines, but try it on \(\sum\frac{5k^4-2k^2+3}{2k^6-k+5}\): the antiderivative is a nightmare, and the function is not obviously decreasing. Yet to the eye this series is just \(\sum\frac5{2k^2}\) in disguise: for large \(k\) the lower-order terms hardly matter. This section turns that impression into theorems. If the terms of a series are smaller than the terms of a series that converges, the smaller series converges too; and if the terms of two series have a positive ratio in the long run, the two series behave alike. The yardsticks are the two families we can already decide completely: geometric series and \(p\)-series.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • state and prove the Comparison Test and the Limit Comparison Test (all cases);

  • choose a comparison series from the dominant behaviour of the terms, using geometric series and \(p\)-series as yardsticks;

  • say why an inequality that points the wrong way proves nothing, and repair it;

  • explain which cases of the Limit Comparison Test are conclusive;

  • use a comparison to bound a sum and to find how many terms give a given accuracy.

Throughout, the series have terms \(\ge0\) (positive terms, in the language of the tests); the next section deals with series whose terms change sign.

The Comparison Test¶

Convergence depends only on the tail of a series (Theorem 10.3.11(5)), so an inequality between terms is needed only from some index \(N\) on.

Theorem 10.5.1: Comparison Test

Let \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) be series and let \(N\) be an integer with \(0\le a_k\le b_k\) for all \(k\ge N\).

(1) If \(\sum b_k\) converges, then \(\sum a_k\) converges, and \(\sum_{k=N}^\infty a_k\le\sum_{k=N}^\infty b_k\).

(2) If \(\sum a_k\) diverges, then \(\sum b_k\) diverges.

(3) If \(\sum b_k\) converges, then for every \(n\ge N\) the remainders satisfy \(0\le\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty a_k\le\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty b_k\).

Proof. By Theorem 10.3.11(5) we may drop the terms before index \(N\) from both series without changing whether they converge, so assume both start at \(k=N\). Let \(s_n=\sum_{k=N}^{N+n-1}a_k\) and \(t_n=\sum_{k=N}^{N+n-1}b_k\) be the partial sums. Because \(a_k\le b_k\), adding the first \(n\) inequalities gives \(0\le s_n\le t_n\) for every \(n\).

(1) Let \(B=\sum_{k=N}^\infty b_k\). The terms of \(\sum b_k\) are \(\ge0\), so \(t_n\le B\) by Theorem 10.3.13(a). Hence \(s_n\le t_n\le B\) for all \(n\): the partial sums of \(\sum a_k\) are bounded above. The terms \(a_k\) are \(\ge0\), so by Theorem 10.3.13(a) the series converges, to a number \(A\). Finally \(s_n\le t_n\) for all \(n\) and limits preserve non-strict inequalities (Theorem C.1), so \(A\le B\).

(2) This is the contrapositive of (1): if \(\sum b_k\) converged, then \(\sum a_k\) would converge.

(3) Suppose \(\sum b_k\) converges and \(n\ge N\). The tail \(\sum_{k=n+1}^\infty b_k\) converges by Theorem 10.3.11(5), and \(0\le a_k\le b_k\) for \(k\ge n+1\). Apply (1) with \(N\) replaced by \(n+1\). ◻ ∎

Read part (2) as: if the smaller series diverges, so does the larger one. Part (1) says the same for convergence in the other direction. The two remaining combinations say nothing, and we return to that below. Because a constant factor \(c>0\) does not change convergence (Theorem 10.3.11), the test also works with \(a_k\le cb_k\) for some constant \(c>0\).

The yardsticks

Every comparison needs a series whose behaviour we already know. We have two families. The geometric series \(\sum r^k\) converges if and only if \(\abs r<1\) (Theorem 10.3.3); the \(p\)-series \(\sum\frac1{k^p}\) converges if and only if \(p>1\) (Theorem 10.4.11). Constant multiples and finitely many changed terms do not matter.

Example 10.5.2: Comparing with a geometric or a \(p\)-series

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{3^k+k}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac k{k^4+2}\) converge.

Solution. (a) Make the denominator smaller to make the fraction larger: \(3^k+k>3^k\), so \(\frac1{3^k+k}<\left(\frac13\right)^k\). The geometric series with \(r=\frac13\) converges, so \(\sum\frac1{3^k+k}\) converges by the Comparison Test, and its sum is less than \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac13\right)^k=\frac{1/3}{1-1/3}=\frac12\). (Its value is about \(0.392\).) (b) Here \(k^4+2>k^4\), so \(\frac k{k^4+2}<\frac k{k^4}=\frac1{k^3}\). The \(p\)-series with \(p=3\) converges, so the series converges.

Example 10.5.3: Comparison for divergence

Show that (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{\sqrt k-1}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^\infty\frac{\ln k}k\) diverge.

Solution. Now the comparison series must be the smaller one, and it must diverge. (a) For \(k\ge2\), \(0<\sqrt k-1<\sqrt k\), so \(\frac1{\sqrt k-1}>\frac1{\sqrt k}\). Since \(\sum\frac1{\sqrt k}\) is a \(p\)-series with \(p=\frac12\le1\), it diverges, and so does the larger series. (b) For \(k\ge3\) we have \(\ln k\ge\ln3\approx1.0986>1\), so \(\frac{\ln k}k>\frac1k\). The harmonic series diverges, so \(\sum_{k=3}^\infty\frac{\ln k}k\) diverges. (The inequality fails at \(k=2\), since \(\ln2\approx0.693<1\); the Comparison Test only needs it from some \(N\) on.)

What each inequality can and cannot say

Let \(0\le a_k\le b_k\). If \(\sum b_k\) converges, so does \(\sum a_k\). If \(\sum a_k\) diverges, so does \(\sum b_k\). But if \(\sum b_k\) diverges, or if \(\sum a_k\) converges, the Comparison Test gives no conclusion: a series can be smaller than a divergent one and still converge (\(\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1k\)) or diverge (\(\frac1{2k}\le\frac1k\)). In short, a convergent series can only be used as an upper bound and a divergent series only as a lower bound.

Example 10.5.4: An inequality that points the wrong way

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k^2-1}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k+1}\) converge.

Solution. (a) The tempting comparison is with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\), which converges. But \(k^2-1<k^2\) gives \(\frac1{k^2-1}>\frac1{k^2}\): our series is larger than a convergent one, and that says nothing. Repair: we need a convergent series that is larger. For \(k\ge2\), \(k^2-1\ge\frac{k^2}2\) (this is \(k^2\ge2\)), so \(\frac1{k^2-1}\le\frac2{k^2}\), and \(\sum\frac2{k^2}\) converges. So the series converges. (In fact it telescopes, \(\frac1{k^2-1}=\frac12\left(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1{k+1}\right)\), with sum \(\frac34\), so the comparison led to the right answer.) (b) Compare with \(\sum\frac1k\), which diverges: \(\frac1{k+1}<\frac1k\) is a smaller series than a divergent one, so again nothing follows. Repair: \(k+1\le2k\) for \(k\ge1\), so \(\frac1{k+1}\ge\frac1{2k}\), and \(\sum\frac1{2k}\) diverges (Theorem 10.3.11(2)). So the series diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.5.1: Choosing a comparison

For each series, name a comparison series and say whether the inequality should make the given series smaller or larger than it: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{4^k+k}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2+9}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k-1}\).

Pause and think 10.5.1: Why nonnegative terms?

Let \(a_k=-1\) and \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\). Then \(a_k\le b_k\) for every \(k\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges. Does \(\sum a_k\) converge? Which line of the proof of the Comparison Test fails?

Answer and discussion

\(\sum a_k=-1-1-1-\cdots\) diverges (to \(-\infty\)). The proof breaks at the first claim: “\(s_n\le t_n\le B\) gives bounded above, so converges” relies on the partial sums being nondecreasing, which needs \(a_k\ge0\). Here the partial sums decrease without bound; being bounded above does not help. Nonnegativity is what turns “bounded above” into “convergent”.

The Limit Comparison Test¶

Inequalities can be fiddly. The series \(\sum\frac1{3^k-k}\) lies above \(\sum3^{-k}\), so the easy comparison goes the wrong way, even though the two series plainly behave alike. The Limit Comparison Test compares the terms by their ratio.

Theorem 10.5.5: Limit Comparison Test

Let \(a_k>0\) and \(b_k>0\) for all \(k\) from some index on, and let \(L=\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{a_k}{b_k}\).

(1) If \(0<L<\infty\), then \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) either both converge or both diverge.

(2) If \(L=0\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges, then \(\sum a_k\) converges.

(3) If \(L=\infty\) and \(\sum b_k\) diverges, then \(\sum a_k\) diverges.

Proof. (1) Take \(\varepsilon=\frac L2>0\) in the definition of the limit: there is \(K\) such that \(\abs{\frac{a_k}{b_k}-L}<\frac L2\) for \(k>K\), that is, \(\frac L2<\frac{a_k}{b_k}<\frac{3L}2\). Multiplying by \(b_k>0\),

\[\frac L2\,b_k<a_k<\frac{3L}2\,b_k\qquad(k>K).\]

If \(\sum b_k\) converges, then \(\sum\frac{3L}2b_k\) converges (Theorem 10.3.11(1)), and \(0<a_k<\frac{3L}2b_k\), so \(\sum a_k\) converges by the Comparison Test (1). If \(\sum b_k\) diverges, then \(\sum\frac L2b_k\) diverges (Theorem 10.3.11(2), since \(\frac L2\ne0\)), and \(0<\frac L2b_k<a_k\), so \(\sum a_k\) diverges by the Comparison Test (2). So \(\sum b_k\) converges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum a_k\) converges, and \(\sum b_k\) diverges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum a_k\) diverges; the second statement is equivalent to “\(\sum a_k\) converges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum b_k\) converges”, so the two series converge or diverge together.

(2) Take \(\varepsilon=1\): there is \(K\) with \(0<\frac{a_k}{b_k}<1\), so \(0<a_k<b_k\) for \(k>K\). If \(\sum b_k\) converges, so does \(\sum a_k\), by the Comparison Test (1).

(3) \(L=\infty\) means that for every number \(M\) there is \(K\) with \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}>M\) for \(k>K\). Take \(M=1\): \(a_k>b_k>0\) for \(k>K\). If \(\sum b_k\) diverges, the larger series \(\sum a_k\) diverges by the Comparison Test (2). ◻ ∎

Cases (2) and (3) are one-sided: they are conclusive only when the comparison series behaves in the stated way. If \(L=0\) and \(\sum b_k\) diverges, or \(L=\infty\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges, the test says nothing (Example 10.5.9).

Procedure 10.5.6: Choosing a comparison series

  1. Check the Divergence Test first: if \(a_k\not\to0\), the series diverges and no comparison is needed.

  2. Find what \(a_k\) is like for large \(k\): keep the highest power in a rational expression, the largest exponential, and discard the rest.

  3. Take \(b_k\) to be that simplified term without its constant factor: a \(p\)-series or a geometric series.

  4. Try the Comparison Test if the inequality is easy and points the right way (smaller than a convergent series, or larger than a divergent one); otherwise compute \(L=\lim\frac{a_k}{b_k}\).

  5. If \(L\) is \(0\) or \(\infty\), check that it is a conclusive case; if not, change \(b_k\).

Example 10.5.7: Rational terms

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k+3}{k^3-k+2}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{4k^2-k}{k^3+7}\) converge.

Solution. (a) The terms are positive, and for large \(k\) they behave like \(\frac k{k^3}=\frac1{k^2}\). Take \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\) (convergent). Then

\[L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{k^2(k+3)}{k^3-k+2}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1+3/k}{1-1/k^2+2/k^3}=1 .\]

Since \(0<L<\infty\), the series converges.

(b) The terms behave like \(\frac{4k^2}{k^3}=\frac4k\). Take \(b_k=\frac1k\) (divergent). Then \(L=\lim\frac{k(4k^2-k)}{k^3+7}=\lim\frac{4-1/k}{1+7/k^3}=4\), so the series diverges.

Example 10.5.8: Roots in the terms

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{\sqrt{k+1}}{k^2+3}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3\sqrt k+2}{k+5}\) converge.

Solution. (a) For large \(k\), \(\frac{\sqrt{k+1}}{k^2+3}\approx\frac{k^{1/2}}{k^2}=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\), a convergent \(p\)-series (\(p=\frac32\)). Then \(L=\lim\frac{k^{3/2}\sqrt{k+1}}{k^2+3}=\lim\frac{k^2\sqrt{1+1/k}}{k^2+3}=1\), so the series converges. (b) The terms behave like \(\frac{3\sqrt k}k=\frac3{\sqrt k}\). Take \(b_k=\frac1{\sqrt k}\) (divergent, \(p=\frac12\)): \(L=\lim\frac{\sqrt k\,(3\sqrt k+2)}{k+5}=\lim\frac{3k+2\sqrt k}{k+5}=3\), so the series diverges.

Example 10.5.9: A logarithm between two \(p\)-series

Decide whether \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{\ln k}{k^{3/2}}\) converges.

Solution. Since \(\ln k\) grows, the terms are a little larger than \(\frac1{k^{3/2}}\) and much smaller than \(\frac1k\). Try both: with \(b_k=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\) (convergent), \(L=\lim\ln k=\infty\), which is the inconclusive case; with \(b_k=\frac1k\) (divergent), \(L=\lim\frac{\ln k}{\sqrt k}=0\), again inconclusive. So choose a \(p\)-series between them, \(b_k=\frac1{k^{5/4}}\) (convergent, \(p=\frac54>1\)):

\[L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\ln k/k^{3/2}}{1/k^{5/4}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\ln k}{k^{1/4}}=0 ,\]

by l’Hôpital’s Rule (the quotient of derivatives is \(\frac4{k^{1/4}}\to0\)). This is a conclusive case, so the series converges.

Example 10.5.10: Exponentials, and checking the Divergence Test first

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2^k+k}{3^k-1}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3\cdot2^k-1}{2^k+7}\) converge.

Solution. (a) The dominant terms give \(\frac{2^k}{3^k}\), so take \(b_k=\left(\frac23\right)^k\), a convergent geometric series. Then \(L=\lim\frac{(2^k+k)\,3^k}{(3^k-1)\,2^k}=\lim\frac{1+k/2^k}{1-3^{-k}}=1\) (because \(\frac k{2^k}\to0\)). The series converges. (b) No comparison is needed: \(a_k=\frac{3-2^{-k}}{1+7\cdot2^{-k}}\to3\ne0\), so the series diverges by the Divergence Test. Comparing first would have wasted effort.

Knowledge Check 10.5.2: The Limit Comparison Test

In each case \(a_k,b_k>0\). Say whether the Limit Comparison Test is conclusive, and what it concludes. (a) \(a_k=\frac1{k^2}\), \(b_k=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\). (b) \(a_k=\frac1k\), \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\). (c) \(a_k=\frac{2k+1}{k^2}\), \(b_k=\frac1k\).

Pause and think 10.5.2: Both sides of the ratio

Find two series \(\sum a_k\) with \(\lim\frac{a_k}{1/k}=0\), one convergent and one divergent. (Use Theorem 10.4.11 and the series of Example 10.4.9.) What does this say about the case \(L=0\) with a divergent comparison series?

Answer and discussion

\(a_k=\frac1{k^2}\): \(\frac{a_k}{1/k}=\frac1k\to0\) and \(\sum a_k\) converges. \(a_k=\frac1{k\ln k}\) (from \(k=2\)): \(\frac{a_k}{1/k}=\frac1{\ln k}\to0\) and \(\sum a_k\) diverges (Example 10.4.9). So \(L=0\) with a divergent \(\sum b_k\) can go either way: the test is inconclusive.

Example 10.5.11: Bounded oscillation in the numerator

Decide whether (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2+\sin k}{k^2+1}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2+\cos k}{\sqrt k}\) converge.

Solution. The numerators oscillate but stay between \(1\) and \(3\), since \(-1\le\sin k,\cos k\le1\); so all terms are positive. (a) \(\frac{2+\sin k}{k^2+1}\le\frac3{k^2+1}<\frac3{k^2}\), and \(\sum\frac3{k^2}\) converges: the series converges. (b) \(\frac{2+\cos k}{\sqrt k}\ge\frac1{\sqrt k}\), and \(\sum\frac1{\sqrt k}\) diverges (\(p=\frac12\)): the series diverges. The limit \(\lim\frac{2+\sin k}{k^2+1}\cdot k^2\) does not exist, so the Limit Comparison Test would not help here; the Comparison Test does.

Estimating a sum by comparison¶

Part (3) of the Comparison Test turns a comparison into a numerical method: a series that is smaller than a series we can sum or bound has a sum that is smaller, and so does every remainder.

Example 10.5.12: Bounds for a sum, and a remainder

Let \(S=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{2^k+k}\). (a) Trap \(S\) between two simple numbers. (b) Use \(s_4=\sum_{k=1}^4\frac1{2^k+k}\) and a remainder bound to find a shorter interval.

Solution. (a) Since \(k<2^k\), \(2^k<2^k+k<2\cdot2^k\), so \(\frac1{2}\cdot2^{-k}<a_k<2^{-k}\). Sum from \(k=1\): \(\sum2^{-k}=1\) and \(\sum\frac12\cdot2^{-k}=\frac12\), so by the Comparison Test (1) \(\frac12\le S\le1\). (In fact \(S\approx0.697\).) (b) The first four terms are \(\frac13,\frac16,\frac1{11},\frac1{20}\), so \(s_4=\frac{141}{220}\approx0.6409\). For the remainder use part (3) with \(b_k=2^{-k}\): \(0\le R_4=\sum_{k=5}^\infty a_k\le\sum_{k=5}^\infty2^{-k}=\frac1{16}\). Therefore \(0.6409<S<0.6409+0.0625=0.7034\). The interval is about \(0.06\) wide and contains \(0.697\).

Knowledge Check 10.5.3: Estimating by comparison

Let \(S=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{4^k+1}\). Use the Comparison Test twice to show that \(\frac16\le S<\frac13\).

Pause and think 10.5.3: Does comparison give the value?

If \(0<a_k<b_k\) for every \(k\) and both series converge, must \(\sum a_k<\sum b_k\) strictly, or could the sums be equal? (Look at the series \(\sum(b_k-a_k)\).)

Answer and discussion

The sums cannot be equal. The series \(\sum(b_k-a_k)\) has positive terms and converges (Theorem 10.3.11(3)) to \(\sum b_k-\sum a_k\). A convergent series of positive terms has a value at least its first term (Theorem 10.3.13), so \(\sum b_k-\sum a_k\ge b_1-a_1>0\). The comparison gives bounds, never the value.

Example 10.5.13: A power of a logarithm

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{(\ln k)^k}\) converges.

Solution. The terms start large (\(a_2\approx2.08\)) but fall quickly. Since \(e^2\approx7.39<8\), for \(k\ge8\) we have \(\ln k\ge\ln8\approx2.079>2\), so \((\ln k)^k>2^k\) and \(\frac1{(\ln k)^k}<\left(\frac12\right)^k\). The geometric series with \(r=\frac12\) converges, so by the Comparison Test (with \(N=8\)) the series converges. The first six terms do not matter for convergence.

Group 1: Direct comparison¶

Example 10.5.14: A geometric upper bound

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac3{2^k+k^2}\) converge?

Solution. Since \(k^2>0\), \(2^k+k^2>2^k\), so \(\frac3{2^k+k^2}<\frac3{2^k}=3\left(\frac12\right)^k\). The series \(\sum3\left(\frac12\right)^k\) is a convergent geometric series (\(r=\frac12\)) times \(3\), so the given series converges by the Comparison Test. Check: the first terms are \(\frac34,\frac37,\frac3{17},\dots\), each smaller than \(\frac3{2^k}=\frac32,\frac34,\frac38,\dots\)

Example 10.5.15: Bounding the numerator as well

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k+1}{k^3+2}\) converge?

Solution. Make the fraction larger in two steps: the numerator satisfies \(k+1\le2k\) for \(k\ge1\), and the denominator \(k^3+2>k^3\). Hence \(\frac{k+1}{k^3+2}\le\frac{2k}{k^3}=\frac2{k^2}\). The \(p\)-series with \(p=2\) converges, so the series converges. Check at \(k=1\): \(\frac23\le2\); at \(k=10\): \(\frac{11}{1002}\approx0.011\le0.02\).

Group 2: Wrong-way inequalities¶

Example 10.5.16: Repairing a convergence comparison

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2-3k+3}\) converge?

Solution. The denominator is positive for all \(k\) (its discriminant is \(9-12<0\)). For \(k\ge2\), \(k^2-3k+3<k^2\), so the terms are larger than \(\frac1{k^2}\): no conclusion from that. We need a lower bound for the denominator of the form \(ck^2\). Completing the square, \(k^2-3k+3=\frac{k^2}4+\frac34(k-2)^2\ge\frac{k^2}4\), so \(\frac1{k^2-3k+3}\le\frac4{k^2}\) for every \(k\ge1\). The series \(\sum\frac4{k^2}\) converges, so the given series converges.

Example 10.5.17: Repairing a divergence comparison

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{\sqrt k+3}\) converge?

Solution. Comparing with \(\frac1{\sqrt k}\) gives \(\frac1{\sqrt k+3}<\frac1{\sqrt k}\): the given series is smaller than a divergent one, which proves nothing. Instead find a divergent series below it: for \(k\ge1\), \(3\le3\sqrt k\), so \(\sqrt k+3\le4\sqrt k\) and \(\frac1{\sqrt k+3}\ge\frac1{4\sqrt k}\). Since \(\sum\frac1{4\sqrt k}\) diverges (\(p=\frac12\), constant factor \(\frac14\)), the series diverges.

Group 3: The Limit Comparison Test¶

Example 10.5.18: A rational function with a quartic denominator

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2k^2+5}{k^4-3k+9}\) converge?

Solution. The denominator is positive (\(k^4-3k+9=7,19,81,253,\dots\) for \(k=1,2,3,4\) and increasing), so the terms are positive. They behave like \(\frac{2k^2}{k^4}=\frac2{k^2}\); take \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\). Then \(L=\lim\frac{k^2(2k^2+5)}{k^4-3k+9}=\lim\frac{2+5/k^2}{1-3/k^3+9/k^4}=2\). Since \(0<L<\infty\) and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges, the series converges.

Example 10.5.19: A square root in the denominator

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac k{\sqrt{k^5+3}}\) converge?

Solution. For large \(k\), \(\sqrt{k^5+3}\approx k^{5/2}\), so the terms behave like \(\frac k{k^{5/2}}=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\). With \(b_k=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\) (convergent),

\[L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{k\cdot k^{3/2}}{\sqrt{k^5+3}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{k^{5/2}}{\sqrt{k^5+3}}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac1{\sqrt{1+3/k^5}}=1 .\]

Since \(0<L<\infty\), the series converges.

Group 4: Logarithms¶

Example 10.5.20: One over the logarithm

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{\ln k}\) converge?

Solution. The terms tend to \(0\), but slowly. Since \(\ln k<k\) for every \(k\ge2\) (the function \(x-\ln x\) has derivative \(1-\frac1x>0\) for \(x>1\) and equals \(1>0\) at \(x=1\)), we get \(\frac1{\ln k}>\frac1k\). The harmonic series diverges, so the larger series diverges.

Example 10.5.21: A squared logarithm

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{(\ln k)^2}{k^3}\) converge?

Solution. Try \(b_k=\frac1{k^3}\) (convergent): \(L=\lim(\ln k)^2=\infty\), inconclusive. Take the larger \(p\)-series \(b_k=\frac1{k^{5/2}}\) (convergent, \(p=\frac52>1\)): \(L=\lim\frac{(\ln k)^2}{k^{1/2}}\). Apply l’Hôpital’s Rule twice: \(\frac{2\ln x/x}{\frac12x^{-1/2}}=\frac{4\ln x}{x^{1/2}}\), and then \(\frac{4/x}{\frac12x^{-1/2}}=\frac8{x^{1/2}}\to0\). So \(L=0\) with a convergent \(\sum b_k\), which is conclusive: the series converges.

Group 5: Estimating sums by comparison¶

Example 10.5.22: Two-sided bounds for a \(p\)-series relative

Let \(S=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2+3}\). Bound \(S\), then sharpen the bound with ten terms.

Solution. For \(k\ge1\), \(k^2<k^2+3\le4k^2\), so \(\frac1{4k^2}\le\frac1{k^2+3}<\frac1{k^2}\). Using \(\sum\frac1{k^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\approx1.645\) (the value stated in Section 10.3), \(\frac{\pi^2}{24}\le S<\frac{\pi^2}6\), that is \(0.411\le S<1.645\). To sharpen, take \(s_{10}=\sum_{k=1}^{10}\frac1{k^2+3}\approx0.6459\) and bound the tail by comparison with \(\sum_{k>10}\frac1{k^2}<\int_{10}^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2}=\frac1{10}\) (Theorem 10.4.13): \(0.6459<S<0.7459\). The true value is \(\approx0.7403\), near the top, because the terms \(\frac1{k^2+3}\) are close to \(\frac1{k^2}\) for large \(k\).

Example 10.5.23: Accuracy \(10^{-4}\) from a geometric tail

How many terms of \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{3^k+k}\) guarantee an error less than \(10^{-4}\), and what is the resulting estimate?

Solution. Since \(\frac1{3^k+k}<3^{-k}\), part (3) of the Comparison Test gives \(0<R_n<\sum_{k>n}3^{-k}=\frac{3^{-n}}2\) (geometric series with first term \(3^{-(n+1)}\) and \(r=\frac13\)). We need \(\frac{3^{-n}}2<10^{-4}\), i.e. \(3^n>5000\). Now \(3^7=2187<5000<6561=3^8\), so \(n=8\) terms are enough; with \(n=7\) the bound is \(\frac1{2\cdot2187}\approx2.3\times10^{-4}\), too large to guarantee anything. With \(n=8\): \(s_8\approx0.392008\) and the error is in \((0,7.7\times10^{-5})\), so \(S\approx0.39201\) to four decimals (the true value is \(0.392084\)).

Group 6: Applications¶

Example 10.5.24: A parameter

For which \(p>0\) does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^p+k}\) converge?

Solution. The dominant term of the denominator depends on \(p\). If \(p>1\), \(k^p\) dominates: compare with \(b_k=\frac1{k^p}\): \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{k^p}{k^p+k}=\frac1{1+k^{1-p}}\to1\) because \(1-p<0\). By the Limit Comparison Test the series behaves like the \(p\)-series: it converges. If \(0<p\le1\), \(k\) dominates (for \(p=1\) the denominator is exactly \(2k\)): compare with \(b_k=\frac1k\): \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac1{1+k^{p-1}}\to1\) if \(p<1\) (as \(p-1<0\)) and equals \(\frac12\) if \(p=1\). Either way \(0<L<\infty\) and the harmonic series diverges, so the series diverges. Answer: it converges if and only if \(p>1\).

Example 10.5.25: A trigonometric term

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(1-\cos\frac1k\right)\) converge?

Solution. All terms are positive (\(\cos\frac1k<1\)), and they tend to \(0\), so the Divergence Test is silent. For large \(k\), \(\frac1k\) is small and \(1-\cos u\approx\frac{u^2}2\), which suggests \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\). With \(u=\frac1k\to0^+\),

\[L=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1-\cos(1/k)}{1/k^2}=\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{1-\cos u}{u^2}=\lim_{u\to0^+}\frac{\sin u}{2u}=\frac12 ,\]

by l’Hôpital’s Rule and the basic limit \(\frac{\sin u}u\to1\). As \(0<L<\infty\) and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges, the series converges. Check at \(k=10\): \(1-\cos0.1\approx0.0049958\) versus \(\frac1{2\cdot100}=0.005\).

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.5.26: Geometric mean of two convergent series

Let \(a_k>0\), \(b_k>0\) and suppose \(\sum a_k\) and \(\sum b_k\) both converge. Prove that \(\sum\sqrt{a_kb_k}\) converges.

Plan. Bound \(\sqrt{a_kb_k}\) above by something whose series we know converges.

Proof. For positive numbers \(x,y\) we have \((\sqrt x-\sqrt y)^2\ge0\), so \(x+y\ge2\sqrt{xy}\), that is \(\sqrt{xy}\le\frac{x+y}2\). With \(x=a_k\) and \(y=b_k\), \(0<\sqrt{a_kb_k}\le\frac12(a_k+b_k)\). The series \(\sum\frac12(a_k+b_k)\) converges by Theorem 10.3.11(1) and (3). By the Comparison Test, \(\sum\sqrt{a_kb_k}\) converges. ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to run the argument backwards: \(\sum\sqrt{a_kb_k}\) can converge while \(\sum a_k\) diverges (take \(a_k=1\) and \(b_k=\frac1{k^4}\), so \(\sqrt{a_kb_k}=\frac1{k^2}\)).

Example 10.5.27: A series and its “compressed” version

Let \(a_k>0\) and \(c_k=\dfrac{a_k}{1+a_k}\). Prove that \(\sum a_k\) converges if and only if \(\sum c_k\) converges.

Plan. One direction is a direct comparison. For the other, show first that \(a_k\to0\), so that \(c_k\) and \(a_k\) are nearly equal.

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Since \(1+a_k>1\), \(0<c_k<a_k\). If \(\sum a_k\) converges, so does \(\sum c_k\) by the Comparison Test.

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\sum c_k\) converges. Then \(c_k\to0\) (Divergence Test), so there is \(K\) with \(c_k<\frac12\) for \(k\ge K\). Solving \(c_k=\frac{a_k}{1+a_k}\) for \(a_k\) gives \(a_k=\frac{c_k}{1-c_k}\) (note \(0<c_k<1\)). For \(k\ge K\) we have \(1-c_k>\frac12\), so \(a_k<2c_k\). The series \(\sum2c_k\) converges (Theorem 10.3.11(1)), so \(\sum a_k\) converges by the Comparison Test. ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to skip showing that \(a_k\to0\): without it, \(c_k\approx a_k\) fails (if \(a_k\) is large, \(c_k\approx1\) and no comparison with \(a_k\) is possible).

Answers and discussion¶

Knowledge Check 10.5.1. (a) Compare with the geometric series \(\sum\left(\frac14\right)^k\): \(4^k+k>4^k\), so the given series is smaller than a convergent series, and converges. (b) Compare with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\): \(k^2+9>k^2\), smaller than a convergent series, so it converges. (c) Compare with the harmonic series: \(k-1<k\) gives \(\frac1{k-1}>\frac1k\), larger than a divergent series, so \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k-1}\) diverges (indeed it is the harmonic series shifted by one).

Knowledge Check 10.5.2. (a) \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{k^{3/2}}{k^2}=k^{-1/2}\to0\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges (\(p=\frac32\)): conclusive, \(\sum a_k\) converges (it is also a convergent \(p\)-series). (b) \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=k\to\infty\), but \(\sum b_k\) converges: inconclusive (and indeed \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges). (c) \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{2k+1}k\to2\in(0,\infty)\): conclusive, both series diverge.

Knowledge Check 10.5.3. Since \(4^k<4^k+1\le2\cdot4^k\), we get \(\frac12\cdot4^{-k}\le\frac1{4^k+1}<4^{-k}\). The geometric series give \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty4^{-k}=\frac{1/4}{1-1/4}=\frac13\) and \(\sum\frac12\cdot4^{-k}=\frac16\), so by the Comparison Test (1) \(\frac16\le S\le\frac13\), and the upper inequality is strict because \(a_1=\frac15<\frac14=b_1\) (Pause and think 10.5.3). The true value is \(S\approx0.2794\).
