10.8 Choosing a Convergence Test¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

You now own nine tools for infinite series, and a series such as \(\sum\frac{\ln k}{k^2}\) or \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) will not announce which one to pick. Choosing is a skill, and it rests on two habits: look at the terms before you compute, and treat an inconclusive test as information, not as a dead end. A test that returns “\(r=1\)” or “\(L=0\)” has told you which tool to put down. This section collects the tests of the chapter in one table, turns them into a strategy, proves the small facts the strategy leans on, and then practises on a large mixed bank.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • state the hypotheses of each test, what it proves, and when it fails to apply or fails to decide;

  • choose a first test from the form of the terms, and change tests when one is inconclusive;

  • classify a series as absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent or divergent, and justify the class;

  • explain why the Ratio and Root Tests are useless for rational terms, and why the Alternating Series Test needs monotone terms;

  • recognise “wrong test” arguments and repair them.

The tests at a glance¶

Tests for series of positive terms (for mixed signs, apply them to \(\sum\abs{a_k}\)):

Test Hypotheses What it proves When it fails
Divergence (Thm 10.4.1) any series diverges if \(a_k\not\to0\) \(a_k\to0\) decides nothing (harmonic series); never proves convergence
Geometric, \(p\)-series (Thms 10.3.3, 10.4.11) \(\sum ar^k\), \(\sum k^{-p}\) converges iff \(\abs r<1\); iff \(p>1\) only for exactly this shape (shift or factor first)
Integral (Thm 10.4.6) \(a_k=f(k)\), \(f\) continuous, positive, decreasing \(\sum a_k\) and \(\int f\) both converge or both diverge no usable antiderivative, or \(f\) not monotone; the integral is not the sum
Comparison (Thm 10.5.1) \(0\le a_k\le b_k\) eventually \(\sum a_k\) converges if \(\sum b_k\) does; \(\sum b_k\) diverges if \(\sum a_k\) does the inequality points the wrong way; terms not \(\ge0\)
Limit Comparison (Thm 10.5.5) \(a_k,b_k>0\); \(L=\lim\frac{a_k}{b_k}\) \(0<L<\infty\): same behaviour \(L=0\) with \(\sum b_k\) divergent, or \(L=\infty\) with \(\sum b_k\) convergent

Tests that accept terms of any sign:

Test Hypotheses What it proves When it fails
Alternating Series (Thm 10.6.2) \((-1)^ka_k\), \(a_k\ge0\) nonincreasing, \(a_k\to0\) converges (possibly only conditionally) \(a_k\) not monotone (Thm 10.8.13) or \(a_k\not\to0\); never proves divergence
Absolute convergence (Thm 10.6.9) any series \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) converges \(\Rightarrow\) \(\sum a_k\) converges \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) diverges: no conclusion about \(\sum a_k\)
Ratio (Thm 10.7.1) \(a_k\ne0\); \(r=\lim\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}\) \(r<1\): absolutely convergent; \(r>1\): divergent \(r=1\); no limit; rational terms (Thm 10.8.10)
Root (Thm 10.7.5) \(r=\lim\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\) same as the Ratio Test \(r=1\); no limit; \((k!)^{1/k}\) has no simple form

Three remarks on the tables. First, the Divergence Test and the tests of the second table accept terms of any sign; the Integral, Comparison and Limit Comparison Tests need positive terms. Second, “fails” has two meanings: a hypothesis is false (the test does not apply), or the test applies and returns no verdict (it is inconclusive). Either way another test must be used. Third, if the Ratio Test limit exists, the Root Test limit exists and is equal to it (Theorem 10.7.8); running both on the same series tells you nothing new when the ratio limit exists, and when that limit is \(1\) both are silent.

A strategy¶

Procedure 10.8.1: Choosing a convergence test

  1. Look and simplify. Is the series geometric, a \(p\)-series after a shift or after factoring, or telescoping? Can it be split into two simpler series? Is there a sign pattern?

  2. Divergence Test. Find \(\lim a_k\). If it is not \(0\), the series diverges: stop. If it is \(0\), nothing is decided: continue.

  3. Match the terms to a test using the table below.

  4. If the test is inconclusive, change tests. A value \(r=1\), a limit \(L=0\) or \(\infty\), or an inequality that points the wrong way means wrong tool, not hard series.

  5. Signs. If the signs are mixed, test \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) first. If it converges, you are done (absolute convergence). If it diverges, look for conditional convergence with the Alternating Series Test, or split the series (Theorem 10.8.2).

  6. Say what you proved: name the test, check its hypotheses, and state the class: converges absolutely, converges conditionally, or diverges.

If the terms contain… try first…
factorials (\(k!\), \((2k)!\)), or \(c^k\) times powers of \(k\) Ratio Test
a whole expression to the \(k\)th power, \((\cdots)^k\) or \((\cdots)^{k^2}\) Root Test
a rational function, or roots of one Limit Comparison with \(\sum k^{-p}\), read off from the leading terms
\(\ln k\) as a factor Comparison with a \(p\)-series (Theorem 10.8.11), or the Integral Test
an \(f(k)\) with an easy antiderivative (\(\frac1{k\ln k}\), \(ke^{-k^2}\)) Integral Test
\(\sin k\), \(\cos k\) or other bounded factors bound them, then Comparison
\((-1)^k\) and decreasing terms \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) first, then the Alternating Series Test
a sum of two different kinds of terms split (Theorem 10.8.2)

Knowledge Check 10.8.1: Which test first?

Say which test you would try first, and why, for (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2k^2+1}{5k^2-3}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k!}{10^k}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k\,(\ln k)^2}\). You do not need to finish the test.

Step 1: simplify and split¶

A series with two kinds of terms is often best split, but the pieces must be handled with care: a sum of two series can be absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent or divergent, depending on the pieces.

Theorem 10.8.2: Classifying a sum of two series

Let \(\sum a_k\) converge absolutely.

(a) If \(\sum b_k\) converges absolutely, then \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converges absolutely.

(b) If \(\sum b_k\) converges conditionally, then \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converges conditionally.

(c) If \(\sum b_k\) diverges, then \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) diverges.

Proof. (a) For every \(k\),

\[\abs{a_k+b_k}\le\abs{a_k}+\abs{b_k}.\]

The series with terms \(\abs{a_k}+\abs{b_k}\) converges (Theorem 10.3.11(3)), so \(\sum\abs{a_k+b_k}\) converges by the Comparison Test (Theorem 10.5.1).

(b) \(\sum a_k\) converges (Theorem 10.6.9), so \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converges, as a sum of two convergent series. Suppose it converged absolutely. Since \(b_k=(a_k+b_k)-a_k\), we have \(\abs{b_k}\le\abs{a_k+b_k}+\abs{a_k}\), and \(\sum\left(\abs{a_k+b_k}+\abs{a_k}\right)\) converges. By the Comparison Test \(\sum\abs{b_k}\) converges, contradicting the assumption that \(\sum b_k\) converges only conditionally. So \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) converges but not absolutely: it converges conditionally.

(c) \(\sum a_k\) converges and \(\sum b_k\) diverges, so \(\sum(a_k+b_k)\) diverges (Theorem 10.3.11(4)). ◻ ∎

The hypothesis that \(\sum a_k\) is absolutely convergent matters for (a) and (b). Two conditionally convergent series can add up to anything: \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k+\sum\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}k\) has every term \(0\), while \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k+\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k=2\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k\) converges only conditionally.

Example 10.8.3: Split a series

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k+k\,3^{-k}}{\sqrt k}\).

Solution. Split each term: \(\dfrac{(-1)^k+k\,3^{-k}}{\sqrt k}=\dfrac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k}+\dfrac{\sqrt k}{3^k}\). The second series has positive terms and an exponential, so use the Ratio Test: \(\dfrac{\sqrt{k+1}/3^{k+1}}{\sqrt k/3^k}=\dfrac13\sqrt{\dfrac{k+1}k}\to\dfrac13<1\), so it converges absolutely. The first series is alternating with \(a_k=\frac1{\sqrt k}\) decreasing to \(0\), so it converges (Alternating Series Test); its absolute values form the \(p\)-series with \(p=\frac12\), which diverges, so it converges only conditionally. By Theorem 10.8.2(b) the original series converges conditionally. (Applying the Ratio Test to the whole series would give \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\) with the sign pattern and the two parts tangled together; splitting is what makes each test clean.)

Example 10.8.4: Special series in disguise

Decide (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=6}^\infty\frac1{\left(k^2-10k+25\right)^{3/4}}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac6{k(k+3)}\).

Solution. (a) Factor first: \(k^2-10k+25=(k-5)^2\), so for \(k\ge6\) the term is \(\frac1{(k-5)^{3/2}}\). With \(j=k-5\) the series is \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac1{j^{3/2}}\), a \(p\)-series with \(p=\frac32>1\): it converges (Theorem 10.4.11). No test was needed beyond recognising the series. (b) The partial fractions \(\frac6{k(k+3)}=2\left(\frac1k-\frac1{k+3}\right)\) show a telescoping series with step \(3\) (Theorem 10.3.8): the partial sums leave \(2\left(1+\frac12+\frac13\right)\) as \(n\to\infty\), so the series converges, and its sum is \(\frac{11}3\). Here the Integral and Comparison Tests would also prove convergence, but only telescoping gives the value.

Step 2: the Divergence Test comes first¶

The Divergence Test needs only a limit, and when it applies it ends the problem. Do it before any test that needs an idea.

Example 10.8.5: Terms that do not tend to \(0\)

Decide (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(1-\frac3k\right)^k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\sin\frac1k\).

Solution. (a) \(\left(1-\frac3k\right)^k\to e^{-3}\approx0.0498\ne0\) (the limit \(\left(1+\frac ak\right)^k\to e^a\) with \(a=-3\)). The terms are small but do not tend to \(0\), so the series diverges. (b) With \(t=\frac1k\to0\), \(k\sin\frac1k=\frac{\sin t}t\to1\ne0\): the series diverges. In both cases the other tests would waste time; one limit settles it.

Pause and think 10.8.1: Why first?

Suppose you skip the Divergence Test and apply the Ratio Test to \(\sum\frac{k+1}{k}\). What happens? Explain why the Divergence Test is the cheapest test to try, and say what it cannot tell you when \(a_k\to0\).

Answer and discussion

For \(a_k=\frac{k+1}k\) the ratio \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{k(k+2)}{(k+1)^2}\to1\): the Ratio Test is inconclusive and you have learned nothing. The Divergence Test finishes at once, since \(a_k\to1\ne0\). It is the cheapest test because it asks for one limit and no idea. When \(a_k\to0\) it tells you nothing: both \(\sum\frac1k\) and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) have terms tending to \(0\), and one diverges and one converges.

Step 3: exponentials, factorials and \(k\)th powers¶

For these terms the Integral Test rarely helps and the Ratio or Root Test usually works at once (Section 10.7). Sometimes more than one test works; then take the shorter route.

Example 10.8.6: More than one test works

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\,e^{-k^2}\) converge?

Solution. Ratio Test: \(\dfrac{(k+1)e^{-(k+1)^2}}{k\,e^{-k^2}}=\left(1+\dfrac1k\right)e^{-2k-1}\to1\cdot0=0<1\), so the series converges absolutely. Integral Test: \(f(x)=xe^{-x^2}\) has \(f'(x)=(1-2x^2)e^{-x^2}<0\) for \(x\ge1\), so \(f\) is positive and decreasing, and \(\int_1^\infty xe^{-x^2}\,dx=\frac1{2e}\) is finite, so the series converges. The Ratio Test is shorter. Notice that the integral \(\frac1{2e}\approx0.184\) is not the sum: the sum is about \(0.405\) (Theorem 10.4.6(b) puts it between \(\frac1{2e}\) and \(\frac1e+\frac1{2e}\approx0.552\)).

Example 10.8.7: A factorial against a power

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^k}{(k!)^2}\) converge?

Solution. Factorials: use the Ratio Test. Since \((k+1)!=(k+1)\,k!\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)^{k+1}}{\left((k+1)!\right)^2}\cdot\frac{(k!)^2}{k^k}=\frac{(k+1)^{k+1}}{(k+1)^2\,k^k}=\frac{\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k}{k+1}\to e\cdot0=0 .\]

So \(r=0<1\) and the series converges absolutely. (The terms are \(1,1,\frac34,\frac49,\dots\): the first few do not shrink, which is why only the limit matters.)

Step 3, continued: rational and algebraic terms¶

For rational terms the Limit Comparison Test compares with the \(p\)-series suggested by the leading terms. Its conclusion, that the two series behave alike, can be sharpened: when \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}\to L>0\), not only convergence but the size of the tails agrees.

Theorem 10.8.8: Terms that behave alike have sums that behave alike

Let \(a_k>0\) and \(b_k>0\) with \(\dfrac{a_k}{b_k}\to L\), where \(0<L<\infty\).

(a) If \(\sum b_k\) converges, then \(\sum a_k\) converges, and the remainders \(R_n(a)=\sum_{k>n}a_k\) and \(R_n(b)=\sum_{k>n}b_k\) satisfy \(\dfrac{R_n(a)}{R_n(b)}\to L\) as \(n\to\infty\).

(b) If \(\sum b_k\) diverges, then \(\sum a_k\) diverges, and the partial sums \(s_n(a)=\sum_{k=1}^na_k\) and \(s_n(b)=\sum_{k=1}^nb_k\) satisfy \(\dfrac{s_n(a)}{s_n(b)}\to L\).

Proof. Let \(0<\varepsilon<L\). Choose \(K\) with \(\abs{\frac{a_k}{b_k}-L}<\varepsilon\) for \(k>K\), that is,

\[(L-\varepsilon)\,b_k<a_k<(L+\varepsilon)\,b_k\qquad(k>K).\]

(a) The series \(\sum(L\pm\varepsilon)b_k\) converge (Theorem 10.3.11(1)). The Comparison Test (Theorem 10.5.1) applied to \(a_k<(L+\varepsilon)b_k\) shows that \(\sum a_k\) converges, and part (3) of that theorem, applied to both inequalities, gives for every \(n\ge K\)

\[(L-\varepsilon)R_n(b)\le R_n(a)\le(L+\varepsilon)R_n(b).\]

Since \(R_n(b)>0\), dividing gives \(L-\varepsilon\le\frac{R_n(a)}{R_n(b)}\le L+\varepsilon\) for all \(n\ge K\). As \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, \(\frac{R_n(a)}{R_n(b)}\to L\).

(b) The terms \(b_k\) are positive, so \(s_n(b)\to\infty\) (Theorem 10.3.13(b)). Put \(A=s_K(a)\) and \(B=s_K(b)\). For \(n>K\), adding the inequalities for \(k=K+1,\dots,n\) gives

\[(L-\varepsilon)\left(s_n(b)-B\right)\le s_n(a)-A\le(L+\varepsilon)\left(s_n(b)-B\right).\]

Since \(A\ge0\) we get \(s_n(a)\ge(L-\varepsilon)\left(s_n(b)-B\right)\to\infty\), so \(\sum a_k\) diverges. Dividing the two inequalities by \(s_n(b)\),

\[(L-\varepsilon)\left(1-\tfrac B{s_n(b)}\right)\le\frac{s_n(a)}{s_n(b)}\le(L+\varepsilon)\left(1-\tfrac B{s_n(b)}\right)+\frac A{s_n(b)}.\]

As \(n\to\infty\), \(\frac B{s_n(b)}\to0\) and \(\frac A{s_n(b)}\to0\), so the left side tends to \(L-\varepsilon\) and the right side to \(L+\varepsilon\). Hence \(L-2\varepsilon<\frac{s_n(a)}{s_n(b)}<L+2\varepsilon\) for all large \(n\), and the ratio tends to \(L\). ◻ ∎

Pause and think 10.8.2: The tails agree, the sums do not

Take \(a_k=\frac1{k^2+1}\) and \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\), so \(L=1\). Theorem 10.8.8 says \(R_n(a)/R_n(b)\to1\). Do the two series have the same sum? What does the theorem say about their sums?

Answer and discussion

No. \(\sum\frac1{k^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\approx1.645\), while \(\sum\frac1{k^2+1}\approx1.077\) (the first terms are \(1\) against \(\frac12\), \(\frac14\) against \(\frac15\), and so on). Theorem 10.8.8 says only that the tails become proportional with factor \(L\), so \(R_n(a)\approx R_n(b)\) for large \(n\). The sums \(\sum_{k\ge1}\) also contain the first terms, where \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) are not close. In general \(\sum a_k\ne L\sum b_k\).

Example 10.8.9: Limit Comparison with roots, and a remainder

(a) Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2k+\sqrt k}{k^{5/2}+7k}\) converge? (b) For \(a_k=\dfrac{2k+1}{k^3+4}\), estimate the error after \(n=50\) terms.

Solution. (a) The leading terms are \(\frac{2k}{k^{5/2}}=\frac2{k^{3/2}}\), so compare with \(b_k=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\) (a convergent \(p\)-series, \(p=\frac32\)):

\[\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{(2k+\sqrt k)\,k^{3/2}}{k^{5/2}+7k}=\frac{2k^{5/2}+k^2}{k^{5/2}+7k}=\frac{2+k^{-1/2}}{1+7k^{-3/2}}\to2 .\]

Since \(0<L=2<\infty\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges, the series converges (Limit Comparison Test). The Ratio Test returns \(r=1\) here as well (the terms behave like a power of \(k\)), and the Integral Test needs an antiderivative of an ugly function.

(b) Compare with \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\): \(k^2a_k=\frac{2k^3+k^2}{k^3+4}\to2=L\). By Theorem 10.8.8(a), \(R_n(a)\approx2R_n(b)\), and \(R_n(b)\approx\frac1n\) for large \(n\) (Theorem 10.4.13 gives \(\frac1{n+1}<R_n(b)<\frac1n\)). So the error after \(50\) terms is about \(2\cdot\frac1{50}=0.04\). The true value is \(R_{50}(a)=0.03980\), and the ratio \(R_{50}(a)/R_{50}(b)=2.0099\) is already close to \(2\).

Now the Ratio and Root Tests. Section 10.7 asserted that they are useless for rational terms; here is the proof.

Theorem 10.8.10: Rational terms: the Ratio and Root Tests say nothing

Let \(p\) and \(q\) be nonzero polynomials and \(a_k=\dfrac{p(k)}{q(k)}\) (defined and nonzero for all large \(k\)). Then

\[\lim_{k\to\infty}\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=1 .\]

So for a series with rational terms both tests return \(r=1\) and are inconclusive.

Proof. Step 1: \(\frac{p(k+1)}{p(k)}\to1\). Let \(p\) have degree \(m\) and leading coefficient \(c\ne0\), so \(p(k)=ck^m+c_{m-1}k^{m-1}+\cdots+c_0\). Dividing by \(k^m\) gives \(\frac{p(k)}{k^m}=c+\frac{c_{m-1}}k+\cdots+\frac{c_0}{k^m}\to c\) by the limit laws, and in the same way \(\frac{p(k+1)}{(k+1)^m}\to c\). Therefore

\[\frac{p(k+1)}{p(k)}=\frac{p(k+1)}{(k+1)^m}\cdot\frac{k^m}{p(k)}\cdot\left(1+\frac1k\right)^m\to c\cdot\frac1c\cdot1=1 .\]

Step 2: the ratio. The same holds for \(q\), so \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{p(k+1)/p(k)}{q(k+1)/q(k)}\to\frac11=1\).

Step 3: the root. A nonzero polynomial has finitely many zeros, so \(a_k\ne0\) for all \(k\ge K\), and Theorem 10.7.8 applied to the sequence \(\{a_k\}_{k\ge K}\) shows \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to1\) (the first \(K-1\) terms do not affect a limit, Theorem B.3). ◻ ∎

The same happens for a power \(k^p\) (the ratio is \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^p\to1\), Theorem 10.2.6) and for terms such as \(\frac1{k\ln k}\). The tests live in the world of exponentials and factorials.

Knowledge Check 10.8.2: When the ratio is silent

Compute \(\lim\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\) for \(a_k=\dfrac{3k+1}{k^2+2}\). What does the Ratio Test say, and which test settles \(\sum a_k\)?

Step 3, continued: logarithms¶

A logarithm grows more slowly than every positive power (Theorem 10.2.13), so a factor \(\ln k\) or \((\ln k)^q\) moves the answer only on the borderline \(p=1\).

Theorem 10.8.11: Logarithmic factors

(a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{\ln k}{k^p}\) converges if and only if \(p>1\).

(b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k\,(\ln k)^q}\) converges if and only if \(q>1\).

Proof. (a) If \(p>1\): let \(s=\frac{1+p}2\), so \(1<s<p\). Then \(\frac{\ln k}{k^p}=\frac{\ln k}{k^{p-s}}\cdot\frac1{k^s}\), and \(\frac{\ln k}{k^{p-s}}\to0\) by Theorem 10.2.13(a) because \(p-s>0\). So \(\frac{\ln k}{k^{p-s}}\le1\) for large \(k\), and \(0<\frac{\ln k}{k^p}\le\frac1{k^s}\). The \(p\)-series with exponent \(s>1\) converges, so \(\sum\frac{\ln k}{k^p}\) converges by the Comparison Test. If \(p\le1\): for \(k\ge3\), \(\ln k>1\), so \(\frac{\ln k}{k^p}>\frac1{k^p}\), and \(\sum\frac1{k^p}\) diverges (\(p\le1\)). By the Comparison Test the series diverges.

(b) If \(q\le0\): the term is \(\frac{(\ln k)^{-q}}k\), and for \(k\ge3\), \((\ln k)^{-q}\ge1\) (because \(\ln k>1\) and \(-q\ge0\)), so the term is at least \(\frac1k\) and the series diverges by comparison with the harmonic series. If \(q>0\): \(f(x)=\frac1{x(\ln x)^q}\) is continuous and positive on \([2,\infty)\) and decreasing, because \(x(\ln x)^q\) is a product of positive increasing functions. By the Integral Test (Theorem 10.4.6) we study \(\int_2^b f(x)\,dx\). The substitution \(u=\ln x\), \(du=\frac{dx}x\), gives

\[\int_2^b\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^q}=\int_{\ln2}^{\ln b}u^{-q}\,du=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(\ln b)^{1-q}-(\ln2)^{1-q}}{1-q}&q\ne1,\\[6pt]\ln\ln b-\ln\ln2&q=1.\end{cases}\]

As \(b\to\infty\), \(\ln b\to\infty\). For \(q>1\) the exponent \(1-q\) is negative, \((\ln b)^{1-q}\to0\), and the integral tends to the finite number \(\frac{(\ln2)^{1-q}}{q-1}\). For \(0<q<1\) the exponent is positive and the integral tends to \(\infty\); for \(q=1\), \(\ln\ln b\to\infty\). So the integral, and with it the series, converges if and only if \(q>1\). ◻ ∎

Example 10.8.12: Three tests fail, a comparison works

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k^2\ln k}\) converge?

Solution. Divergence Test: \(a_k\to0\), no verdict. Ratio Test: \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{k^2}{(k+1)^2}\cdot\frac{\ln k}{\ln(k+1)}\to1\cdot1=1\), no verdict. Integral Test: \(\int\frac{dx}{x^2\ln x}\) has no elementary antiderivative, so it is not practical. Comparison: for \(k\ge3\), \(\ln k\ge\ln3>1\), so \(0<\frac1{k^2\ln k}<\frac1{k^2}\), and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges. The series converges. The lesson: the logarithm in the denominator only helps convergence, so a comparison with the \(p\)-series without the logarithm is enough here.

Step 5: signs, and the limits of the Alternating Series Test¶

The Alternating Series Test (Theorem 10.6.2) needs three things: alternating signs, terms tending to \(0\), and terms that are nonincreasing. The first two are easy to see and the third is easy to forget. Dropping it is not a harmless shortcut.

Theorem 10.8.13: Monotonicity cannot be dropped

Let \(a_k=\dfrac1{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) for \(k\ge2\). Then \(a_k>0\) and \(a_k\to0\), the series \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty(-1)^ka_k\) alternates in sign, and yet \(\sum_{k=2}^\infty\dfrac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) diverges. The sequence \(\{a_k\}\) is not monotone.

Proof. Positive, tending to \(0\). For \(k\ge2\), \(\sqrt k\ge\sqrt2>1\), so \(\sqrt k+(-1)^k\ge\sqrt k-1>0\) and \(a_k>0\). Since \(\sqrt k-1\to\infty\), \(0<a_k\le\frac1{\sqrt k-1}\to0\).

Not monotone. \(a_2=\frac1{\sqrt2+1}\approx0.414\) and \(a_3=\frac1{\sqrt3-1}\approx1.366\), so \(a_3>a_2\). (The same happens at every step from an even index to the next odd index.)

Divergence. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by \(\sqrt k-(-1)^k\). Since

\[\left(\sqrt k+(-1)^k\right)\left(\sqrt k-(-1)^k\right)=k-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left((-1)^k\right)^2=1,\]

we get

\[\frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}=\frac{(-1)^k\sqrt k-1}{k-1}=\underbrace{\frac{(-1)^k\sqrt k}{k-1}}_{u_k}-\underbrace{\frac1{k-1}}_{v_k}\qquad(k\ge2).\]

The series \(\sum v_k=\sum_{k\ge2}\frac1{k-1}\) is the harmonic series, which diverges. The series \(\sum u_k\) alternates with \(c_k=\frac{\sqrt k}{k-1}\), and \(c_k=\frac1{\sqrt k}\cdot\frac k{k-1}\to0\cdot1=0\). Moreover \(f(x)=\frac{\sqrt x}{x-1}\) has

\[f'(x)=\frac{\frac{x-1}{2\sqrt x}-\sqrt x}{(x-1)^2}=-\frac{x+1}{2\sqrt x\,(x-1)^2}<0\qquad(x>1),\]

so \(\{c_k\}\) is decreasing, and \(\sum u_k\) converges by the Alternating Series Test. Now \(\sum u_k\) converges and \(\sum v_k\) diverges, so \(\sum(u_k-v_k)\) diverges (Theorem 10.3.11(2) and (4)). ◻ ∎

The divergence is slow: the partial sums behave like \(-\ln n\) (the second series is the harmonic series, with a minus sign), and \(s_{10000}\approx-8.87\).

Pause and think 10.8.3: Where is monotonicity used?

In the proof of the Alternating Series Test (Theorem 10.6.2) the hypothesis \(a_1\ge a_2\ge\cdots\) is used in two places. Find them, and say which step of the proof breaks for \(a_k=\frac1{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\).

Answer and discussion

The proof uses \(a_{2m-1}\ge a_{2m}\) to show that the even partial sums \(s_{2m}=s_{2m-2}+(a_{2m-1}-a_{2m})\) are nondecreasing, and \(a_{k}\ge a_{k+1}\) again to show that \(s_{2m}\le a_1\) (every bracket \(a_{2j}-a_{2j+1}\) is \(\ge0\)). For \(\sum_{k\ge2}(-1)^ka_k\) with \(a_k=\frac1{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) the pairs are \(a_{2j}-a_{2j+1}\), and these are negative because \(a_{2j}=\frac1{\sqrt{2j}+1}<\frac1{\sqrt{2j+1}-1}=a_{2j+1}\). So the partial sums do not form a monotone bounded sequence, and the Monotone Convergence Theorem cannot be used; in fact they tend to \(-\infty\) (Theorem 10.8.13).

Example 10.8.14: Absolute or conditional?

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{\ln k}k\).

Solution. Absolute values first. \(\frac{\ln k}k\ge\frac1k\) for \(k\ge3\) (because \(\ln k>1\)), and \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges, so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) diverges by the Comparison Test (this is Theorem 10.8.11(a) with \(p=1\)). The series is not absolutely convergent. The series itself. The terms alternate, \(\frac{\ln k}k\to0\) (Theorem 10.2.13(a)), and \(f(x)=\frac{\ln x}x\) has \(f'(x)=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}<0\) for \(x>e\), so the terms decrease from \(k=3\) on (the test only needs this eventually). The Alternating Series Test shows that the series converges. It converges conditionally.

Example 10.8.15: Two wrong tests

Each argument below is wrong. Find the error and give the right conclusion. (a) “For \(\sum\frac{k^2+3}{k^3+k+1}\) the ratio of successive terms tends to \(1\), so the Ratio Test shows that the series diverges.” (b) “The terms of \(\sum_{k\ge2}\frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) alternate in sign and tend to \(0\), so the series converges by the Alternating Series Test.”

Solution. (a) \(r=1\) is inconclusive, and by Theorem 10.8.10 it will always be \(1\) here. The correct tool for a rational term is the Limit Comparison Test with the harmonic series: \(k\,a_k=\frac{k^3+3k}{k^3+k+1}\to1\), so the series diverges, but because of the comparison, not because \(r=1\). (b) The Alternating Series Test needs \(a_k=\frac1{\sqrt k+(-1)^k}\) to be nonincreasing, and it is not (\(a_3>a_2\)). By Theorem 10.8.13 this series diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.8.3: Classify

Classify as absolutely convergent, conditionally convergent or divergent: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{k+4}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{k^2+4}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{k+4}{k}\).

Looking ahead

A power series is a series \(\sum c_kx^k\) whose terms contain a variable. For each fixed \(x\) it is an ordinary series, and the strategy of this section applies to it: the Ratio or Root Test finds the values of \(x\) where it converges absolutely, and an interval of such \(x\) appears. Its half-length is the radius of convergence. At the two end points the Ratio and Root Tests return \(r=1\) and say nothing, so those two series must be settled one at a time with the rest of the toolbox: the Divergence Test, \(p\)-series, the Alternating Series Test. That is exactly the situation of this section, repeated for every power series. Taylor series are power series built from the derivatives of a function, and the question “for which \(x\) does the series converge?” is answered in the chapter on power series by the same tests.

Group 1: Divergence, special series and the Integral Test¶

Example 10.8.16: A difference of square roots

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\sqrt{k^2+k}-k\right)\) converge?

Solution. The term has the form “large minus large”, so the sign of the difference is not obvious and the Divergence Test is the first thing to try. Multiply by the conjugate: \(\sqrt{k^2+k}-k=\dfrac{k}{\sqrt{k^2+k}+k}=\dfrac1{\sqrt{1+1/k}+1}\to\dfrac12\). The limit is not \(0\), so the series diverges. (The terms are positive and tend to \(\frac12\): the partial sums grow like \(\frac n2\).)

Example 10.8.17: A perfect square under a root

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=4}^\infty\frac1{\sqrt{k^2-6k+9}}\) converge?

Solution. Before choosing a test, look at the expression: \(k^2-6k+9=(k-3)^2\), so for \(k\ge4\) the term is \(\frac1{k-3}\) (the root of a square is the absolute value, and \(k-3>0\)). With \(j=k-3\) the series is \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac1j\), the harmonic series, which diverges.

Example 10.8.18: An Integral Test that is immediate

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k}{(k^2+4)^{3/2}}\) converge?

Solution. The numerator is half the derivative of \(k^2+4\), so the antiderivative is immediate and the Integral Test is natural. (The Limit Comparison Test with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) would work too.) Let \(f(x)=x(x^2+4)^{-3/2}\). Then \(f'(x)=\dfrac{4-2x^2}{(x^2+4)^{5/2}}<0\) for \(x>\sqrt2\), so \(f\) is positive and decreasing on \([2,\infty)\), and

\[\int_2^\infty\frac{x\,dx}{(x^2+4)^{3/2}}=\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+4}}\right]_2^\infty=\frac1{\sqrt8}=\frac{\sqrt2}4\approx0.354 .\]

The integral is finite, so the series converges (the first term \(k=1\) does not affect convergence). The value \(0.354\) is not the sum.

Group 2: Comparison and Limit Comparison¶

Example 10.8.19: A bounded numerator

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2+\sin k}{k^2+k}\) converge?

Solution. The signs are all positive (the numerator is between \(1\) and \(3\)), so \(\sin k\) does not make the series alternating, and a Ratio Test would be hopeless because \(\sin(k+1)/\sin k\) misbehaves. Bound the bounded factor: \(1\le2+\sin k\le3\) gives \(0<\frac{2+\sin k}{k^2+k}\le\frac3{k^2+k}<\frac3{k^2}\). The series \(\sum\frac3{k^2}\) converges, so the series converges by the Comparison Test.

Example 10.8.20: Exponentials with a small correction

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3^k+k}{5^k-2}\) converge?

Solution. The dominant terms are \(\frac{3^k}{5^k}=\left(\frac35\right)^k\), a convergent geometric series, so use the Limit Comparison Test with \(b_k=\left(\frac35\right)^k\):

\[\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{\left(3^k+k\right)5^k}{3^k\left(5^k-2\right)}=\frac{1+k\,3^{-k}}{1-2\cdot5^{-k}}\to\frac{1+0}{1-0}=1,\]

because \(k\,3^{-k}\to0\) (Theorem 10.2.13(b)). Since \(0<L=1<\infty\) and \(\sum b_k\) converges, the series converges. (The Ratio Test also works and gives \(r=\frac35\).)

Example 10.8.21: A cube root, and a divergent comparison

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{\sqrt[3]{k^2+1}}{k+2}\) converge?

Solution. For large \(k\) the term is about \(\frac{k^{2/3}}k=\frac1{k^{1/3}}\), a divergent \(p\)-series (\(p=\frac13\le1\)). Let \(b_k=\frac1{k^{1/3}}\). Then \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\dfrac{(k^2+1)^{1/3}\,k^{1/3}}{k+2}=\dfrac{(k^3+k)^{1/3}}{k+2}=\dfrac{\left(1+k^{-2}\right)^{1/3}}{1+2/k}\to1\). With \(0<L=1<\infty\) and \(\sum b_k\) divergent, the series diverges by the Limit Comparison Test. (Check the Divergence Test first: \(a_k\to0\), so it decides nothing.)

Group 3: Ratio and Root Tests¶

Example 10.8.22: A polynomial against two factorials

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^3\,(k+1)!}{(2k)!}\) converge?

Solution. Factorials with different arguments: use the Ratio Test. With \((k+2)!=(k+2)(k+1)!\) and \((2k+2)!=(2k+2)(2k+1)(2k)!\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)^3}{k^3}\cdot\frac{(k+2)!}{(k+1)!}\cdot\frac{(2k)!}{(2k+2)!}=\left(1+\frac1k\right)^3\frac{k+2}{(2k+2)(2k+1)}\to1\cdot0=0 .\]

So \(r=0<1\) and the series converges absolutely. The factorial of \(2k\) dominates everything else.

Example 10.8.23: An exponent of \(k^2\)

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\left(1-\frac1k\right)^{k^2}\) converge?

Solution. The whole expression is raised to a power that grows with \(k\): use the Root Test. \(a_k^{1/k}=\left(1-\frac1k\right)^k\to e^{-1}\approx0.368<1\) (the limit \(\left(1+\frac ak\right)^k\to e^a\) with \(a=-1\)). The series converges absolutely. (The Ratio Test would involve \(\left(1-\frac1{k+1}\right)^{(k+1)^2}\) against \(\left(1-\frac1k\right)^{k^2}\): possible, but much messier.)

Example 10.8.24: Logarithms inside a \(k\)th power

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\left(\frac{\ln k}{\sqrt k}\right)^k\) converge?

Solution. A \(k\)th power again: use the Root Test. \(a_k^{1/k}=\dfrac{\ln k}{\sqrt k}\to0<1\) by Theorem 10.2.13(a) (with \(p=\frac12\), \(q=1\)). The series converges absolutely. In fact \(\frac{\ln x}{\sqrt x}\) has derivative \(\frac{2-\ln x}{2x^{3/2}}\), so its largest value is \(\frac2e\approx0.736\) at \(x=e^2\), and every root \(a_k^{1/k}\) is below \(0.74\): the terms are dominated by a geometric series from the very start.

Group 4: The first test fails¶

Example 10.8.25: A square root of a logarithm

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k\sqrt{\ln k}}\) converge?

Solution. First attempt: the terms tend to \(0\) (Divergence Test: no verdict), and the ratio tends to \(1\) (Ratio Test: no verdict). Second attempt: the logarithm suggests the Integral Test. \(f(x)=\frac1{x\sqrt{\ln x}}\) is positive and decreasing on \([2,\infty)\), and \(\frac d{dx}\left(2\sqrt{\ln x}\right)=\frac1{x\sqrt{\ln x}}\), so \(\int_2^bf\,dx=2\sqrt{\ln b}-2\sqrt{\ln2}\to\infty\) as \(b\to\infty\). The series diverges. (This is Theorem 10.8.11(b) with \(q=\frac12\le1\).)

Example 10.8.26: Alternating signs, but not an Alternating Series Test

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\,\frac{2+(-1)^k}{k}\) converge?

Solution. The terms \((-1)^ka_k\) with \(a_k=\frac{2+(-1)^k}k\) alternate and tend to \(0\), so the Alternating Series Test looks tempting. But \(a_1=1<a_2=\frac32\): the sequence is not decreasing, so the test does not apply (Theorem 10.8.13 shows what can go wrong). Split instead: \((-1)^k\frac{2+(-1)^k}k=\frac{2(-1)^k}k+\frac1k\). The series \(\sum\frac{2(-1)^k}k\) converges (alternating harmonic series times \(2\)), and \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges, so the sum diverges (Theorem 10.3.11(4)).

Example 10.8.27: A comparison from an inequality

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\ln\left(1+\frac1{k^2}\right)\) converge?

Solution. The terms tend to \(0\) (no verdict) and the ratio tends to \(1\) (no verdict). Use the inequality \(\ln(1+t)\le t\) for \(t\ge0\): the function \(g(t)=t-\ln(1+t)\) has \(g(0)=0\) and \(g'(t)=\frac t{1+t}\ge0\), so \(g(t)\ge0\). With \(t=\frac1{k^2}\), \(0<\ln\left(1+\frac1{k^2}\right)\le\frac1{k^2}\), and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges, so the series converges by the Comparison Test. (The Limit Comparison Test also works: \(\frac{\ln(1+t)}t\to1\) as \(t\to0\).)

Group 5: Absolute, conditional or divergent¶

Example 10.8.28: A \(k\)th power with alternating signs

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\left(\frac k{2k+1}\right)^k\).

Solution. The absolute values are a \(k\)th power: \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac k{2k+1}\to\frac12<1\). By the Root Test \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) converges, so the series converges absolutely. No Alternating Series Test is needed.

Example 10.8.29: Conditional convergence by telescoping

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\left(\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k\right)\).

Solution. Write \(a_k=\sqrt{k+1}-\sqrt k=\frac1{\sqrt{k+1}+\sqrt k}\). The denominator increases, so \(a_k\) decreases, and \(a_k\to0\): the Alternating Series Test shows that the series converges. Absolute values: \(\sum a_k\) telescopes, with partial sums \(\sqrt{n+1}-1\to\infty\), so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) diverges. (Equivalently, \(\sqrt k\,a_k\to\frac12\) and \(\sum\frac1{\sqrt k}\) diverges.) The series converges conditionally.

Example 10.8.30: A parameter decides the class

For each \(p>0\) classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\frac k{k^p+1}\).

Solution. Let \(a_k=\frac k{k^p+1}\). Case \(p\le1\): \(a_k\to\infty\) if \(p<1\) and \(a_k=\frac k{k+1}\to1\) if \(p=1\), so the terms do not tend to \(0\) and the series diverges. Case \(p>1\): \(a_k\to0\), and \(f(x)=\frac x{x^p+1}\) has \(f'(x)=\frac{1-(p-1)x^p}{(x^p+1)^2}<0\) once \(x^p>\frac1{p-1}\), so \(a_k\) is eventually decreasing and the Alternating Series Test shows convergence. For absolute convergence compare \(a_k\) with \(k^{1-p}=\frac1{k^{p-1}}\): \(\frac{a_k}{k^{1-p}}=\frac{k^p}{k^p+1}\to1\), so \(\sum a_k\) converges if and only if \(p-1>1\). So: absolutely convergent for \(p>2\), conditionally convergent for \(1<p\le2\), divergent for \(0<p\le1\). (For \(p=\frac32\) the terms behave like \(\frac1{\sqrt k}\); for \(p=3\) like \(\frac1{k^2}\).)

Group 6: Wrong tests¶

Example 10.8.31: A limit of \(0\) with a divergent comparison

A student compares \(\sum\frac{\ln k}{k^2}\) with \(\sum\frac1k\): “\(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{\ln k}k\to0\), so the series behaves like the harmonic series and diverges.” Find the error and decide the series.

Solution. The case \(L=0\) of the Limit Comparison Test proves convergence when \(\sum b_k\) converges; it says nothing when \(\sum b_k\) diverges, and \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges. The student chose a comparison series that is too large. Choose a convergent one: with \(b_k=\frac1{k^{3/2}}\), \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{\ln k}{\sqrt k}\to0\) and \(\sum\frac1{k^{3/2}}\) converges, so \(\sum\frac{\ln k}{k^2}\) converges. (This is also Theorem 10.8.11(a) with \(p=2\).)

Example 10.8.32: The integral is not the sum

A student writes: “\(\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2+1}=\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+1}=\frac\pi4\).” Find the error and bracket the sum.

Solution. The Integral Test says that the series and the integral converge or diverge together; it does not say that they are equal. The function \(\frac1{x^2+1}\) is positive and decreasing, and \(\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+1}=\frac\pi4\approx0.785\), so the series converges, and Theorem 10.4.6(b) gives \(\frac\pi4<S<a_1+\frac\pi4=\frac12+\frac\pi4\approx1.285\). The actual sum is about \(1.0767\) (it equals \(\frac{\pi\coth\pi-1}2\), a closed form we do not need), between the two bounds and different from \(\frac\pi4\).

Example 10.8.33: Terms tend to \(0\): two opposite answers

Show that \(\sum\sin\frac1k\) diverges and \(\sum\left(1-\cos\frac1k\right)\) converges, although both have terms tending to \(0\).

Solution. For both series the Divergence Test is inconclusive, which shows once more that \(a_k\to0\) does not decide anything. Both terms are positive for \(k\ge1\) (as \(0<\frac1k\le1<\frac\pi2\)). Use the Limit Comparison Test. With \(t=\frac1k\): \(k\sin\frac1k=\frac{\sin t}t\to1\) and \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges, so \(\sum\sin\frac1k\) diverges. And \(k^2\left(1-\cos\frac1k\right)=\frac{1-\cos t}{t^2}\to\frac12\), so by comparison with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) the second series converges.

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.8.34: Squares of the terms

Prove: if \(a_k\ge0\) and \(\sum a_k\) converges, then \(\sum a_k^2\) converges. Is the converse true?

Plan. Use that \(a_k\to0\) to get \(a_k\le1\), hence \(a_k^2\le a_k\), and compare.

Proof. Since \(\sum a_k\) converges, \(a_k\to0\) (Theorem 10.4.1), so there is \(K\) with \(0\le a_k\le1\) for \(k\ge K\). For these \(k\), multiplying \(a_k\le1\) by \(a_k\ge0\) gives \(0\le a_k^2\le a_k\). The series \(\sum a_k\) converges, so \(\sum_{k\ge K}a_k^2\) converges by the Comparison Test, and then so does \(\sum a_k^2\) (Theorem 10.3.11(5)). ◻ ∎

The converse is false: \(a_k=\frac1k\) has \(\sum a_k^2=\sum\frac1{k^2}\) convergent, but \(\sum a_k\) is the harmonic series. A common mistake is to say “\(a_k^2\le a_k\)” for all \(k\) without noting that this needs \(a_k\le1\): for \(a_k=2\) it is false. That is why the proof first uses \(a_k\to0\).

Example 10.8.35: A series and the same series in a fraction

Prove: if \(a_k\ge0\), then \(\sum a_k\) converges if and only if \(\sum\frac{a_k}{1+a_k}\) converges.

Plan. One direction is a direct comparison. For the other, show that convergence of \(\sum\frac{a_k}{1+a_k}\) forces \(a_k\) to be small, so that \(1+a_k\le2\).

Proof. Let \(c_k=\frac{a_k}{1+a_k}\). Since \(a_k\ge0\) we have \(0\le c_k\le a_k\). (\(\Rightarrow\) direction, from \(\sum a_k\) to \(\sum c_k\)): if \(\sum a_k\) converges, then \(\sum c_k\) converges by the Comparison Test. (\(\Leftarrow\)): suppose \(\sum c_k\) converges. Then \(c_k\to0\), so \(c_k<\frac12\) for \(k\ge K\). Solving \(c_k=\frac{a_k}{1+a_k}\) for \(a_k\) gives \(a_k=\frac{c_k}{1-c_k}\), and for \(k\ge K\) we have \(1-c_k>\frac12\), so \(a_k<2c_k\). The series \(\sum2c_k\) converges, so \(\sum a_k\) converges by the Comparison Test. ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to stop after the first direction. Comparison in one direction only gives “converges if \(\sum a_k\) converges”; the other direction needs a reason why the fraction does not shrink the terms too much, and that reason is \(a_k\to0\). Example: for \(a_k=\frac1k\), \(c_k=\frac1{k+1}\), and both series diverge.

Answers and discussion¶

Knowledge Check 10.8.1. (a) The Divergence Test: \(\frac{2k^2+1}{5k^2-3}\to\frac25\ne0\), so the series diverges. (b) The factorial suggests the Ratio Test: \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{k+1}{10}\to\infty>1\), so it diverges (the terms eventually grow, so the Divergence Test also works). (c) The Integral Test: \(\frac1{x(\ln x)^2}\) has the antiderivative \(-\frac1{\ln x}\) (Theorem 10.8.11(b) with \(q=2\)), so the series converges.

Knowledge Check 10.8.2. The ratio is \(\frac{(3k+4)(k^2+2)}{(3k+1)\left((k+1)^2+2\right)}\to1\), so the Ratio Test is inconclusive (as it is for every rational term, Theorem 10.8.10). Limit Comparison with \(\sum\frac1k\): \(k\,a_k=\frac{3k^2+k}{k^2+2}\to3\), and the harmonic series diverges, so \(\sum a_k\) diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.8.3. (a) The terms \(\frac1{k+4}\) decrease to \(0\), so the series converges (Alternating Series Test); \(\sum\frac1{k+4}\) diverges (Limit Comparison with \(\sum\frac1k\)), so the convergence is conditional. (b) \(\frac1{k^2+4}<\frac1{k^2}\), so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) converges by comparison: absolutely convergent. (c) \(\abs{a_k}=\frac{k+4}k\to1\ne0\): divergent (Divergence Test).
