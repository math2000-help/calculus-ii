10.7 The Ratio and Root Tests¶

Download the PDF version

Guiding question

Try the Integral Test on \(\sum\frac{7^k}{k!}\): there is no antiderivative of \(\frac1{k!}\) in sight. Try a comparison test: which simple series should \(\frac{7^k}{k!}\) be compared with? The Ratio and Root Tests avoid both questions. They build the comparison series, a geometric series, out of the series itself. If each term is about a fixed fraction \(r<1\) of the one before, the series behaves like \(\sum r^k\) and converges; if the terms grow, it diverges. Both tests work with absolute values, so they apply to terms of any sign, and what they prove is absolute convergence. They are the standard tools for factorials, exponentials and \(k\)-th powers, and the chapter on power series is built on them.

By the end of this section you should be able to:

  • apply the Ratio Test and the Root Test, including the algebra of factorials and the limits \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\to e\) and \(k^{1/k}\to1\);

  • prove both tests by comparison with a geometric series, and say exactly what each case proves;

  • explain why \(r=1\) is inconclusive and why neither test can prove conditional convergence;

  • choose between the two tests, and between these tests and the earlier ones;

  • describe how the tests will give the radius of convergence of a power series.

The idea: successive terms and a geometric series¶

In the geometric series \(\sum ar^k\) every term is \(r\) times the one before: \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\abs r\) for all \(k\). The series converges exactly when \(\abs r<1\). Now take \(\sum\frac{7^k}{k!}\). The ratio of successive terms is \(\frac{7^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\cdot\frac{k!}{7^k}=\frac7{k+1}\), which is \(\frac72,\frac73,\frac74,\dots\) and keeps shrinking: from \(k=13\) on it is below \(\frac12\). So from there on each term is less than half the previous one, and the tail is smaller than a geometric series with ratio \(\frac12\). That observation is the whole proof. The theorem makes it precise.

Factorial arithmetic

\(k!=k(k-1)\cdots2\cdot1\) and \(0!=1\). Since \((k+1)!=(k+1)\,k!\), we get \(\frac{(k+1)!}{k!}=k+1\), \(\ \frac{(k+2)!}{k!}=(k+2)(k+1)\), \(\ \frac{k!}{(k+1)!}=\frac1{k+1}\) and \(\frac{(2k+2)!}{(2k)!}=(2k+2)(2k+1)\). Be careful: \((2k)!\ne2\,k!\) and \((k!)^2\ne(2k)!\); for \(k=2\), \(2!\,2!=4\) but \(4!=24\). For powers, \(\frac{(k+1)^{k+1}}{k^k}=(k+1)\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\), and \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\to e\) (the Calculus I definition of \(e\)).

Theorem 10.7.1: Ratio Test

Let \(\sum a_k\) be a series with \(a_k\ne0\) for all \(k\), and suppose that

\[r=\lim_{k\to\infty}\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}\]

exists (or is \(\infty\)).

  1. If \(r<1\), the series converges absolutely, and therefore it converges.

  2. If \(r>1\) (including \(r=\infty\)), the series diverges.

  3. If \(r=1\), the test is inconclusive: the series may converge or diverge.

Proof. Case 1 (\(r<1\)). Choose a number \(\rho\) with \(r<\rho<1\), for example \(\rho=\frac{1+r}2\). Because \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\to r<\rho\), there is an index \(K\) such that \(\abs{a_{k+1}}<\rho\abs{a_k}\) for all \(k\ge K\). Then \(\abs{a_{K+j}}\le\rho^j\abs{a_K}\) for \(j=0,1,2,\dots\): this is true for \(j=0\), and if it is true for \(j\) then \(\abs{a_{K+j+1}}<\rho\abs{a_{K+j}}\le\rho^{j+1}\abs{a_K}\). The series \(\sum_{j=0}^\infty\abs{a_K}\rho^j\) is geometric with \(0<\rho<1\), so it converges (Theorem 10.3.3). By the Comparison Test (Theorem 10.5.1), \(\sum_{j=0}^\infty\abs{a_{K+j}}\) converges. Adding the finitely many terms \(\abs{a_1},\dots,\abs{a_{K-1}}\) does not change convergence (Theorem 10.3.11(5)), so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) converges. That is absolute convergence, and absolute convergence implies convergence (Theorem 10.6.9).

Case 2 (\(r>1\), including \(r=\infty\)). Because the ratios tend to a limit larger than \(1\) (or to \(\infty\)), there is \(K\) with \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}>1\) for all \(k\ge K\). Then \(\abs{a_{k+1}}>\abs{a_k}\) for \(k\ge K\), so \(\abs{a_k}\ge\abs{a_K}>0\) for every \(k\ge K\). The terms do not tend to \(0\), and the series diverges by the Divergence Test (Theorem 10.4.1).

Case 3 (\(r=1\)). We show that \(r=1\) occurs both for a divergent and for a convergent series, so \(r=1\) decides nothing. For \(\sum\frac1k\), \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\frac k{k+1}\to1\), and the series diverges (harmonic series). For \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\), \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\left(\frac k{k+1}\right)^2\to1\), and the series converges (Theorem 10.4.11, \(p=2\)). ◻ ∎

The proof uses a number \(\rho\) strictly between \(r\) and \(1\): the ratios are eventually below \(\rho\), and a geometric series with ratio \(\rho\) is available. When \(r=1\) there is no room for such a \(\rho\), which is why the case fails.

Pause and think 10.7.1: Below one is not enough

The ratios \(\frac k{k+1}\) of the harmonic series are below \(1\) for every \(k\), yet the series diverges. Where does the proof of Case 1 need \(r<1\) rather than “every ratio is below \(1\)”?

Answer and discussion

The proof needs one fixed number \(\rho<1\) that is larger than all the ratios from some index on, so that every term is at most \(\rho\) times the previous one and the tail is dominated by \(\sum\rho^k\). If the ratios tend to \(r<1\), such a \(\rho\) exists (any number between \(r\) and \(1\)). If the ratios are below \(1\) but tend to \(1\), as \(\frac k{k+1}\) does, no such \(\rho\) exists: for every \(\rho<1\) the ratios eventually exceed \(\rho\). The terms shrink, but not geometrically.

Example 10.7.2: The Ratio Test with factorials

Decide whether each series converges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{7^k}{k!}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k!}{100^k}\).

Solution. (a) The terms are positive, and

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{7^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\cdot\frac{k!}{7^k}=\frac7{k+1}\to0=r .\]

Since \(r=0<1\), the series converges absolutely. (The terms grow at first, \(a_6=a_7=\frac{117649}{720}\approx163.4\), and then collapse. The Ratio Test only looks at the tail.) (b) Now

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)!}{100^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{100^k}{k!}=\frac{k+1}{100}\to\infty .\]

So \(r=\infty>1\) and the series diverges. The first hundred terms shrink, to about \(9.3\times10^{-43}\) at \(k=100\), but then they grow without bound: \(a_{300}\approx3.1\times10^{14}\).

Example 10.7.3: Powers against exponentials

Decide whether each series converges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^3}{2^k}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^{k+1}\frac{5^k}{k^4+1}\).

Solution. (a) The ratio is

\[\frac{(k+1)^3}{2^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{2^k}{k^3}=\frac12\left(1+\frac1k\right)^3\to\frac12 .\]

Since \(r=\frac12<1\) the series converges absolutely. The test does not give the sum; the partial sums approach \(26\) (numerically). (b) Use the absolute values \(\abs{a_k}=\frac{5^k}{k^4+1}\):

\[\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\frac{5^{k+1}}{(k+1)^4+1}\cdot\frac{k^4+1}{5^k}=5\cdot\frac{k^4+1}{(k+1)^4+1}\to5 .\]

Since \(r=5>1\), the series diverges. The alternating signs do not help: \(\abs{a_k}\to\infty\), so the terms do not tend to \(0\).

Example 10.7.4: A factorial against \(k^k\)

For which of \(c=2\) and \(c=3\) does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{c^k\,k!}{k^k}\) converge?

Solution. With \(a_k=\frac{c^kk!}{k^k}\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{c^{k+1}(k+1)!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{k^k}{c^kk!}=c\,\frac{(k+1)\,k^k}{(k+1)^{k+1}}=c\left(\frac k{k+1}\right)^k=\frac c{(1+1/k)^k}\to\frac ce .\]

Here \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\to e\approx2.718\). For \(c=2\), \(r=\frac2e\approx0.736<1\): the series converges absolutely. For \(c=3\), \(r=\frac3e\approx1.104>1\): the series diverges. (For \(c=e\) the test gives \(r=1\) and says nothing.)

Knowledge Check 10.7.1: Factorials and the Ratio Test

(a) Simplify \(\dfrac{(k+2)!}{k!}\) and \(\dfrac{(2k+2)!}{(2k)!}\). (b) Use the Ratio Test on \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k!}{(k+2)!\,2^k}\).

The Root Test¶

When the terms are \(k\)-th powers, the \(k\)-th root removes the power at once, and \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\) is easier to find than the ratio. The idea is the same: if \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\) is near \(r\) then \(\abs{a_k}\) is near \(r^k\).

Theorem 10.7.5: Root Test

Let \(\sum a_k\) be a series and suppose that

\[r=\lim_{k\to\infty}\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\]

exists (or is \(\infty\)).

  1. If \(r<1\), the series converges absolutely, and therefore it converges.

  2. If \(r>1\) (including \(r=\infty\)), the series diverges.

  3. If \(r=1\), the test is inconclusive.

Proof. Case 1 (\(r<1\)). Choose \(\rho\) with \(r<\rho<1\). Since \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to r<\rho\), there is \(K\) with \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}<\rho\) for all \(k\ge K\). Raising both sides to the \(k\)-th power (which preserves inequalities between nonnegative numbers) gives \(\abs{a_k}<\rho^k\) for \(k\ge K\). The geometric series \(\sum\rho^k\) converges, so \(\sum_{k\ge K}\abs{a_k}\) converges by the Comparison Test, hence \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) converges (Theorem 10.3.11(5)). Absolute convergence implies convergence (Theorem 10.6.9).

Case 2 (\(r>1\), including \(r=\infty\)). There is \(K\) with \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}>1\) for all \(k\ge K\), so \(\abs{a_k}>1\) for \(k\ge K\). The terms do not tend to \(0\), and the series diverges by the Divergence Test.

Case 3 (\(r=1\)). For \(\sum\frac1k\), \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac1{k^{1/k}}\to1\) by Theorem E.1, and the series diverges. For \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\), \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\left(\frac1{k^{1/k}}\right)^2\to1\), and the series converges. So \(r=1\) occurs for both kinds of series. ◻ ∎

Example 10.7.6: The Root Test

Decide whether each series converges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\left(\frac{2k+1}{3k+5}\right)^k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(1+\frac1{2k}\right)^{k^2}\).

Solution. (a) \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac{2k+1}{3k+5}\to\frac23<1\). The series converges absolutely. (The \(k\)-th root cancels the \(k\)-th power exactly, and that is why the Root Test suits such terms.) (b) The terms are positive and \(a_k^{1/k}=\left(1+\frac1{2k}\right)^k=\left[\left(1+\frac1{2k}\right)^{2k}\right]^{1/2}\to e^{1/2}\approx1.649>1\), because \(\left(1+\frac1{2k}\right)^{2k}\to e\) and the square root is continuous. The series diverges. (The Ratio Test would be messy here.)

Example 10.7.7: Roots of powers of \(k\)

Decide whether each series converges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^7}{5^k}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{4^k}{k^2+1}\).

Solution. (a) \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\dfrac{\left(k^{1/k}\right)^7}5\to\dfrac15\), because \(k^{1/k}\to1\) (Theorem E.1). So \(r=\frac15<1\) and the series converges absolutely. The Ratio Test gives the same value, \(\frac{(k+1)^7}{5k^7}\to\frac15\). The approach to the limit is slow: at \(k=1000\) the root is still \(0.2099\). (b) \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\dfrac4{(k^2+1)^{1/k}}\to\dfrac41=4\), since \((k^2+1)^{1/k}\to1\) (Theorem E.3 with \(p=2\) and \(c_k=1+\frac1{k^2}\to1\)). So \(r=4>1\) and the series diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.7.2: The Root Test

Find \(r\) and decide: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac{3k+1}{4k+7}\right)^k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{6^k}{k^3}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(1+\frac1k\right)^{k^2}\).

The two tests compared¶

Both tests compare with a geometric series, and they are closely linked. If the ratios settle down to \(r\), so do the roots.

Theorem 10.7.8: Ratio limit and root limit

Let \(a_k\ne0\) for all \(k\). If \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\to r\), where \(r\) is a number \(\ge0\) or \(\infty\), then \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to r\) as well.

Proof. Write \(\rho_k=\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\). Case \(r<\infty\). Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and choose \(K\) with \(r-\varepsilon<\rho_k<r+\varepsilon\) for all \(k\ge K\). Applying the right inequality repeatedly, \(\abs{a_k}<\abs{a_K}\,(r+\varepsilon)^{k-K}=C(r+\varepsilon)^k\) for \(k>K\), with the constant \(C=\abs{a_K}(r+\varepsilon)^{-K}>0\). Taking \(k\)-th roots, \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}<C^{1/k}(r+\varepsilon)\). Since \(C^{1/k}\to1\) (Theorem E.2), \(C^{1/k}<1+\frac\varepsilon{r+\varepsilon}\) for large \(k\), and then \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}<(r+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon=r+2\varepsilon\). If \(r=0\) this finishes the proof, since \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\ge0\). If \(r>0\), take \(\varepsilon<r\): the left inequality gives \(\abs{a_k}>C'(r-\varepsilon)^k\) with \(C'=\abs{a_K}(r-\varepsilon)^{-K}>0\), so \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}>C'^{1/k}(r-\varepsilon)>(r-\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac\varepsilon{r-\varepsilon}\right)=r-2\varepsilon\) for large \(k\), because \(C'^{1/k}\to1\). Thus \(r-2\varepsilon<\abs{a_k}^{1/k}<r+2\varepsilon\) for all large \(k\), and \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to r\).

Case \(r=\infty\). Let \(M>1\). There is \(K\) with \(\rho_k>M\) for \(k\ge K\), so \(\abs{a_k}>\abs{a_K}M^{k-K}=C''M^k\) for \(k>K\), with \(C''=\abs{a_K}M^{-K}>0\), and \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}>C''^{1/k}M\). Since \(C''^{1/k}\to1\), we have \(C''^{1/k}>\frac12\) for large \(k\), so \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}>\frac M2\) for large \(k\). As \(M\) is arbitrary, \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to\infty\). ◻ ∎

So whenever the Ratio Test is conclusive, the Root Test is conclusive with the same \(r\); the Ratio Test is never stronger. The Root Test can be stronger, because the roots can have a limit when the ratios do not (the next example). The Ratio Test is often easier: for a factorial, the ratio simplifies at once, while \((k!)^{1/k}\) has no simple form.

Example 10.7.9: The ratios oscillate but the roots settle

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2+(-1)^k}{2^k}\) converges, and find its sum.

Solution. The first terms are \(a_1=\frac12\), \(a_2=\frac34\), \(a_3=\frac18\), \(a_4=\frac3{16}\), and the ratios \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\) are \(\frac32,\frac16,\frac32,\frac16,\dots\) alternately. They have no limit, so the Ratio Test cannot be used. (Ratios above \(1\) every other time do not prove divergence either: Case 2 needs the limit to exceed \(1\).) For the Root Test, \(1\le2+(-1)^k\le3\) gives \(1\le\left(2+(-1)^k\right)^{1/k}\le3^{1/k}\), and \(3^{1/k}\to1\) (Theorem E.2). By the Squeeze Theorem \(\left(2+(-1)^k\right)^{1/k}\to1\), so \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\to\frac12<1\). The series converges absolutely. Its sum can be found by splitting it into two geometric series: \(\sum_{k\ge1}\frac2{2^k}=2\) and \(\sum_{k\ge1}\left(-\frac12\right)^k=\frac{-1/2}{1+1/2}=-\frac13\), so the sum is \(2-\frac13=\frac53\).

Example 10.7.10: A series for the Ratio Test

Decide whether each series converges: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(k!)^2}{(2k)!}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(2k)!}{3^k\,(k!)^2}\).

Solution. (a) Using \((k+1)!=(k+1)k!\) and \((2k+2)!=(2k+2)(2k+1)(2k)!\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{\left((k+1)!\right)^2}{(2k+2)!}\cdot\frac{(2k)!}{(k!)^2}=\frac{(k+1)^2}{(2k+2)(2k+1)}=\frac{k+1}{2(2k+1)}\to\frac14 .\]

So \(r=\frac14<1\) and the series converges (the terms are \(\frac12,\frac16,\frac1{20},\frac1{70},\dots\)).

(b) In the same way

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(2k+2)(2k+1)}{3\,(k+1)^2}=\frac{2(2k+1)}{3(k+1)}\to\frac43 .\]

So \(r=\frac43>1\) and the series diverges. The Root Test would need \(\left((k!)^2/(2k)!\right)^{1/k}\), which has no elementary form, and that is the usual situation with factorials: use the Ratio Test.

Pause and think 10.7.2: The \(k\)-th root of \(k!\)

The ratios of \(\sum\frac1{k!}\) tend to \(0\). What does Theorem 10.7.8 say about \(\left(\frac1{k!}\right)^{1/k}\), and hence about \((k!)^{1/k}\)? Check with \(k=10\) and \(k=100\).

Answer and discussion

Since \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\frac1{k+1}\to0\), Theorem 10.7.8 gives \(\left(\frac1{k!}\right)^{1/k}\to0\), so \((k!)^{1/k}\to\infty\): \((k!)^{1/k}\) grows without bound. Numerically \((10!)^{1/10}\approx4.53\) and \((100!)^{1/100}\approx38.0\). It grows roughly like \(k/e\) (\(10/e\approx3.7\), \(100/e\approx36.8\)), but this needs more than we have here. It is the reason that the Root Test is hard to use for factorials.

Procedure 10.7.11: Choosing between the tests

  1. Factorials, or products such as \((2k)!\), or terms where the ratio simplifies: use the Ratio Test.

  2. A \(k\)-th power \((\cdots)^k\), or \(c^k\) times powers of \(k\): use the Root Test (the ratio also works for \(c^kk^p\)).

  3. Only powers of \(k\), rational functions, logarithms: both tests give \(r=1\) and are useless. Use comparison, Limit Comparison, the Integral Test or the Alternating Series Test.

  4. The ratios have no limit: the Ratio Test does not apply; try the Root Test or a comparison.

  5. If \(r\ne1\) you are done. If \(r=1\), go back to the earlier tests. A conclusion \(r<1\) means absolute convergence; \(r>1\) means the terms do not tend to \(0\).

The next section collects all the tests of the chapter into one strategy; this procedure is its Ratio-and-Root part.

What the tests do not say: \(r=1\) and absolute convergence¶

Example 10.7.12: Three series with \(r=1\)

Compute the Ratio Test limit and decide: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}k\); (d) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3k+1}{k^3+5}\).

Solution. In (a), (b), (c), \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\) equals \(\frac k{k+1}\), \(\left(\frac k{k+1}\right)^2\), \(\frac k{k+1}\) and tends to \(1\) each time. In (d) the ratio is \(\frac{(3k+4)(k^3+5)}{(3k+1)\left((k+1)^3+5\right)}\), and dividing numerator and denominator by \(k^4\) shows that it also tends to \(1\). The Root Test gives \(r=1\) as well (Theorem E.3). So both tests say nothing. The answers, from earlier sections, are: (a) diverges (harmonic series); (b) converges absolutely (\(p=2\)); (c) converges, but only conditionally (Alternating Series Test, with \(\sum\frac1k\) divergent); (d) converges, by the Limit Comparison Test with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\), since \(k^2a_k=\frac{3k^3+k^2}{k^3+5}\to3\). The same happens for every nonzero rational function of \(k\): the leading terms of \(a_{k+1}\) and \(a_k\) agree in the limit, so \(r=1\). The Ratio and Root Tests are never useful for rational terms.

The series (c) shows something else. It converges, yet the tests cannot detect it, and the reason is general.

Theorem 10.7.13: Conditional convergence forces \(r=1\)

Suppose \(\sum a_k\) converges conditionally. If \(\lim\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}\) exists, it equals \(1\). If \(\lim\abs{a_k}^{1/k}\) exists, it equals \(1\).

Proof. Let \(r\) be the limit of either quantity. If \(r<1\), the series converges absolutely (Ratio or Root Test), which contradicts conditional convergence. If \(r>1\), the series diverges, which also contradicts it. So \(r=1\). ◻ ∎

In words: the Ratio and Root Tests are tests for absolute convergence. They look only at \(\abs{a_k}\), so the signs play no role. A conditionally convergent series, whose convergence depends on the signs, always leaves them with \(r=1\). When \(r<1\) you learn more than convergence (absolute convergence, so the terms can be reordered, see Section 10.6); when \(r>1\) you learn divergence, even for alternating series.

Pause and think 10.7.3: Why absolute convergence?

A series has \(r=\frac12\) in the Ratio Test. Could it converge only conditionally? Could \(r=1\) occur for a conditionally convergent series? For one that converges absolutely?

Answer and discussion

If \(r=\frac12<1\), the series converges absolutely, so it cannot be conditionally convergent (Theorem 10.7.13). A conditionally convergent series, whose limit \(r\) exists, must have \(r=1\). But \(r=1\) does not mean the series is conditionally convergent: \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges absolutely, both with \(r=1\) (Example 10.7.12). So \(r<1\) rules out conditional convergence, \(r>1\) rules out convergence, and \(r=1\) rules out nothing.

Example 10.7.14: Absolute, conditional or divergent

Classify: (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{3^k}{k!}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{k+3}{k+1}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt{k+1}}\).

Solution. (a) \(\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}=\frac3{k+1}\to0<1\): absolutely convergent. (b) The ratio tends to \(1\): inconclusive. But \(\abs{a_k}=\frac{k+3}{k+1}\to1\ne0\), so the series diverges (Divergence Test). (c) The ratio \(\sqrt{\frac{k+1}{k+2}}\to1\): inconclusive. The terms \(\frac1{\sqrt{k+1}}\) decrease to \(0\), so the series converges (Alternating Series Test). Absolute values: \(\frac{1/\sqrt{k+1}}{1/\sqrt k}=\sqrt{\frac k{k+1}}\to1\), and \(\sum\frac1{\sqrt k}\) diverges (\(p=\frac12\)), so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) diverges (Limit Comparison Test). The series converges conditionally.

Knowledge Check 10.7.3: What does \(r=1\) tell you?

For each series compute the Ratio Test limit and say whether it settles the question; if not, name a test that does. (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{k^2+3}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{\sqrt k}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{3^k}{k^2}\).

Looking ahead: the radius of convergence¶

Example 10.7.15: For which \(x\) does the series converge?

For each series, find all numbers \(x\) for which it converges (here \(x\) is a fixed number, and the limit is taken as \(k\to\infty\)): (a) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{x^k}{k\,3^k}\); (b) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{x^k}{k!}\); (c) \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty k!\,x^k\).

Solution. (a) \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\frac{\abs x^{k+1}}{(k+1)3^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{k\,3^k}{\abs x^k}=\frac{\abs x}3\cdot\frac k{k+1}\to\frac{\abs x}3\). So \(r<1\) when \(\abs x<3\): absolute convergence. For \(\abs x>3\), \(r>1\): divergence. The tests say nothing at \(x=\pm3\), where \(r=1\); these are checked separately: at \(x=3\) the series is \(\sum\frac1k\), which diverges; at \(x=-3\) it is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}k\), which converges (conditionally). The series converges exactly for \(-3\le x<3\). (b) \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\frac{\abs x}{k+1}\to0\) for every \(x\): the series converges absolutely for all \(x\). (c) \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=(k+1)\abs x\to\infty\) for every \(x\ne0\): it diverges for all \(x\ne0\), and at \(x=0\) it is just \(0+0+\cdots\). In each case the set of \(x\) is an interval centred at \(0\): half-length \(3\) in (a), unbounded in (b), zero in (c). This half-length is the radius of convergence.

Looking ahead

A power series is a series \(\sum c_kx^k\) whose terms contain powers of a variable \(x\). Applying the Ratio Test with \(x\) held fixed gives \(r=\abs x\cdot L\), where \(L=\lim\abs{c_{k+1}/c_k}\) (when it exists). The series converges absolutely when \(\abs x<\frac1L\) and diverges when \(\abs x>\frac1L\), so the radius of convergence is \(\frac1L\) (infinite if \(L=0\), zero if \(L=\infty\)). The endpoints, where \(r=1\), are decided by other tests, as in Example 10.7.15(a). The chapter on power series is built on this idea, and the Root Test gives the same radius by the same argument.

Group 1: The Ratio Test¶

Example 10.7.16: A shifted factorial

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{4^k}{(k+2)!}\) converge?

Solution. Because \((k+3)!=(k+3)(k+2)!\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{4^{k+1}}{(k+3)!}\cdot\frac{(k+2)!}{4^k}=\frac4{k+3}\to0 .\]

So \(r=0<1\), and the series converges absolutely.

Example 10.7.17: Three factorials

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(3k)!}{(k!)^3\,30^k}\) converge?

Solution. Use \((3k+3)!=(3k+3)(3k+2)(3k+1)(3k)!\) and \(\left((k+1)!\right)^3=(k+1)^3(k!)^3\):

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(3k+3)(3k+2)(3k+1)}{(k+1)^3\cdot30}=\frac{(3k+2)(3k+1)}{10\,(k+1)^2}\to\frac9{10}.\]

Since \(r=\frac9{10}<1\) the series converges. (With \(27^k\) in place of \(30^k\) the limit would be exactly \(1\) and the test would say nothing.)

Group 2: The Root Test¶

Example 10.7.18: A fraction to the \(k\)-th power

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\left(\frac{3k^2+1}{2k^2+7}\right)^k\) converge?

Solution. \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\dfrac{3k^2+1}{2k^2+7}\to\dfrac32>1\). By the Root Test the series diverges. (Indeed \(\abs{a_k}>1\) as soon as \(3k^2+1>2k^2+7\), that is \(k\ge3\), so the terms do not tend to \(0\).)

Example 10.7.19: An inverse tangent to the \(k\)-th power

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac{\tan^{-1}k}3\right)^k\) converge?

Solution. The terms are positive and \(a_k^{1/k}=\frac{\tan^{-1}k}3\to\frac{\pi/2}3=\frac\pi6\approx0.524<1\), because \(\tan^{-1}k\to\frac\pi2\). By the Root Test the series converges. (Note that \(\tan^{-1}k<\frac\pi2<3\) for every \(k\), so even the cruder comparison with \(\sum\left(\frac\pi6\right)^k\) works.)

Group 3: Mixed terms and choosing a test¶

Example 10.7.20: Power, exponential and factorial together

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k^2\,2^k}{(k+1)!}\) converge?

Solution. With \((k+2)!=(k+2)(k+1)!\),

\[\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)^2\,2^{k+1}}{(k+2)!}\cdot\frac{(k+1)!}{k^2\,2^k}=\frac{2\,(k+1)^2}{(k+2)\,k^2}\to0 .\]

So \(r=0<1\) and the series converges. The factorial dominates the power and the exponential, as in the growth rates of Section 10.2.

Example 10.7.21: Repeated terms: only the Root Test works

Let \(a_k=2^{-\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\), so the series is \(1+\frac12+\frac12+\frac14+\frac14+\frac18+\frac18+\cdots\). Does it converge?

Solution. The ratios are \(\frac12,1,\frac12,1,\dots\) (each term is followed by a repeat). They have no limit, so the Ratio Test does not apply. For the Root Test, \(\frac{k-1}2\le\lfloor k/2\rfloor\le\frac k2\), so the exponent \(-\frac{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}k\) lies between \(-\frac12\) and \(-\frac{k-1}{2k}\), which tends to \(-\frac12\). By the Squeeze Theorem \(a_k^{1/k}=2^{-\lfloor k/2\rfloor/k}\to2^{-1/2}\approx0.707<1\), and the series converges. Its sum is \(1+2\left(\frac12+\frac14+\cdots\right)=3\).

Group 4: When \(r=1\)¶

Example 10.7.22: A rational function

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{2k+3}{k^3+4k}\) converge?

Solution. The ratio of successive terms tends to \(1\), as it does for every rational function, so the Ratio Test is useless. Compare with \(b_k=\frac1{k^2}\): \(\frac{a_k}{b_k}=\frac{2k^3+3k^2}{k^3+4k}\to2\), and \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) converges, so the series converges by the Limit Comparison Test.

Example 10.7.23: A logarithm: the Integral Test

Does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac1{k\ln k}\) converge?

Solution. The ratio is \(\frac{k\ln k}{(k+1)\ln(k+1)}\to1\) (since \(\frac{\ln(k+1)}{\ln k}\to1\)), so the Ratio Test fails. The function \(f(x)=\frac1{x\ln x}\) is positive, continuous and decreasing on \([2,\infty)\), and \(\int_2^bf(x)\,dx=\ln\ln b-\ln\ln2\to\infty\). By the Integral Test the series diverges. Compare \(\sum\frac1{k(\ln k)^2}\): it also has \(r=1\), but \(\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^2}=\frac1{\ln2}\) is finite, so that series converges. The same value \(r=1\) goes with both answers.

Group 5: Absolute, conditional or divergent¶

Example 10.7.24: Powers of \(\frac34\)

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^kk\left(\frac34\right)^k\).

Solution. \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\frac{k+1}k\cdot\frac34\to\frac34<1\). The series converges absolutely.

Example 10.7.25: Conditional convergence with \(r=1\)

Classify \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^\infty(-1)^k\frac{k+2}{k^2+k}\).

Solution. The ratio tends to \(1\), so the tests of this section do not decide. Absolute values: compare \(\frac{k+2}{k^2+k}\) with \(\frac1k\): the quotient \(\frac{k^2+2k}{k^2+k}\to1\) and \(\sum\frac1k\) diverges, so \(\sum\abs{a_k}\) diverges (Limit Comparison Test). The series itself: \(a_k=\frac{k+2}{k^2+k}\to0\), and \(f(x)=\frac{x+2}{x^2+x}\) has \(f'(x)=-\frac{x^2+4x+2}{(x^2+x)^2}<0\) for \(x>0\), so the terms decrease, and the Alternating Series Test shows convergence. The series converges conditionally.

Group 6: Applications¶

Example 10.7.26: An interval of convergence

For which \(x\) does \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(x-1)^k}{k^2+1}\) converge?

Solution. \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\abs{x-1}\,\frac{k^2+1}{(k+1)^2+1}\to\abs{x-1}\). So the series converges absolutely if \(\abs{x-1}<1\), that is \(0<x<2\), and diverges if \(\abs{x-1}>1\). At \(x=2\) the series is \(\sum\frac1{k^2+1}\), which converges (compare with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\)); at \(x=0\) it is \(\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{k^2+1}\), which converges absolutely for the same reason. So the series converges exactly for \(0\le x\le2\): centre \(1\), radius \(1\), both endpoints included.

Example 10.7.27: Estimating \(e\) with a geometric bound

Taking for granted that \(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k!}=e\), bound the error of \(s_8=\sum_{k=0}^8\frac1{k!}\).

Solution. The error is \(e-s_8=\sum_{k\ge9}\frac1{k!}\). For \(k\ge9\) the ratio is \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac1{k+1}\le\frac1{10}\), so \(a_{9+j}\le\frac{a_9}{10^j}\) and the tail is at most the geometric series

\[\frac1{9!}\left(1+\frac1{10}+\frac1{10^2}+\cdots\right)=\frac1{9!}\cdot\frac{10}9=\frac{10}{9\cdot9!}\approx3.06\times10^{-6}.\]

Here \(s_8=\frac{109601}{40320}\approx2.7182788\), so \(2.7182788\le e\le2.7182788+0.0000031=2.7182819\). (The true value is \(2.7182818\ldots\) and the actual error is \(3.059\times10^{-6}\), just below the bound.)

Group 7: Proofs¶

Example 10.7.28: Ratios that stay at least \(1\)

Prove: if \(a_K\ne0\) and \(\abs{a_{k+1}}\ge\abs{a_k}\) for all \(k\ge K\), then \(\sum a_k\) diverges.

Plan. Show that the terms cannot tend to \(0\), and use the Divergence Test.

Proof. By induction, \(\abs{a_k}\ge\abs{a_K}\) for all \(k\ge K\): this is true for \(k=K\), and if \(\abs{a_k}\ge\abs{a_K}\) then \(\abs{a_{k+1}}\ge\abs{a_k}\ge\abs{a_K}\). Since \(\abs{a_K}>0\), the terms do not tend to \(0\), and \(\sum a_k\) diverges by the Divergence Test. ◻ ∎

A common mistake is to use “every ratio is below \(1\)” as a reason for convergence. The harmonic series has \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac k{k+1}<1\) for every \(k\), and it diverges. The Ratio Test needs the limit to be below \(1\).

Example 10.7.29: Polynomial factors do not change \(r\)

Prove: if \(r=\lim\abs{a_{k+1}/a_k}<1\) (with every \(a_k\ne0\)), then \(\sum k^pa_k\) converges absolutely for every real \(p\).

Plan. Compute the Ratio Test limit for \(b_k=k^pa_k\).

Proof. Since \(b_k\ne0\), we may form \(\abs{\frac{b_{k+1}}{b_k}}=\left(\frac{k+1}k\right)^p\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\left(1+\frac1k\right)^p\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}\). As \(k\to\infty\), \(1+\frac1k\to1\), and \(y\mapsto y^p\) is continuous at \(1\), so \(\left(1+\frac1k\right)^p\to1\) (Theorem 10.2.6). By the product law the ratio tends to \(1\cdot r=r<1\), and \(\sum b_k\) converges absolutely by the Ratio Test. ◻ ∎

For example, \(\sum\frac{k^{100}}{2^k}\) converges at once (\(a_k=2^{-k}\), \(r=\frac12\)). The result fails if \(r=1\): for \(a_k=\frac1{k^2}\), \(\sum k^pa_k=\sum k^{p-2}\) converges for \(p<1\) and diverges for \(p\ge1\).

Knowledge Check 10.7.1. (a) \(\frac{(k+2)!}{k!}=(k+2)(k+1)\) and \(\frac{(2k+2)!}{(2k)!}=(2k+2)(2k+1)\). (b) \(a_k=\frac{k!}{(k+2)!\,2^k}=\frac1{(k+1)(k+2)\,2^k}\), and \(\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)\,2^k}{(k+2)(k+3)\,2^{k+1}}=\frac{k+1}{2(k+3)}\to\frac12\). Since \(r=\frac12<1\), the series converges absolutely.

Knowledge Check 10.7.2. (a) \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac{3k+1}{4k+7}\to\frac34<1\): converges absolutely. (b) \(\abs{a_k}^{1/k}=\frac6{(k^{1/k})^3}\to6>1\): diverges. (c) \(a_k^{1/k}=\left(1+\frac1k\right)^k\to e>1\): diverges.

Knowledge Check 10.7.3. (a) The ratio is \(\frac{k^2+3}{(k+1)^2+3}\to1\): inconclusive; compare with \(\sum\frac1{k^2}\) (Comparison Test, since \(\frac1{k^2+3}<\frac1{k^2}\)): converges. (b) \(\abs{\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}}=\sqrt{\frac k{k+1}}\to1\): inconclusive; the Alternating Series Test gives convergence, and since \(\sum\frac1{\sqrt k}\) diverges, the convergence is conditional. (c) The ratio is \(3\left(\frac k{k+1}\right)^2\to3>1\): it settles the question, and the series diverges.
